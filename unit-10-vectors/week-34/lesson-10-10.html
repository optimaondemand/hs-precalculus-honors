Forces in Balance
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to resolve a force into its horizontal and vertical (or parallel and perpendicular) components.

  • I will be able to state and apply the condition for equilibrium — that the vector sum of all forces on a stationary object is the zero vector.

  • I will be able to solve for one or more unknown forces (magnitudes and/or angles) that keep a system in equilibrium.

  • I will be able to resolve gravity into components parallel and perpendicular to an inclined surface, and use those components to analyze forces on a ramp.

📚 VOCABULARY

Force vector: a vector representing a push or pull on an object, with magnitude (measured in pounds or newtons) and direction.

Resultant force (net force): the vector sum of every force acting on an object — the single force that would produce the same effect as all of them combined.

Equilibrium: the state of an object whose net force is the zero vector, \(\vec{0}=(0,0)\) — every horizontal component sums to zero, and every vertical component sums to zero, simultaneously.

Equilibrant: the single additional force that, added to an existing set of forces, would bring the system into equilibrium. It has the same magnitude as the resultant of the other forces, but points in exactly the opposite direction.

Component of a force parallel/perpendicular to an incline: the two projections of a force (typically gravity) onto directions running along the slope of an inclined surface and directly into it.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

The Flemish engineer Simon Stevin, working around 1586, is usually credited with the first clear statement of the parallelogram of forces — the observation that two forces acting on a single point combine exactly the way you have been adding vectors all unit: tip to tail, or by completing a parallelogram. Stevin illustrated the idea with a chain of weights draped over a triangular wedge, and the argument still standing on his diagram four centuries later is the same one this lesson uses: a system stays still only when its forces, added as vectors, sum to exactly zero. Not approximately zero. Not mostly balanced. Exactly \(\vec{0}\), in every direction at once.

The Condition for Equilibrium

\(\vec{F}_1+\vec{F}_2+\cdots+\vec{F}_n = \vec{0}\)

An object at rest, or moving at constant velocity, is in equilibrium: the vector sum of every force acting on it is the zero vector. Because vector addition happens component by component, this single vector equation is really two ordinary equations at once — the sum of every horizontal (\(x\)) component must equal \(0\), and the sum of every vertical (\(y\)) component must equal \(0\), simultaneously. A system that balances horizontally but not vertically is not in equilibrium at all.

Example 1

A sign weighing \(150\) lb hangs in equilibrium from two cables. Cable \(A\) runs up and to the right at \(40^\circ\) above horizontal; cable \(B\) runs up and to the left at \(55^\circ\) above horizontal. Find the tension in each cable.

Solution:

Set up components for all three forces: cable \(A\)'s tension \(T_A\) at \(40^\circ\) gives \(\big(T_A\cos40^\circ,\ T_A\sin40^\circ\big)\). Cable \(B\)'s tension \(T_B\) pulls up and to the left, so at \(55^\circ\) above horizontal on the other side: \(\big(-T_B\cos55^\circ,\ T_B\sin55^\circ\big)\). The weight pulls straight down: \((0,-150)\).

Equilibrium requires both component sums to equal zero:

\(x:\ \ T_A\cos40^\circ - T_B\cos55^\circ = 0 \qquad\qquad y:\ \ T_A\sin40^\circ+T_B\sin55^\circ-150=0\)

From the \(x\)-equation, \(T_A = T_B\cdot\dfrac{\cos55^\circ}{\cos40^\circ}\). Substituting into the \(y\)-equation and solving for \(T_B\):

\(T_B\!\left(\dfrac{\cos55^\circ}{\cos40^\circ}\sin40^\circ+\sin55^\circ\right)=150 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T_B\approx115.3\text{ lb}\)

Then \(T_A = T_B\cdot\dfrac{\cos55^\circ}{\cos40^\circ}\approx86.4\) lb.

Answer: \(T_A\approx86.4\) lb, \(T_B\approx115.3\) lb. Check: \(86.4\cos40^\circ-115.3\cos55^\circ\approx0\) and \(86.4\sin40^\circ+115.3\sin55^\circ\approx150\). ✓ Notice that the two tensions are not equal, even though both cables hold up the same sign — the cable running at the shallower angle to horizontal (\(A\), at \(40^\circ\)) carries less of the load than the steeper one (\(B\), at \(55^\circ\)), because more of \(B\)'s pull is already aimed straight up.

🤝 Service in Equilibrium

Notice what Example 1 actually shows: neither cable holds up the sign by itself. Cable \(A\) alone would let the sign swing and fall toward \(B\)'s side; \(B\) alone would let it fall toward \(A\)'s. It is only in mutual cooperation — each cable's pull exactly compensating for what the other cannot supply alone — that the sign hangs still. This is the shape this unit's virtue, service, takes in mathematics: not one force overpowering the rest, but every force contributing precisely what the whole system needs, no more and no less, so that something else — a sign, a bridge deck, a roof — can rest secure.

Example 2

Two forces act on a stalled cart: \(F_1=80\) lb at \(25^\circ\) above the positive \(x\)-axis, and \(F_2=55\) lb at \(130^\circ\) from the positive \(x\)-axis. Find the equilibrant — the third force that would bring the cart into equilibrium.

Solution:

\(F_1=\big(80\cos25^\circ,\ 80\sin25^\circ\big)\approx(72.5,\ 33.8)\).

\(F_2=\big(55\cos130^\circ,\ 55\sin130^\circ\big)\approx(-35.4,\ 42.1)\).

Resultant \(=F_1+F_2\approx(37.2,\ 75.9)\)

Resultant magnitude: \(\sqrt{37.2^2+75.9^2}\approx84.5\) lb, at angle \(\arctan\!\left(\dfrac{75.9}{37.2}\right)\approx63.9^\circ\) from the positive \(x\)-axis.

The equilibrant is equal in magnitude but opposite in direction to this resultant — the same \(84.5\) lb, at \(63.9^\circ+180^\circ=243.9^\circ\).

Answer: Equilibrant \(\approx84.5\) lb at \(243.9^\circ\). Adding this force to \(F_1\) and \(F_2\) brings every component sum to exactly zero — verify it yourself by adding all three vectors.

Example 3 — Inclined Plane

A \(40\)-lb crate rests on a frictionless ramp inclined at \(25^\circ\) to the horizontal. Find the components of the crate's weight parallel and perpendicular to the ramp's surface, and state the force required, acting parallel to the ramp, to hold the crate in equilibrium.

Solution:

Set up axes tilted with the ramp: one direction runs along the slope (parallel), the other directly into the ramp's surface (perpendicular). Gravity, \(40\) lb straight down, splits into these two directions by the incline angle \(\theta=25^\circ\):

Parallel component (down the slope) \(=40\sin25^\circ\approx16.9\) lb

Perpendicular component (into the ramp) \(=40\cos25^\circ\approx36.3\) lb

The perpendicular component is balanced automatically by the ramp's surface pushing back (the normal force); it never causes the crate to slide. Only the parallel component pulls the crate down the slope, so equilibrium requires an equal and opposite force, \(16.9\) lb directed up the slope.

Answer: Parallel component \(\approx16.9\) lb (down-slope); perpendicular component \(\approx36.3\) lb (into the ramp); a \(16.9\)-lb force directed up the slope holds the crate in equilibrium. Notice this is exactly the same skill as Example 1 — decomposing one force into two perpendicular directions — only here the “horizontal and vertical” axes have been tilted to match the ramp.

▶  Try it yourself: Resolve a \(60\)-lb force acting at \(35^\circ\) above horizontal into its horizontal and vertical components.
Horizontal: \(60\cos35^\circ\approx49.1\) lb. Vertical: \(60\sin35^\circ\approx34.4\) lb. Check: \(\sqrt{49.1^2+34.4^2}\approx60\) lb. ✓

💻 On Your Calculator: Visualizing Equilibrium

At desmos.com/calculator, plot the three forces from Example 2 as vectors from the origin — \(F_1\approx(72.5,33.8)\), \(F_2\approx(-35.4,42.1)\), and the equilibrant \(\approx(-37.2,-75.9)\) — and confirm by eye that the three arrowheads, placed tip to tail, form a closed triangle that returns exactly to the origin. A closed polygon of force vectors is a visual test for equilibrium: if the arrows don't close, the system isn't balanced.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Checking only one direction — for instance, confirming the horizontal forces balance and stopping there. Equilibrium is two conditions at once: the horizontal components must sum to zero, and the vertical components must sum to zero, independently. A system can look balanced left-to-right and still be accelerating straight down. Always write both equations before you declare a system in equilibrium.

Conclusion

A hanging sign, a stalled cart, a crate on a ramp — every one of today's systems balanced for the same reason: forces added as vectors, summing to exactly zero in every direction at once. None of these objects held themselves up alone; each depended on other forces supplying exactly what it could not supply for itself. Tomorrow this same component-splitting tool turns toward a new question — not whether two vectors sum to zero, but what it means for two vectors to multiply.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does equilibrium require two separate equations (horizontal and vertical) rather than one single check?

2. In Example 1, why does the more steeply angled cable end up carrying more of the sign's weight than the shallower one?

3. Why is the equilibrant always exactly opposite in direction to the resultant of the other forces, rather than some other direction entirely?

4. On a steeper ramp than Example 3's, would the parallel (sliding) component of gravity increase or decrease? What does this tell you about why steep ramps feel harder to hold something still on?
