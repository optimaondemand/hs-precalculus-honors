Angles Between Vectors and Orthogonality
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to find the angle between two vectors using \(\cos\theta=\dfrac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|}\).

  • I will be able to test whether two vectors are orthogonal by computing their dot product, and explain why \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=0\) is the correct test rather than a rough visual check.

  • I will be able to decompose a vector into components parallel and perpendicular to a second vector.

  • I will be able to evaluate an AI tool's claim about orthogonality by actually computing the dot product myself, rather than accepting the claim on its word.

📚 VOCABULARY

Orthogonal vectors: two vectors whose angle is exactly \(90^\circ\) — equivalently, and much more useful for computation, two vectors whose dot product equals exactly \(0\).

Component of \(\mathbf{v}\) parallel to \(\mathbf{u}\): the piece of \(\mathbf{v}\) that points in the same (or exactly opposite) direction as \(\mathbf{u}\); the remainder, perpendicular to \(\mathbf{u}\), is the component of \(\mathbf{v}\) perpendicular to \(\mathbf{u}\). Together they add back up to \(\mathbf{v}\) exactly.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Yesterday's derivation gave the dot product a job: \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\cos\theta\) turns a component computation into an angle. Today puts that formula to its two most important uses — finding the exact angle between any two vectors, and recognizing the single special case, \(\theta=90^\circ\), where entire branches of geometry and physics simplify dramatically because \(\cos90^\circ=0\).

Finding the Angle Between Two Vectors

\(\theta = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|}\right)\)

Solve the angle formula for \(\theta\) directly: compute the dot product, divide by the product of the two magnitudes, and take the inverse cosine. Because \(0^\circ\le\theta\le180^\circ\) for the angle between two vectors, there is no ambiguity to resolve — unlike some inverse-trig situations from earlier units, this one always has a single correct answer in that range.

Example 1

Find the angle between \(\mathbf{u}=(1,\sqrt3)\) and \(\mathbf{v}=(-1,\sqrt3)\).

Solution:

\(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=(1)(-1)+(\sqrt3)(\sqrt3)=-1+3=2\). \(|\mathbf{u}|=\sqrt{1+3}=2\), \(|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{1+3}=2\).

\(\cos\theta=\dfrac{2}{2\cdot2}=\dfrac12 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta=\cos^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac12\right)=60^\circ\)

Answer: \(\theta=60^\circ\). A clean answer like this is worth double-checking by sketch: both vectors have the same length (\(2\)) and sit symmetrically on either side of the \(y\)-axis, which is exactly the picture a \(60^\circ\) angle between them should produce.

Key Fact: The Orthogonality Test

Two nonzero vectors \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) are orthogonal (perpendicular) exactly when \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=0\). This follows immediately from \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\cos\theta\): since \(|\mathbf{u}|\) and \(|\mathbf{v}|\) are never zero, the dot product vanishes only when \(\cos\theta=0\) — that is, only when \(\theta=90^\circ\). No angle needs to be computed at all; the dot product alone answers the question.

Example 2

Determine whether \(\mathbf{u}=(2,3)\) and \(\mathbf{v}=(-3,2)\) are orthogonal.

Solution:

\(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=(2)(-3)+(3)(2)=-6+6=0\)

Answer: Yes, \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) are orthogonal, because their dot product is exactly \(0\). No angle, magnitude, or arccosine is even needed once the dot product itself comes out to zero.

🤖 AI CHECKPOINT — FIND THE PLANTED ERROR

A student asked an AI tutor: “Are \(\mathbf{u}=(7,2)\) and \(\mathbf{v}=(4,-9)\) orthogonal?” The AI answered:

“Yes. \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=(7)(4)+(2)(-9)=28-18=0\), so the vectors are orthogonal.”

Recompute the AI's arithmetic yourself, one term at a time. Is its conclusion correct? If not, find the exact step where the arithmetic goes wrong, and state the correct dot product and what it actually tells you about the angle between \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\).

▶  See the fix: what did the AI's answer get wrong?
\((7)(4)=28\) and \((2)(-9)=-18\) are both correct — the AI's setup was right. The error is in the final subtraction: \(28-18=10\), not \(0\). Since \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=10\ne0\), the vectors are not orthogonal. (In fact, \(\cos\theta=\dfrac{10}{|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|}=\dfrac{10}{\sqrt{53}\cdot\sqrt{97}}\approx0.14\), so \(\theta\approx91.9^\circ\) — very close to perpendicular, which is likely exactly why the AI's slip felt so plausible. Being close to \(90^\circ\) is not the same as being exactly \(90^\circ\), and the dot-product test does not round.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
This is exactly the shape most AI arithmetic errors take: not a wildly wrong answer, but a single dropped sign or slipped subtraction buried inside an otherwise correct-looking setup. An AI tool that shows its work is easier to audit than one that doesn't — but “shows its work” only helps if you actually read every step instead of skimming to the conclusion.

Orthogonality also makes it possible to split any vector into two meaningful pieces relative to a second vector — one piece parallel to it, one piece perpendicular. This decomposition is the exact tool tomorrow's lesson names formally as a projection, but the dot product already gives you everything you need to build it today.

Example 3 — Decomposing a Vector

Let \(\mathbf{u}=(3,1)\) and \(\mathbf{v}=(4,2)\). Write \(\mathbf{v}\) as the sum of a component parallel to \(\mathbf{u}\) and a component perpendicular to \(\mathbf{u}\).

Solution:

The parallel component is a scalar multiple of \(\mathbf{u}\) itself, say \(k\mathbf{u}\), chosen so that \(\mathbf{v}-k\mathbf{u}\) is orthogonal to \(\mathbf{u}\) — that is, \((\mathbf{v}-k\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{u}=0\). Expanding: \(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{u}-k(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u})=0\), so \(k=\dfrac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u}}\).

\(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=(3)(4)+(1)(2)=14\). \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u}=(3)(3)+(1)(1)=10\). So \(k=\dfrac{14}{10}=\dfrac75\).

Parallel component: \(k\mathbf{u} = \dfrac75(3,1) = \left(\dfrac{21}{5},\ \dfrac75\right) = (4.2,\ 1.4)\)

Perpendicular component: \(\mathbf{v}-k\mathbf{u} = (4,2)-(4.2,1.4) = (-0.2,\ 0.6)\)

Answer: \(\mathbf{v} = (4.2,1.4) + (-0.2,0.6)\), where \((4.2,1.4)\) is parallel to \(\mathbf{u}\) and \((-0.2,0.6)\) is perpendicular to it. Check the perpendicular claim: \((-0.2,0.6)\cdot(3,1)=-0.6+0.6=0\). ✓ And the two pieces do add back to \(\mathbf{v}\): \((4.2-0.2,\ 1.4+0.6)=(4,2)\). ✓

💻 On Your Calculator: Confirming Orthogonality Visually

At desmos.com/calculator, plot Example 2's \(\mathbf{u}=(2,3)\) and \(\mathbf{v}=(-3,2)\) as segments from the origin, then use Desmos's angle-measurement tool to confirm the angle between them reads exactly \(90^\circ\). Do the same with the AI checkpoint's \(\mathbf{u}=(7,2)\) and \(\mathbf{v}=(4,-9)\) — you should see an angle just barely off from \(90^\circ\), a visual echo of why the AI's error was so easy to make and so important to catch anyway.

▶  Try it yourself: Are \(\mathbf{u}=(5,-2)\) and \(\mathbf{v}=(4,10)\) orthogonal?
\(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=(5)(4)+(-2)(10)=20-20=0\). Yes — the dot product is exactly \(0\), so \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) are orthogonal.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Deciding two vectors are “basically perpendicular” from a rough sketch, without computing the dot product at all. A pair of vectors can look extremely close to a right angle and still have a dot product of \(10\), \(2\), or any other nonzero number — exactly what happened in this lesson's AI checkpoint. Orthogonality is an exact condition (\(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=0\)), never an eyeballed one.

Conclusion

The dot product now does three jobs at once: it reports the angle between two vectors, it flags orthogonality the instant it equals zero, and it supplies exactly the tool needed to split one vector into a piece that follows a second vector and a piece that ignores it entirely. That last idea — decomposition into parallel and perpendicular pieces — is not a side note. Next week's Lesson 10-13 names the parallel piece a projection and puts it to work computing something entirely new: the physical concept of work, the amount of a force that actually acts along the direction of motion.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does the orthogonality test \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=0\) never need you to compute magnitudes or an angle at all?

2. In the AI checkpoint, exactly which arithmetic step failed, and why was the AI's setup — the two products it multiplied — entirely correct even though its conclusion was wrong?

3. In Example 3's decomposition, why must the perpendicular component always satisfy \((\mathbf{v}-k\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{u}=0\) — what would it mean if it didn't?

4. Why is “the vectors look about perpendicular” never an acceptable substitute for computing \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}\), even when a sketch is available?
