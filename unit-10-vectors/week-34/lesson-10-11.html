The Dot Product
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to compute the dot product of two vectors from their components, \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=u_1v_1+u_2v_2\).

  • I will be able to derive the formula \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\cos\theta\) from the Law of Cosines, and explain why the same theorem from Unit 7 reappears here.

  • I will be able to use either form of the dot product — components, or magnitudes and angle — to solve for whichever quantity is unknown.

📚 VOCABULARY

Dot product (scalar product): an operation on two vectors \(\mathbf{u}=(u_1,u_2)\) and \(\mathbf{v}=(v_1,v_2)\) that returns a single number (a scalar), not a vector: \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=u_1v_1+u_2v_2\).

Angle between two vectors: the angle \(\theta\), with \(0^\circ\le\theta\le180^\circ\), formed when both vectors are drawn from the same starting point.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Every vector operation so far in this unit has produced another vector: adding two vectors gives a vector, scaling a vector gives a vector. The dot product is the first vector operation in this course that deliberately produces something else — a single number. That number turns out to measure something genuinely useful: how much two vectors point in the same direction. Two vectors pointing the same way have a large positive dot product; two pointing oppositely have a large negative one; two at right angles, as the next lesson makes precise, have a dot product of exactly zero.

The Dot Product, by Components

\(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = u_1v_1+u_2v_2\)

For \(\mathbf{u}=(u_1,u_2)\) and \(\mathbf{v}=(v_1,v_2)\), multiply matching components and add the results. The output is a single real number, positive, negative, or zero — never another vector.

Example 1

Let \(\mathbf{u}=(3,-2)\) and \(\mathbf{v}=(4,5)\). Compute \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}\).

Solution:

\(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = (3)(4)+(-2)(5) = 12-10 = 2\)

Answer: \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=2\). A small positive number: these two vectors point in roughly the same general direction, though not closely aligned — you'll be able to say exactly how closely once tomorrow's lesson turns this number into an angle.

The component formula is fast, but it hides why the dot product knows anything about direction at all. To see that, this unit reaches back for a theorem you have not used since Unit 7: the Law of Cosines.

Deriving \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\cos\theta\) from the Law of Cosines

Draw \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) from the same starting point, with angle \(\theta\) between them. The segment connecting their two tips is exactly the difference vector \(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\), and the three vectors \(\mathbf{u}\), \(\mathbf{v}\), and \(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\) form a triangle with sides \(|\mathbf{u}|\), \(|\mathbf{v}|\), and \(|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}|\), and included angle \(\theta\) between the first two. Lesson 7-11's Law of Cosines applies directly to this triangle:

\(|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}|^2 = |\mathbf{u}|^2+|\mathbf{v}|^2-2|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\cos\theta\)

Now expand the left side using the dot product instead, treating \(|\mathbf{w}|^2=\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{w}\) for any vector \(\mathbf{w}\):

\(|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}|^2=(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v})\cdot(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v})=\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u}-2(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v})+\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}=|\mathbf{u}|^2-2(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v})+|\mathbf{v}|^2\)

Both expressions equal \(|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}|^2\), so they equal each other:

\(|\mathbf{u}|^2-2(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v})+|\mathbf{v}|^2 = |\mathbf{u}|^2+|\mathbf{v}|^2-2|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\cos\theta\)

The \(|\mathbf{u}|^2\) and \(|\mathbf{v}|^2\) terms cancel from both sides, leaving \(-2(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v})=-2|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\cos\theta\), or:

\(\boxed{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\cos\theta}\)

This is a genuine callback, not a coincidence of notation: the same Law of Cosines that solved oblique triangles in Unit 7 is, underneath the algebra, the entire reason the dot product carries angle information at all. Every time you use \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\cos\theta\), you are quietly re-running a Law of Cosines computation you've already done once by hand.

Example 2 — Verifying the Derivation Numerically

Let \(\mathbf{u}=(3,4)\) and \(\mathbf{v}=(-1,2)\). Verify that the component formula and the Law-of-Cosines formula agree.

Solution:

Component dot product: \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=(3)(-1)+(4)(2)=-3+8=5\).

Magnitudes: \(|\mathbf{u}|=\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5\); \(|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{(-1)^2+2^2}=\sqrt5\).

Difference vector: \(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}=(4,2)\), so \(|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{4^2+2^2}=\sqrt{20}\).

Law of Cosines: \(\cos\theta=\dfrac{|\mathbf{u}|^2+|\mathbf{v}|^2-|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}|^2}{2|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|}=\dfrac{25+5-20}{2(5)(\sqrt5)}=\dfrac{10}{10\sqrt5}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt5}\).

Compare with the dot-product formula: \(\cos\theta=\dfrac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|}=\dfrac{5}{5\sqrt5}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt5}\).

Answer: Both methods give \(\cos\theta=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt5}\approx0.447\), so \(\theta\approx63.4^\circ\). ✓ The Law of Cosines, run on the actual triangle these two vectors form, produces exactly the same angle the dot-product shortcut gives instantly — the shortcut is trustworthy because the theorem behind it already checks out.

Example 3

Let \(\mathbf{u}=(3,4)\) and \(\mathbf{v}=(5,-12)\). Find \(\cos\theta\), the cosine of the angle between them.

Solution:

\(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=(3)(5)+(4)(-12)=15-48=-33\). \(|\mathbf{u}|=5\), \(|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{5^2+12^2}=13\).

\(\cos\theta=\dfrac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|}=\dfrac{-33}{5(13)}=-\dfrac{33}{65}\approx-0.508\)

Answer: \(\cos\theta\approx-0.508\), so \(\theta\approx120.5^\circ\). A negative dot product is not an error — it simply means the angle between the vectors is obtuse, exactly as a negative cosine always signals an angle past \(90^\circ\).

💻 On Your Calculator: Checking an Angle with Desmos

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), plot \(\mathbf{u}=(3,4)\) and \(\mathbf{v}=(5,-12)\) as segments from the origin. Desmos's angle tool (or typing an inverse-cosine expression directly, e.g. acos((3*5+4*(-12))/(sqrt(3^2+4^2)*sqrt(5^2+12^2))) in degree mode) will report the same \(\approx120.5^\circ\) Example 3 found by hand — a fast way to check any angle-between-vectors computation before you commit to it on paper.

▶  Try it yourself: Let \(\mathbf{u}=(-2,7)\) and \(\mathbf{v}=(3,4)\). Find \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}\), then find \(\cos\theta\).
\(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=(-2)(3)+(7)(4)=-6+28=22\). \(|\mathbf{u}|=\sqrt{4+49}=\sqrt{53}\), \(|\mathbf{v}|=5\). \(\cos\theta=\dfrac{22}{5\sqrt{53}}\approx0.604\), so \(\theta\approx52.8^\circ\).
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Writing the dot product as if it were a new vector — for instance, “\(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=(12,-10)\)” instead of correctly adding those two products together. The dot product of two vectors is always a single scalar (an ordinary number), never a pair of coordinates. If your answer to a dot-product problem still has two components in it, you have not finished the computation.

Conclusion

The dot product's component formula is quick to compute and its angle formula is quick to interpret, and today's derivation showed they are not two separate facts to memorize but one fact, viewed twice — the second time through the same Law of Cosines that has been sitting in your notebook since Unit 7. Tomorrow puts this formula to work on the question it was built to answer: exactly which angle two vectors make, and the special case — \(\theta=90^\circ\) — where that angle vanishes into perpendicularity altogether.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does it make sense that the dot product returns a scalar rather than a vector, given what it is meant to measure?

2. In the derivation, which specific term cancels from both sides of the equation, and why is that cancellation exactly what makes the formula work out so cleanly?

3. What does a negative dot product tell you about the angle between two vectors — and why should a negative answer never be treated as a sign of a mistake?

4. Example 2 confirmed the dot-product-angle formula on one specific pair of vectors. Why does verifying it once, honestly, give you grounds to trust the general derivation above it, rather than needing to re-verify it every time you use it?
