🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to multiply a vector by a scalar algebraically, by multiplying each component by the scalar.

  • I will be able to describe the graphical effect of scalar multiplication: stretching or shrinking a vector, and reversing its direction when the scalar is negative.

  • I will be able to find the magnitude of a scalar multiple \(k\mathbf{v}\) without recomputing from scratch, using \(|k\mathbf{v}|=|k||\mathbf{v}|\).

📚 VOCABULARY

Scalar multiplication: multiplying a vector \(\mathbf{v}=\langle a,b\rangle\) by a real number (scalar) \(k\), producing \(k\mathbf{v}=\langle ka,kb\rangle\).

Parallel vectors: two nonzero vectors that point along the same line, either in the same direction or in exactly opposite directions; \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(k\mathbf{v}\) are always parallel for any nonzero scalar \(k\).

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

This unit has, so far, always combined two different vectors: adding them tip-to-tail, subtracting one from another. Today's operation is different in kind — it combines a vector with an ordinary number, a scalar, and the result is not a new direction cooperating with an old one, but the same direction, made longer, shorter, or turned to face exactly backward. If a single force of \(\langle3,4\rangle\) newtons represents one worker's effort, doubling that effort to \(\langle6,8\rangle\) newtons does not change what direction the work is aimed — it changes only how much of it there is. That distinction matters for the unit's virtue of service: scaling a contribution up or down is a different act from redirecting it, and vector arithmetic keeps the two acts strictly separate.

Scalar Multiplication, Algebraically and Graphically

\(k\mathbf{v} = k\langle a,b\rangle = \langle ka,\ kb\rangle\)

Multiply every component by \(k\) — never only one of them. Graphically: if \(k>1\), \(k\mathbf{v}\) points the same way as \(\mathbf{v}\) but is longer; if \(0opposite direction, with length scaled by \(|k|\). The magnitude obeys a shortcut worth memorizing: \(|k\mathbf{v}|=|k|\cdot|\mathbf{v}|\) — no need to recompute a square root from scratch every time.

Example 1

Let \(\mathbf{v}=\langle3,4\rangle\), so \(|\mathbf{v}|=5\). Compute \(2\mathbf{v}\), its magnitude, and its direction angle. Compare the direction angle to \(\mathbf{v}\)'s own.

Solution:

\(2\mathbf{v} = \langle 2(3),\ 2(4)\rangle = \langle6,8\rangle\)

Magnitude by the shortcut: \(|2\mathbf{v}|=|2|\cdot|\mathbf{v}|=2(5)=10\) (check directly: \(\sqrt{6^2+8^2}=\sqrt{100}=10\) ✓). Direction angle of \(\mathbf{v}\): \(\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac43\right)\approx53.13^\circ\) (Quadrant I). Direction angle of \(2\mathbf{v}=\langle6,8\rangle\): \(\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac86\right)=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac43\right)\approx53.13^\circ\) — the identical angle, since \(\frac{8}{6}=\frac{4}{3}\).

Answer: \(2\mathbf{v}=\langle6,8\rangle\), magnitude \(10\), direction angle \(\approx53.13^\circ\) — exactly \(\mathbf{v}\)'s own direction, twice as long. A positive scalar never changes the ratio \(\frac{b}{a}\), so it can never change the direction angle.

Example 2

Using the same \(\mathbf{v}=\langle3,4\rangle\), compute \(-1\mathbf{v}\) and find its magnitude and direction angle.

Solution:

\(-\mathbf{v} = \langle -3,-4\rangle\)

Magnitude: \(|-\mathbf{v}|=|-1|\cdot5=5\) (unchanged). Both components are now negative, so \(-\mathbf{v}\) sits in Quadrant III: reference angle \(\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac43\right)\approx53.13^\circ\), so \(\theta=180^\circ+53.13^\circ=233.13^\circ\).

Answer: \(-\mathbf{v}=\langle-3,-4\rangle\), magnitude \(5\), direction angle \(\approx233.13^\circ\) — exactly \(53.13^\circ+180^\circ\), confirming that multiplying by \(-1\) reverses a vector's direction without touching its length.

Example 3

Using \(\mathbf{v}=\langle3,4\rangle\) once more, compute \(\frac12\mathbf{v}\) and \(-3\mathbf{v}\), and state each one's magnitude using the shortcut.

Solution:

\(\tfrac12\mathbf{v} = \left\langle \tfrac32,\ 2\right\rangle \qquad\qquad -3\mathbf{v} = \langle-9,-12\rangle\)

\(\left|\tfrac12\mathbf{v}\right| = \tfrac12(5)=2.5\). \(|-3\mathbf{v}|=|-3|(5)=15\).

Answer: \(\tfrac12\mathbf{v}=\langle1.5,2\rangle\) with magnitude \(2.5\) — same direction as \(\mathbf{v}\), half as long. \(-3\mathbf{v}=\langle-9,-12\rangle\) with magnitude \(15\) — opposite direction, three times as long.

Key Fact: The Sign of \(k\) Controls Direction; the Size of \(k\) Controls Length

These two effects are completely independent. \(k=2\) and \(k=-2\) scale \(\mathbf{v}\) to the exact same new length, but point in opposite directions. \(k=\frac12\) and \(k=2\) point the exact same direction as \(\mathbf{v}\), but scale it to very different lengths. A vector and every one of its nonzero scalar multiples are parallel — they all lie along one shared line through the origin, just at different distances from it and possibly on opposite sides.

▶  Try it yourself: Let \(\mathbf{v}=\langle4,-3\rangle\) (so \(|\mathbf{v}|=5\)). Find \(3\mathbf{v}\) and \(-2\mathbf{v}\), with each one's magnitude.
\(3\mathbf{v}=\langle12,-9\rangle\), magnitude \(3(5)=15\). \(-2\mathbf{v}=\langle-8,6\rangle\), magnitude \(2(5)=10\), pointing opposite to \(\mathbf{v}\).

Before computing, ask your AI tutor to predict in words (no arithmetic yet) what will happen to \(\mathbf{v}=\langle5,-12\rangle\)'s length and direction under \(k=-4\) and under \(k=\frac14\). Then do the arithmetic yourself and grade the AI's prediction: did it correctly separate what the sign of \(k\) controls from what the size of \(k\) controls, or did it blur the two together?
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Multiplying only one component by the scalar — writing \(3\langle2,5\rangle\) as \(\langle6,5\rangle\) instead of \(\langle6,15\rangle\). Scalar multiplication distributes across both components, every time, with no exceptions; skipping the second one silently changes the vector's direction as well as its length, which defeats the entire point of a scalar multiple staying parallel to the original.

Conclusion

Three operations are now in hand: adding vectors combines two directions into one resultant; subtracting finds the gap between two vectors; and scaling stretches, shrinks, or reverses a single vector without ever changing the line it lies on. Together, addition, subtraction, and scalar multiplication are the entire algebra this unit needs. Next week, that algebra turns outward: unit vectors, the linear and trigonometric forms of a vector, and resultants built from magnitudes and directions alone, on the way to genuine forces, wind, and current.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does a positive scalar never change a vector's direction angle, no matter how large or small that scalar is?

2. Why does multiplying by \(-1\) always add exactly \(180^\circ\) to a vector's direction angle?

3. If \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(k\mathbf{v}\) are always parallel, what has to be true about \(k\) for \(k\mathbf{v}\) to point in the exact opposite direction from \(\mathbf{v}\), rather than the same direction?

4. How does keeping "what the sign of \(k\) controls" separate from "what the size of \(k\) controls" connect to this unit's virtue of service — scaling a contribution up or down without ever redirecting it?
