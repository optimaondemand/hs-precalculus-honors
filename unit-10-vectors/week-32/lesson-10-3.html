Adding and Subtracting Vectors Algebraically
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to add and subtract vectors in component form by adding or subtracting corresponding components.

  • I will be able to identify the zero vector and a vector's opposite, and use them to interpret vector subtraction as adding an opposite.

  • I will be able to verify a graphical vector sum or difference by an independent algebraic computation.

  • I will be able to evaluate an AI-generated vector computation, identify an error, and correct it with my own reasoning.

📚 VOCABULARY

Component-wise addition/subtraction: for \(\mathbf{u}=\langle u_1,u_2\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{v}=\langle v_1,v_2\rangle\), \(\mathbf{u}\pm\mathbf{v}=\langle u_1\pm v_1,\ u_2\pm v_2\rangle\) — add or subtract the first components together and the second components together, never across.

Zero vector \(\mathbf{0}=\langle0,0\rangle\): the vector with no magnitude and no defined direction; \(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{u}\) for every vector \(\mathbf{u}\).

Opposite vector \(-\mathbf{u}\): the vector with the same magnitude as \(\mathbf{u}\) but the opposite direction, satisfying \(\mathbf{u}+(-\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{0}\); found by negating both components.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Yesterday you built resultants with a ruler and a steady hand: sliding vectors tip to tail, completing parallelograms, reading a difference off the gap between two tips. Today the picture becomes pure algebra — and the reassuring truth of this lesson is that the algebra never disagrees with the picture. It cannot: component-wise addition and subtraction are simply the tip-to-tail and difference constructions, written in numbers instead of drawn on paper. Where yesterday you cooperated with a compass and a straightedge, today two numbers cooperate with two other numbers — the same virtue of service, expressed in arithmetic.

Adding and Subtracting Component-Wise

\(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} = \langle u_1+v_1,\ u_2+v_2\rangle \qquad\qquad \mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v} = \langle u_1-v_1,\ u_2-v_2\rangle\)

Add (or subtract) the first components together, and separately add (or subtract) the second components together. Nothing is multiplied, and the two components never interact with each other — exactly the rule you already trusted for complex-number addition in Unit 9, because a vector's component form and a complex number's rectangular form are the same bookkeeping wearing two different names.

Example 1

Let \(\mathbf{u}=\langle 4,1\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{v}=\langle 2,3\rangle\) — the same two vectors from yesterday's tip-to-tail construction. Compute \(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\) algebraically.

Solution:

\(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} = \langle 4+2,\ 1+3\rangle = \langle 6,4\rangle\)

Answer: \(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}=\langle6,4\rangle\) — exactly the resultant yesterday's tip-to-tail and parallelogram constructions both landed on. The algebra confirms the picture.

Example 2

Using the same \(\mathbf{u}=\langle 4,1\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{v}=\langle 2,3\rangle\), compute \(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\) algebraically, and verify it matches the difference-vector construction from yesterday's Try It.

Solution:

\(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v} = \langle 4-2,\ 1-3\rangle = \langle 2,-2\rangle\)

Answer: \(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}=\langle2,-2\rangle\). Graphically, this is the vector running from \(\mathbf{v}\)'s tip \((2,3)\) to \(\mathbf{u}\)'s tip \((4,1)\): \(\langle4-2,\ 1-3\rangle=\langle2,-2\rangle\). Same answer, same arrow — the construction from yesterday was computing exactly this subtraction the whole time.

Example 3

Let \(\mathbf{u}=\langle -3,5\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{v}=\langle 6,-2\rangle\). Compute \(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\) and \(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\).

Solution:

\(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} = \langle -3+6,\ 5+(-2)\rangle = \langle 3,3\rangle\)

\(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v} = \langle -3-6,\ 5-(-2)\rangle = \langle -9,7\rangle\)

Answer: \(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}=\langle3,3\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}=\langle-9,7\rangle\). Negative components add and subtract exactly like positive ones — keep every sign, and combine like with like.

🤖 AI CHECKPOINT — FIND THE PLANTED ERROR

A student asked an AI tutor: “Let \(\mathbf{u}=\langle7,-2\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{v}=\langle-3,5\rangle\). Find \(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\).” The AI answered:

“To add vectors, add the magnitudes: \(|\mathbf{u}|=\sqrt{7^2+(-2)^2}=\sqrt{53}\) and \(|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{(-3)^2+5^2}=\sqrt{34}\), so \(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\) has magnitude \(\sqrt{53}+\sqrt{34}\approx13.11\).”

Compute the correct \(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\) yourself, in component form. Then, in a sentence, name exactly what the AI confused — and explain why adding two vectors' magnitudes almost never equals the magnitude of their sum (Example 1's own \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) make a fast check: is \(|\mathbf{u}|+|\mathbf{v}|\) equal to \(|\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}|\)?).

▶  See the fix: what did the AI's answer get wrong?
\(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}=\langle7+(-3),\ -2+5\rangle=\langle4,3\rangle\), with magnitude \(\sqrt{4^2+3^2}=5\) — nowhere near the AI's \(\sqrt{53}+\sqrt{34}\approx13.11\). The AI confused vector addition (add components) with a rule that does not exist: adding magnitudes directly. Two vectors pointing in different directions never simply add their lengths — that would be true only if both vectors pointed the same way. Using Example 1's own vectors, \(|\mathbf{u}|=\sqrt{17}\approx4.12\) and \(|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{13}\approx3.61\), summing to about \(7.73\); but \(|\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}|=|\langle6,4\rangle|=\sqrt{52}\approx7.21\) — close, but not equal, and the gap only grows the more the two vectors' directions differ.
▶  Try it yourself: Let \(\mathbf{u}=\langle5,6\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{v}=\langle8,1\rangle\). Find \(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\), then find \(-\mathbf{v}\) and confirm \(\mathbf{u}+(-\mathbf{v})\) gives the identical answer.
\(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}=\langle5-8,\ 6-1\rangle=\langle-3,5\rangle\). Also, \(-\mathbf{v}=\langle-8,-1\rangle\), so \(\mathbf{u}+(-\mathbf{v})=\langle5+(-8),\ 6+(-1)\rangle=\langle-3,5\rangle\) — identical. Subtracting \(\mathbf{v}\) is always the same as adding its opposite.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Adding or subtracting the components in the wrong pairing — combining \(\mathbf{u}\)'s first component with \(\mathbf{v}\)'s second component, especially under time pressure on a test. Keep the components in strict columns: first-with-first, second-with-second, the same discipline you already use when adding two ordered pairs or two complex numbers in rectangular form. A quick habit that catches this every time: write both vectors stacked vertically, one below the other, before combining.

Conclusion

Vector addition and subtraction turned out to have no new rules at all — only the old rule of combining like components with like, the same rule complex numbers obeyed in rectangular form back in Unit 9. What is new is the confidence that the algebra and the picture are the same fact told twice, in two languages. Tomorrow a third operation joins the other two: multiplying a vector by a single number, a scalar, and watching what that does to a vector's length and direction.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why must component-wise addition always produce the same resultant as the tip-to-tail construction, rather than merely happening to agree in the examples above?

2. Why is subtracting \(\mathbf{v}\) always the same as adding \(-\mathbf{v}\)? What does this tell you about why vector subtraction needs no separate geometric rule of its own?

3. In the AI checkpoint, why does adding two vectors' magnitudes directly almost always give the wrong answer for the magnitude of their sum?

4. What must be true about two vectors \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) for \(|\mathbf{u}|+|\mathbf{v}|\) to actually equal \(|\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}|\)?
