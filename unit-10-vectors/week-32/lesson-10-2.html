Adding and Subtracting Vectors Graphically
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to add two vectors graphically using the tip-to-tail method and identify the resultant vector.

  • I will be able to add two vectors graphically using the parallelogram method and show it produces the same resultant as tip-to-tail.

  • I will be able to construct the difference vector \(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\) graphically and interpret what it represents.

  • I will be able to plot vectors and their resultant using the Desmos graphing calculator to check hand-drawn work.

📚 VOCABULARY

Resultant vector: the single vector that has the same overall effect as two or more vectors combined — the vector sum.

Tip-to-tail method: a method for adding vectors by placing the tail of the second vector at the tip of the first; the resultant runs from the first vector's tail to the second vector's tip.

Parallelogram method: a method for adding vectors by placing both at a common tail, completing the parallelogram they form, and drawing the resultant as the diagonal from that common tail to the opposite corner.

Difference vector \(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\): the vector that, added to \(\mathbf{v}\), produces \(\mathbf{u}\); drawn tail-to-tail, it runs from the tip of \(\mathbf{v}\) to the tip of \(\mathbf{u}\).

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Yesterday's lesson closed with Simon Stevin's wreath of spheres, silently balanced on two inclined planes because the alternative — motion with no cause — was impossible. Stevin's proof did more than settle one clever puzzle about a necklace of spheres: it planted the seed of an idea that engineers spent the rest of the 1600s working out in full, that two forces pulling in two different directions can always be replaced by one single force with exactly the same effect. By the time Isaac Newton wrote the Principia in 1687, that idea had a name and a precise geometric rule: the parallelogram of forces. Today you learn to build that single combined effect — the resultant — by hand, two different ways, and see that both ways agree.

The Tip-to-Tail Method

To add \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) graphically, draw \(\mathbf{u}\) first. Then, without changing its length or direction, slide \(\mathbf{v}\) so that its tail sits exactly at the tip of \(\mathbf{u}\). The resultant \(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\) is the single vector drawn from \(\mathbf{u}\)'s original tail all the way to \(\mathbf{v}\)'s new tip — it is the shortcut across the two-step journey. This matches the algebra you will practice tomorrow: walking \(\langle 4,1\rangle\) and then \(\langle 2,3\rangle\) lands in exactly the same place as walking the single vector \(\langle 6,4\rangle\) once.

Example 1

Let \(\mathbf{u}=\langle 4,1\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{v}=\langle 2,3\rangle\), both starting at the origin. Find \(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\) by the tip-to-tail method, and confirm the resultant algebraically.

Solution:

Place \(\mathbf{u}\) from \((0,0)\) to \((4,1)\). Now slide \(\mathbf{v}=\langle 2,3\rangle\) so its tail sits at \((4,1)\): its tip lands at \((4+2,\ 1+3)=(6,4)\). The resultant runs from \((0,0)\) to \((6,4)\).

Answer: \(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}=\langle 6,4\rangle\). Algebraically, adding components gives the identical answer: \(\langle 4+2,\ 1+3\rangle=\langle 6,4\rangle\) — the picture and the arithmetic agree, as they always will.

Key Fact: The Parallelogram Method Gives the Same Resultant

Instead of sliding \(\mathbf{v}\) to \(\mathbf{u}\)'s tip, draw both \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) from the same starting point, and complete the four-sided figure they form into a parallelogram (a copy of \(\mathbf{v}\) parallel to itself from \(\mathbf{u}\)'s tip, and a copy of \(\mathbf{u}\) parallel to itself from \(\mathbf{v}\)'s tip). The resultant \(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\) is the parallelogram's diagonal, running from the shared starting point to the far corner. This is exactly Stevin's picture, generalized: two directions pulling from one point combine into one diagonal effect. Try it on Example 1's own vectors — \(\mathbf{u}=\langle4,1\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{v}=\langle2,3\rangle\) from the same origin — and the diagonal lands on precisely \((6,4)\), the same resultant tip-to-tail found. The two methods are not two different rules; they are the same rule, drawn two different ways.

💻 On Your Calculator: Plotting Vectors and Resultants

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), plot a vector's tail and tip as two ordered pairs — for \(\mathbf{u}=\langle4,1\rangle\) from the origin, plot (0,0) and (4,1). Click the "+" (Add Item) menu and choose the arrow/vector tool to draw a directed arrow between the two points, or connect them with the segment tool if your version does not offer a dedicated vector object. Repeat, sliding \(\mathbf{v}=\langle2,3\rangle\)'s tail to \(\mathbf{u}\)'s tip at \((4,1)\), landing its tip at \((6,4)\). Finally draw the resultant arrow straight from \((0,0)\) to \((6,4)\) and confirm by eye that it closes the triangle exactly — the visual proof that tip-to-tail addition works.

Example 2

Using \(\mathbf{u}=\langle 6,2\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{v}=\langle 2,5\rangle\) from a common tail at the origin, construct the difference vector \(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\) graphically, and confirm it algebraically.

Solution:

Plot \(\mathbf{u}\)'s tip at \((6,2)\) and \(\mathbf{v}\)'s tip at \((2,5)\), both drawn from the same origin. The difference vector \(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\) is drawn from \(\mathbf{v}\)'s tip to \(\mathbf{u}\)'s tip: from \((2,5)\) to \((6,2)\), a displacement of \(\langle 6-2,\ 2-5\rangle=\langle 4,-3\rangle\).

Answer: \(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}=\langle 4,-3\rangle\). Notice why this works: \(\mathbf{v}\) plus "the vector from \(\mathbf{v}\)'s tip to \(\mathbf{u}\)'s tip" has to land exactly on \(\mathbf{u}\)'s tip — that is only true if that connecting vector really is \(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\).

▶  Try it yourself: Using \(\mathbf{u}=\langle3,2\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{v}=\langle1,4\rangle\) from a common tail, find \(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\) by the parallelogram method.
Completing the parallelogram on \(\mathbf{u}=\langle3,2\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{v}=\langle1,4\rangle\) drawn from the origin, the diagonal runs to \((3+1,\ 2+4)=(4,6)\). So \(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}=\langle4,6\rangle\).
▶  Try it yourself: For \(\mathbf{u}=\langle6,2\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{v}=\langle2,5\rangle\) (Example 2), find \(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}\) graphically. How does it compare to \(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\)?
\(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}\) runs from \(\mathbf{u}\)'s tip to \(\mathbf{v}\)'s tip: \(\langle2-6,\ 5-2\rangle=\langle-4,3\rangle\). This is exactly \(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}=\langle4,-3\rangle\) reversed — same length, opposite direction, since swapping the order of a subtraction always reverses the arrow.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Sliding the second vector to the wrong point when adding tip-to-tail — placing \(\mathbf{v}\)'s tail back at the origin instead of at \(\mathbf{u}\)'s tip. That does not add the vectors; it just redraws \(\mathbf{v}\) on top of \(\mathbf{u}\)'s starting point. The whole point of tip-to-tail is that the second vector's tail moves to the first vector's tip — only then does the resultant correctly represent "first this displacement, then that one."

Conclusion

Two methods, two pictures, one answer: tip-to-tail chains two displacements end to end, and the parallelogram method draws the same two vectors from a shared starting point and reads the resultant off the diagonal — the exact geometry Stevin's balanced spheres were quietly obeying centuries before anyone called it a "vector." Tomorrow you set the pictures aside and add and subtract vectors by pure algebra on their components, and you will use today's own examples to prove that the algebra never disagrees with the arrow.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why must the tip-to-tail method and the parallelogram method always produce the same resultant, given the same two starting vectors?

2. In Example 2, why does the difference vector \(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\) run from \(\mathbf{v}\)'s tip to \(\mathbf{u}\)'s tip, rather than the other way around?

3. How does Stevin's parallelogram of forces connect to this unit's virtue of service — magnitudes and directions cooperating toward a common resultant?

4. If you added three vectors tip-to-tail instead of two, how would you extend the tip-to-tail method to find their combined resultant?
