What Is a Vector? Components, Magnitude, Direction
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to distinguish a vector (magnitude and direction) from a scalar (magnitude alone), and represent a vector as a directed line segment.

  • I will be able to write a vector in component form \(\langle a,b\rangle\) from its initial and terminal points, and place any vector in standard position.

  • I will be able to compute a vector's magnitude \(|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\).

  • I will be able to find a vector's direction angle from \(\tan\theta=\dfrac{b}{a}\), correcting the raw arctangent result for the vector's actual quadrant.

📚 VOCABULARY

Scalar: a quantity with magnitude only — a plain real number, such as a distance of 12 miles or a temperature of 70°.

Vector: a quantity with both magnitude and direction — a displacement of 12 miles northeast, a wind of 20 mph from the southwest. Represented geometrically as a directed line segment (an arrow) and algebraically in component form.

Component form \(\langle a,b\rangle\): the notation for a vector whose horizontal displacement is \(a\) and vertical displacement is \(b\).

Standard position: a vector drawn with its initial point at the origin; every vector, wherever it starts, is equivalent to exactly one vector in standard position with the same magnitude and direction.

Magnitude \(|\mathbf{v}|\): the length of a vector, found with the same distance formula used for any two points: \(|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\) for \(\mathbf{v}=\langle a,b\rangle\).

Direction angle: the angle \(\theta\), measured counterclockwise from the positive \(x\)-axis, that a vector makes; found from \(\tan\theta=\frac{b}{a}\) and corrected for quadrant.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In 1586, a Flemish engineer named Simon Stevin published a short book on the mathematics of weighing and balancing things, and inside it he offered one of the most quietly astonishing proofs in the history of mechanics. He imagined a closed loop of identical spheres, strung together like a necklace, draped over a triangular stand made of two inclined planes joined at the top. If the loop ever started sliding — even by the tiniest amount — it would slide forever, since the necklace looks exactly the same after the slide as before it: a perpetual motion machine, which nature does not allow. Since the loop cannot move, Stevin reasoned backward to the only conclusion left standing: the pull of gravity along each incline had to be in exact balance, in a ratio set only by the lengths of the two inclines. He was so pleased with the argument that he had it engraved beside a picture of the device with the motto Wonder en is gheen wonder — “what appears a wonder is no wonder.” Stevin's balance of forces along two different directions, resolved into a single condition of equilibrium, is the ancestor of an idea every engineer since has depended on: that two forces pulling in two different directions combine into one single, definite, calculable effect. Later mathematicians spent the rest of the 1600s making that idea fully precise as the parallelogram of forces, the rule you will meet tomorrow. This unit's virtue is service — magnitudes and directions cooperating toward a common resultant — and Stevin's necklace of spheres, silent and motionless on its two inclines, is where that cooperation was first proven to hold.

Before any of that combining can happen, you need a precise way to write down a single push, pull, or displacement — and that is the entire business of today's lesson. Every quantity you have measured so far in this course has been a scalar: a plain number, with a size but no direction. A distance of 12 miles is a scalar. But a displacement of 12 miles to the northeast is a different kind of quantity altogether — a vector — because knowing only "12 miles" leaves out exactly the information that matters most for figuring out where you end up. Wind speed of 20 mph is a scalar; wind velocity of 20 mph from the southwest is a vector. A force of 40 newtons is incomplete until you say which way it pushes. Precalculus has quietly been building toward vectors all year: a translation in the complex plane (Unit 9) was already a vector in disguise, sliding every point in the plane the same distance in the same direction.

Directed Line Segments and Component Form

A vector is pictured as an arrow: a directed line segment running from an initial point to a terminal point. Written \(\overrightarrow{AB}\), the arrow runs from \(A\) (the tail) to \(B\) (the tip). To turn that picture into algebra, subtract the initial point's coordinates from the terminal point's coordinates — exactly the same subtraction you would do to find a slope's rise and run: for \(A=(x_1,y_1)\) and \(B=(x_2,y_2)\),

\(\overrightarrow{AB} = \langle x_2-x_1,\ y_2-y_1\rangle\)

The two numbers inside the brackets are the vector's components: how far it moves horizontally, and how far it moves vertically. A vector's component form never mentions where it started — and that is the point. Two arrows of the same length pointing the same way are the same vector, no matter where either one is drawn. That is what it means to say a vector has magnitude and direction, but no fixed position.

Example 1

A hiker walks from \(A(-2,1)\) to \(B(3,4)\). Write the displacement as a vector in component form, and find how far the hiker actually traveled in a straight line (the vector's magnitude).

Solution:

\(\overrightarrow{AB} = \langle 3-(-2),\ 4-1\rangle = \langle 5,\ 3\rangle\)

Magnitude is the distance formula in disguise: \(|\overrightarrow{AB}| = \sqrt{5^2+3^2} = \sqrt{34}\).

Answer: \(\overrightarrow{AB}=\langle 5,3\rangle\), with magnitude \(\sqrt{34}\approx5.83\) units. Notice this is the same vector as the one from the origin to \((5,3)\) — standard position simply slides the same arrow so its tail sits at \((0,0)\).

Direction Angle: Arctangent Needs a Quadrant Check

For \(\mathbf{v}=\langle a,b\rangle\) in standard position, the direction angle \(\theta\) satisfies \(\tan\theta=\dfrac{b}{a}\). The trouble is that every calculator's \(\tan^{-1}\) key is built to return an angle strictly between \(-90^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\) — it can only ever answer for Quadrant I or Quadrant IV, even when your vector actually lives in Quadrant II or III. The fix, familiar from Unit 6's reference angles: compute \(\left|\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)\right|\) to get the reference angle, then use the signs of \(a\) and \(b\) to place \(\theta\) in its true quadrant.

Signs of \((a,b)\)QuadrantDirection angle \(\theta\), reference angle \(r\)
\((+,+)\)I\(\theta = r\)
\((-,+)\)II\(\theta = 180^\circ - r\)
\((-,-)\)III\(\theta = 180^\circ + r\)
\((+,-)\)IV\(\theta = 360^\circ - r\)

Example 2

Find the magnitude and direction angle of \(\mathbf{v}=\langle 5,3\rangle\) (the hiker's vector from Example 1).

Solution:

Magnitude: \(|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{5^2+3^2}=\sqrt{34}\approx5.83\). Both components are positive, so \(\mathbf{v}\) is in Quadrant I, and no correction is needed: \(\theta=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{3}{5}\right)\approx30.96^\circ\).

Answer: \(|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{34}\approx5.83\), \(\theta\approx30.96^\circ\).

Example 3

Find the magnitude and direction angle of \(\mathbf{v}=\langle -4,3\rangle\).

Solution:

Magnitude: \(|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{(-4)^2+3^2}=\sqrt{25}=5\). Reference angle: \(\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)\approx36.87^\circ\). Since \(a<0\) and \(b>0\), \(\mathbf{v}\) is in Quadrant II, so \(\theta=180^\circ-36.87^\circ=143.13^\circ\).

Answer: \(|\mathbf{v}|=5\), \(\theta\approx143.13^\circ\).

Example 4

Find the magnitude and direction angle of \(\mathbf{v}=\langle -5,-12\rangle\).

Solution:

Magnitude: \(|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{(-5)^2+(-12)^2}=\sqrt{25+144}=\sqrt{169}=13\). Reference angle: \(\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{12}{5}\right)\approx67.38^\circ\). Both components are negative, so \(\mathbf{v}\) is in Quadrant III: \(\theta=180^\circ+67.38^\circ=247.38^\circ\).

Answer: \(|\mathbf{v}|=13\), \(\theta\approx247.38^\circ\). This \(5\text{-}12\text{-}13\) triple is worth remembering — it will reappear all unit.

▶  Try it yourself: Find the magnitude and direction angle of \(\mathbf{v}=\langle 8,-6\rangle\).
\(|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{8^2+(-6)^2}=\sqrt{100}=10\). Reference angle \(\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{6}{8}\right)\approx36.87^\circ\). Since \(a>0,\ b<0\), \(\mathbf{v}\) is in Quadrant IV: \(\theta=360^\circ-36.87^\circ=323.13^\circ\).
▶  Try it yourself: A skater glides from \(P(3,-2)\) to \(Q(-1,5)\). Write the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\) in component form and find its magnitude.
\(\overrightarrow{PQ}=\langle -1-3,\ 5-(-2)\rangle=\langle -4,\ 7\rangle\). \(|\overrightarrow{PQ}|=\sqrt{(-4)^2+7^2}=\sqrt{65}\approx8.06\).
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Reporting whatever a calculator's \(\tan^{-1}\) key returns as the final direction angle, without checking the quadrant. A calculator has no idea whether your vector's second component is positive or negative once you have divided \(b\) by \(a\) — it only ever answers in \((-90^\circ,90^\circ)\). For \(\mathbf{v}=\langle -4,3\rangle\) in Example 3, a calculator returns \(\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{3}{-4}\right)\approx-36.87^\circ\), which is not this vector's direction angle at all — it is the reference angle with the wrong sign attached. Always plot (or at least picture) the quadrant first, then use the table above to place \(\theta\) correctly.

Conclusion

Every vector you will meet this unit — a wind, a force, a velocity, a resultant of several forces at once — reduces to exactly what you practiced today: a pair of components, a magnitude found by the distance formula, and a direction angle found by arctangent with a quadrant check. Tomorrow those single arrows start cooperating: you will place two vectors tip to tail, or build the parallelogram Stevin's balance of forces first pointed toward, and watch two directions combine into one resultant.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does component form never record where a vector starts — and why is that actually a feature, not a missing piece of information?

2. Stevin's wreath of spheres could never start sliding without becoming a perpetual motion machine. Why does that fact alone force the two inclines' pulls into balance, without Stevin ever needing to measure a single force directly?

3. Why does a calculator's \(\tan^{-1}\) key always return an angle in Quadrant I or IV, no matter what vector you actually give it?

4. Give a real example (other than wind or force) of a quantity that is a vector, and a related quantity that looks similar but is really only a scalar. What information does the scalar version leave out?
