Resultants from Magnitudes and Directions
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to convert several vectors, each given only by magnitude and direction angle, into component form.

  • I will be able to add those components to find a resultant vector, then convert the resultant back into magnitude-and-direction form.

  • I will be able to explain why the resultant of several vectors can be smaller than any single vector that forms it, and why it can be zero.

📚 VOCABULARY

Resultant: the single vector that results from adding two or more vectors — the net effect of every contributing vector, combined.

Equilibrium: the special case in which a resultant is the zero vector — every contributing vector's effect is exactly balanced out by the others.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Lessons 10-2 and 10-3 taught you to add vectors that were already handed to you as components. In practice, though, vectors rarely arrive that way. A pilot is told a wind's speed and compass heading, not its \((x,y)\) parts. A rigger is told each rope's tension and angle, not a coordinate pair. Today's lesson supplies the missing bridge: given any collection of vectors specified only by magnitude and direction, convert each one to components using exactly the trigonometry you practiced in Lessons 10-6 and 10-7, add the components the ordinary way, and then convert the sum — the resultant — back into a magnitude and a direction that a person, not just a coordinate grid, can act on.

The Three-Step Resultant

Step 1. For each vector given as magnitude \(m\) at direction angle \(\theta\), convert to components: \((m\cos\theta,\ m\sin\theta)\).

Step 2. Add all the components, coordinate by coordinate, to get the resultant's components.

Step 3. Convert the resultant's components back to magnitude and direction — magnitude by the distance formula, direction by an inverse tangent adjusted for the resultant's quadrant.

Example 1

Two ropes pull on a ring: one with a force of magnitude \(8\) at \(30^\circ\), the other with a force of magnitude \(5\) at \(120^\circ\). Find the resultant force's magnitude and direction.

Solution:

Step 1 — components:

\(\mathbf{F}_1 = (8\cos30^\circ,\,8\sin30^\circ) = \left(8\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2},\,8\cdot\dfrac12\right) = (4\sqrt3,\,4) \approx (6.93,\,4.00)\)

\(\mathbf{F}_2 = (5\cos120^\circ,\,5\sin120^\circ) = \left(5\cdot\left(-\dfrac12\right),\,5\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) = \left(-2.5,\,2.5\sqrt3\right) \approx (-2.50,\,4.33)\)

Step 2 — add: \(\mathbf{F}_1+\mathbf{F}_2 \approx (6.93-2.50,\ 4.00+4.33) = (4.43,\ 8.33)\).

Step 3 — convert back: \(|\mathbf{F}_1+\mathbf{F}_2|=\sqrt{4.43^2+8.33^2}=\sqrt{19.6+69.4}=\sqrt{89.0}\approx9.43\). Both components positive, Quadrant I: \(\theta=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{8.33}{4.43}\right)\approx62.0^\circ\).

Answer: The resultant force has magnitude \(\approx9.43\) at direction \(\approx62.0^\circ\) — a single force, weaker in magnitude than a simple sum of \(8\) and \(5\) would suggest, because the two ropes are not pulling in quite the same direction.

🤝 Service: Cooperation Toward One Common Result

A resultant is arithmetic, but it is also a small picture of this unit's virtue. No single vector in Example 1 pointed at \(62.0^\circ\) — that direction belongs to neither rope alone, only to both of them contributing together. Service works the same way: what a team accomplishes is rarely identical to what any one member intended alone, and it is often better, precisely because it is the honest sum of several people's honest efforts pulling in their own true directions. The next example carries this further — contributors that do not merely blend, but that perfectly balance one another out.

Example 2

Three vectors, each of magnitude \(10\), point at direction angles \(0^\circ\), \(120^\circ\), and \(240^\circ\) — evenly spaced around a full circle. Find their resultant.

Solution:

\(\mathbf{v}_1=(10\cos0^\circ,10\sin0^\circ)=(10,\,0)\)

\(\mathbf{v}_2=(10\cos120^\circ,10\sin120^\circ)=\left(-5,\,5\sqrt3\right)\)

\(\mathbf{v}_3=(10\cos240^\circ,10\sin240^\circ)=\left(-5,\,-5\sqrt3\right)\)

Sum: \((10-5-5,\ 0+5\sqrt3-5\sqrt3) = (0,\,0)\).

Answer: The resultant is the zero vector — equilibrium. Three forces of equal strength, spaced evenly around the compass, cooperate so completely that none of them dominates; the ring they pull on does not move at all. This is the geometry behind why a three-legged stool sits stable and a tug-of-war with three evenly matched, evenly spaced teams goes nowhere.

Example 3

Two vectors, each of magnitude \(6\), point at direction angles \(45^\circ\) and \(135^\circ\). Find their resultant.

Solution:

\(\mathbf{v}_1=(6\cos45^\circ,6\sin45^\circ)=\left(3\sqrt2,\,3\sqrt2\right)\)

\(\mathbf{v}_2=(6\cos135^\circ,6\sin135^\circ)=\left(-3\sqrt2,\,3\sqrt2\right)\)

Sum: \((3\sqrt2-3\sqrt2,\ 3\sqrt2+3\sqrt2)=(0,\,6\sqrt2)\).

Answer: The resultant is \((0,6\sqrt2)\approx(0,8.49)\), magnitude \(6\sqrt2\) pointing straight up at \(\theta=90^\circ\). The two vectors' horizontal parts, being exact opposites, canceled completely; their vertical parts, being identical, doubled. Symmetry made this resultant predictable without a single decimal of rounding.

💻 On Your Calculator: Watching Vectors Combine

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), plot Example 1's two force vectors as ordered pairs, (8*cos(30 deg), 8*sin(30 deg)) and (5*cos(120 deg), 5*sin(120 deg)), then plot their sum (8*cos(30 deg)+5*cos(120 deg), 8*sin(30 deg)+5*sin(120 deg)). Draw all three as segments from the origin and notice the resultant sits between the two originals in direction — a visual confirmation of Step 3's arithmetic before you even compute the angle by hand.

▶  Try it yourself: Find the resultant of magnitude \(5\) at \(0^\circ\) and magnitude \(5\) at \(90^\circ\).
\(\mathbf{v}_1=(5,0)\), \(\mathbf{v}_2=(0,5)\). Sum \(=(5,5)\). Magnitude: \(\sqrt{5^2+5^2}=\sqrt{50}=5\sqrt2\approx7.07\). Direction: both components positive (Quadrant I), \(\theta=\tan^{-1}(1)=45^\circ\) — exactly halfway between the two original directions, since the two vectors are equal in magnitude.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Adding the magnitudes and directions directly — treating "resultant" as if it meant \(m_1+m_2\) at some average of \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\). It does not. Example 1's two magnitudes, \(8\) and \(5\), sum to \(13\), but the actual resultant's magnitude is only about \(9.43\) — nowhere close. Magnitudes and direction angles cannot be added on their own; only the components can be added directly. Always convert to components first, add, and only then convert the sum back to magnitude and direction.

Conclusion

Three steps — convert to components, add, convert back — turn any collection of magnitude-and-direction vectors into one honest answer: a single resultant that tells you exactly how hard and which way the combined effect actually pushes. Sometimes that combination is stronger than any one contributor and sometimes, as Example 2 showed, several honest contributions cancel into perfect stillness; both outcomes are the arithmetic of service done faithfully. Tomorrow this same three-step method goes to work on a genuinely moving problem: an airplane's own heading and airspeed, combined with the wind's speed and direction, to find out where the plane is actually going.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why can't you simply add two vectors' magnitudes together to find a resultant's magnitude, the way the Common Mistake box describes?

2. In Example 2, why does perfect symmetry among three equal, evenly spaced vectors guarantee a resultant of exactly zero, rather than merely a small number?

3. In what sense is a resultant vector "the honest sum" of its contributors, in a way that connects to this unit's virtue of service?

4. If you added a fourth vector to Example 1's two ropes, would you have to start the three-step process over, or could you build on the resultant you already found? Explain.
