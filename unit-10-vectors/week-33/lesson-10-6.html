Linear Form and Trigonometric Form
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to write a vector given in component form in linear form, \(a\mathbf{i}+b\mathbf{j}\), and explain what \(\mathbf{i}\) and \(\mathbf{j}\) each stand for.

  • I will be able to write a vector in trigonometric form, \(|\mathbf{v}|(\cos\theta\,\mathbf{i}+\sin\theta\,\mathbf{j})\), by finding its magnitude and direction angle.

  • I will be able to convert a vector given in trigonometric form back into component or linear form.

  • I will be able to name the historical link between Hamilton's quaternions and the vector notation used in this lesson.

📚 VOCABULARY

Linear form: a vector \(\mathbf{v}=(a,b)\) written as \(a\mathbf{i}+b\mathbf{j}\), where \(\mathbf{i}=(1,0)\) and \(\mathbf{j}=(0,1)\) are the standard unit vectors along the \(x\)- and \(y\)-axes.

Standard unit vectors \(\mathbf{i}\), \(\mathbf{j}\): the two perpendicular unit vectors that every planar vector can be built from — \(a\mathbf{i}\) is the horizontal part of \(\mathbf{v}\), and \(b\mathbf{j}\) is the vertical part.

Trigonometric form: a vector written as \(|\mathbf{v}|(\cos\theta\,\mathbf{i}+\sin\theta\,\mathbf{j})\), where \(|\mathbf{v}|\) is the magnitude and \(\theta\) is the direction angle — the vector's own magnitude and direction, spelled out explicitly in the notation itself.

Quaternion: a four-part number, \(a+bi+cj+dk\), invented by William Rowan Hamilton to extend complex-number arithmetic into three dimensions; the ancestor of the vector notation used in this lesson.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

On the morning of October 16, 1843, the Irish mathematician William Rowan Hamilton was walking along the towpath of the Royal Canal in Dublin with his wife, on his way to a meeting of the Royal Irish Academy. He had been stuck for years on a single question: complex numbers \(a+bi\) let you rotate and scale a flat plane by multiplying, so what set of numbers would do the same job in three-dimensional space? Walking past Broom(e) Bridge, the answer arrived all at once — not three numbers, but four: \(i^2=j^2=k^2=ijk=-1\). Certain the insight would slip away before he reached paper, Hamilton stopped and carved the equation directly into the stone of the bridge. He called this new four-part number system the quaternion, and spent the rest of his career convinced it was the deepest discovery of his life.

Quaternions worked, but they were heavier machinery than most physicists actually needed. Forty years later, the American physicist Josiah Willard Gibbs and the English engineer Oliver Heaviside, working independently, noticed that the useful part of Hamilton's four-part system was really just the \(i\), \(j\), \(k\) directions themselves — the three-dimensional cousins of the \(\mathbf{i}\) and \(\mathbf{j}\) you are about to use. They stripped away the extra quaternion machinery and kept only what modeled forces, velocities, and directions cleanly: the vector algebra this unit teaches. The notation you are learning today, \(a\mathbf{i}+b\mathbf{j}\), is Gibbs and Heaviside's practical distillation of Hamilton's more elaborate discovery — a case of two later mathematicians doing a genuine service to the field by simplifying, not by inventing something new from nothing.

Linear Form: Naming the Same Vector a New Way

Every vector you have written as \(\mathbf{v}=(a,b)\) since Lesson 10-1 can be written instead as \(\mathbf{v}=a\mathbf{i}+b\mathbf{j}\), where \(\mathbf{i}=(1,0)\) and \(\mathbf{j}=(0,1)\). Nothing about the vector changes — \(a\mathbf{i}\) is still \(a\) units of horizontal motion and \(b\mathbf{j}\) is still \(b\) units of vertical motion. Linear form simply writes the component form out as a sum, which makes vector addition read exactly like combining like terms: \((a\mathbf{i}+b\mathbf{j})+(c\mathbf{i}+d\mathbf{j})=(a+c)\mathbf{i}+(b+d)\mathbf{j}\).

Example 1

Write \(\mathbf{v}=(6,8)\) in linear form, then find its magnitude and direction angle and write it in trigonometric form.

Solution:

Linear form: \(\mathbf{v}=6\mathbf{i}+8\mathbf{j}\).

\(|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{6^2+8^2}=\sqrt{36+64}=\sqrt{100}=10\). Both components positive, Quadrant I: \(\theta=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{8}{6}\right)\approx53.13^\circ\).

Answer: \(\mathbf{v}=6\mathbf{i}+8\mathbf{j}=10(\cos53.13^\circ\,\mathbf{i}+\sin53.13^\circ\,\mathbf{j})\). Three names, one vector.

Example 2

Convert \(\mathbf{w}=8(\cos150^\circ\,\mathbf{i}+\sin150^\circ\,\mathbf{j})\) to linear form.

Solution:

\(\cos150^\circ=-\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\), \(\sin150^\circ=\dfrac12\).

\(\mathbf{w}=8\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)\mathbf{i}+8\left(\dfrac12\right)\mathbf{j} = -4\sqrt3\,\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j}\)

Check: \(|-4\sqrt3\,\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j}|=\sqrt{(4\sqrt3)^2+4^2}=\sqrt{48+16}=\sqrt{64}=8\). ✓

Answer: \(\mathbf{w}=-4\sqrt3\,\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j}\approx-6.93\,\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j}\).

Same Idea, Two Uniforms

Look closely at trigonometric form and recall Lesson 9-4's polar form of a complex number, \(z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\). They are not merely similar — they are the identical idea, wearing two different uniforms. A magnitude riding on a cosine and a sine, describing a location by how far and which way, is exactly the move Argand and Gauss made for complex numbers and exactly the move you are making for vectors today. Unit 10's closing lesson, 10-15, will make this equivalence completely explicit; for now, simply notice that you already know this trick — you are only learning its second name.

Example 3

Convert \(\mathbf{v}=-3\mathbf{i}+3\mathbf{j}\) to trigonometric form.

Solution:

\(|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{(-3)^2+3^2}=\sqrt{9+9}=\sqrt{18}=3\sqrt2\). Since \(x<0\), \(y>0\), Quadrant II. Reference angle: \(\tan^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{3}{3}\right)=45^\circ\), so \(\theta=180^\circ-45^\circ=135^\circ\).

Answer: \(\mathbf{v}=3\sqrt2(\cos135^\circ\,\mathbf{i}+\sin135^\circ\,\mathbf{j})\). Notice this is precisely Unit 9's complex number \(-3+3i=3\sqrt2\,\mathrm{cis}\,135^\circ\), with \(i\) traded for \(\mathbf{j}\) and a different name on the same address.

Example 4

Convert \(\mathbf{u}=6(\cos240^\circ\,\mathbf{i}+\sin240^\circ\,\mathbf{j})\) to linear form.

Solution:

\(\cos240^\circ=-\dfrac12\), \(\sin240^\circ=-\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\).

\(\mathbf{u}=6\left(-\dfrac12\right)\mathbf{i}+6\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)\mathbf{j}=-3\mathbf{i}-3\sqrt3\,\mathbf{j}\)

Check: \(|-3\mathbf{i}-3\sqrt3\,\mathbf{j}|=\sqrt{9+27}=\sqrt{36}=6\). ✓

Answer: \(\mathbf{u}=-3\mathbf{i}-3\sqrt3\,\mathbf{j}\approx-3\mathbf{i}-5.20\,\mathbf{j}\).

💻 On Your Calculator: Confirming a Trigonometric-Form Conversion

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), plot Example 3's vector as the ordered pair (-3, 3), then check its magnitude by typing distance((-3,3),(0,0)) and confirming it returns \(3\sqrt2\approx4.24\). Then plot (6*cos(240 deg), 6*sin(240 deg)) directly and confirm it lands on the same point as your hand-converted answer to Example 4, \((-3,-3\sqrt3)\).

▶  Try it yourself: Convert \(\mathbf{v}=(0,-5)\) to trigonometric form.
\(|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{0^2+(-5)^2}=5\). The vector points straight down the negative \(y\)-axis, so \(\theta=270^\circ\). \(\mathbf{v}=5(\cos270^\circ\,\mathbf{i}+\sin270^\circ\,\mathbf{j})\). Check: \(\cos270^\circ=0\), \(\sin270^\circ=-1\), giving \(5(0)\mathbf{i}+5(-1)\mathbf{j}=-5\mathbf{j}=(0,-5)\). ✓
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Finding the reference angle correctly and then forgetting to adjust it for the quadrant — the exact slip Unit 9 warned about for complex numbers, and it costs you just as much here. For \(\mathbf{v}=-3\mathbf{i}+3\mathbf{j}\) in Example 3, the reference angle is \(45^\circ\), but the vector itself sits in Quadrant II, so the true direction angle is \(180^\circ-45^\circ=135^\circ\), not \(45^\circ\). Always ask which quadrant the original components place the vector in before trusting the raw output of an inverse tangent.

Conclusion

A vector now has three names that all describe the same arrow: component form for reading off horizontal and vertical distance at a glance, linear form for treating addition like combining like terms, and trigonometric form for reading off magnitude and direction at a glance — the very form Gibbs and Heaviside distilled out of Hamilton's more elaborate quaternions so that engineers could use vectors without needing all four of Hamilton's dimensions. Tomorrow's lesson asks you to move fluently among all three forms on command, including catching an AI tutor that skips exactly the quadrant step this lesson's Common Mistake just warned you about.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. What problem was Hamilton actually trying to solve when he arrived at quaternions on Broom Bridge, and why did complex numbers alone not answer it?

2. In what sense did Gibbs and Heaviside perform a service to mathematics rather than a discovery, when they distilled vector algebra out of Hamilton's quaternions?

3. Why is trigonometric form for a vector, \(|\mathbf{v}|(\cos\theta\,\mathbf{i}+\sin\theta\,\mathbf{j})\), essentially the same idea as trigonometric form for a complex number, \(r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\), from Lesson 9-5?

4. Why must you determine a vector's quadrant from its original components before trusting an inverse tangent's raw output for the direction angle?
