Converting Between Vector Forms
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to rewrite any vector fluently among component, linear, and trigonometric forms, choosing whichever form a problem calls for.

  • I will be able to identify the exact step an AI tutor skips when it converts a vector into the wrong quadrant, and correct the error with my own reasoning.

  • I will be able to verify a form conversion by converting the result back and confirming it matches the original vector.

📚 VOCABULARY

Reference angle: the acute angle a vector's direction line makes with the \(x\)-axis, computed as \(\tan^{-1}\left|\dfrac{b}{a}\right|\) — the raw output of an inverse tangent, before any quadrant adjustment.

Round-trip verification: converting a vector from one form to another and then back again, to confirm the two forms genuinely describe the same vector.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

You now hold three names for the same vector — component form \((a,b)\), linear form \(a\mathbf{i}+b\mathbf{j}\), and trigonometric form \(|\mathbf{v}|(\cos\theta\,\mathbf{i}+\sin\theta\,\mathbf{j})\) — and Lesson 10-6 asked you to move between any two of them one direction at a time. Today's work is fluency: converting in whichever direction a problem hands you, and, crucially, checking your own conversion by converting back. That check matters for a reason more serious than tidiness. Every conversion from component form to trigonometric form runs through an inverse tangent, and an inverse tangent, by itself, only ever reports a reference angle between \(0^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\) — it has no idea which quadrant your vector actually lives in. You supply that fact yourself, by looking at the signs of the original components. Skip that step, and the magnitude comes out correct while the direction comes out entirely wrong — not off by a rounding error, but pointing somewhere else on the compass altogether.

Example 1 — Component to Trigonometric

Convert \(\mathbf{v}=(7,-7)\) to trigonometric form.

Solution:

\(|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{7^2+(-7)^2}=\sqrt{49+49}=\sqrt{98}=7\sqrt2\). Reference angle: \(\tan^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{7}{7}\right)=45^\circ\). Since \(x>0,y<0\), \(\mathbf{v}\) is in Quadrant IV: \(\theta=360^\circ-45^\circ=315^\circ\).

Answer: \(\mathbf{v}=7\sqrt2(\cos315^\circ\,\mathbf{i}+\sin315^\circ\,\mathbf{j})\). Check: \(7\sqrt2\cos315^\circ=7\sqrt2\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}=7\); \(7\sqrt2\sin315^\circ=7\sqrt2\cdot\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)=-7\). ✓ Back to \((7,-7)\).

Example 2 — Trigonometric to Component

Convert \(\mathbf{w}=10(\cos210^\circ\,\mathbf{i}+\sin210^\circ\,\mathbf{j})\) to component form.

Solution:

\(\cos210^\circ=-\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\), \(\sin210^\circ=-\dfrac12\).

\(\mathbf{w}=\left(10\cdot\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right),\ 10\cdot\left(-\dfrac12\right)\right)=(-5\sqrt3,-5)\)

Answer: \(\mathbf{w}\approx(-8.66,-5)\). Check: \(\sqrt{(-5\sqrt3)^2+(-5)^2}=\sqrt{75+25}=\sqrt{100}=10\). ✓

Example 3 — The Full Round Trip

Starting from \(\mathbf{v}=(2,-2\sqrt3)\), convert to trigonometric form, then to linear form, then verify you are back where you started.

Solution:

Component → trigonometric: \(|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{2^2+(2\sqrt3)^2}=\sqrt{4+12}=\sqrt{16}=4\). Reference angle: \(\tan^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{2\sqrt3}{2}\right)=\tan^{-1}(\sqrt3)=60^\circ\). Since \(x>0,y<0\), Quadrant IV: \(\theta=360^\circ-60^\circ=300^\circ\). So \(\mathbf{v}=4(\cos300^\circ\,\mathbf{i}+\sin300^\circ\,\mathbf{j})\).

Trigonometric → linear: \(\cos300^\circ=\dfrac12\), \(\sin300^\circ=-\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\), so \(\mathbf{v}=4\left(\dfrac12\right)\mathbf{i}+4\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)\mathbf{j}=2\mathbf{i}-2\sqrt3\,\mathbf{j}\).

Answer: \(2\mathbf{i}-2\sqrt3\,\mathbf{j}\) is exactly \((2,-2\sqrt3)\) written in linear form — the round trip returns the original vector. ✓ Whenever a conversion and its reverse do not return your starting point, the error is in the conversion, not in the check.

🤖 AI CHECKPOINT — FIND THE PLANTED ERROR

A student asked an AI tutor: “Convert \(\mathbf{v}=(-5,-12)\) to trigonometric form.” The AI answered:

“\(|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{(-5)^2+(-12)^2}=\sqrt{169}=13\). \(\theta=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{-12}{-5}\right)=\tan^{-1}(2.4)\approx67.38^\circ\). So \(\mathbf{v}=13(\cos67.38^\circ\,\mathbf{i}+\sin67.38^\circ\,\mathbf{j})\).”

The magnitude is correct. Is the direction angle correct? Determine which quadrant \((-5,-12)\) actually lies in, find the true direction angle, and verify your corrected trigonometric form converts back to \((-5,-12)\). Then, in a sentence, name the exact step the AI skipped.

▶  See the fix: what did the AI's answer miss?
Both \(-5\) and \(-12\) are negative, so \((-5,-12)\) lies in Quadrant III — not Quadrant I, where \(67.38^\circ\) would point. The true direction angle is \(\theta=180^\circ+67.38^\circ=247.38^\circ\). Check: \(13\cos247.38^\circ\approx-5\) and \(13\sin247.38^\circ\approx-12\). ✓ The correct form is \(\mathbf{v}=13(\cos247.38^\circ\,\mathbf{i}+\sin247.38^\circ\,\mathbf{j})\). The AI computed only the reference angle from the ratio \(\dfrac{-12}{-5}\) — and because the two negatives canceled inside the fraction, the ratio looked exactly like a Quadrant I ratio. It never checked the original signs of \(a\) and \(b\) to place the angle in the correct quadrant.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
This is the identical failure mode you were asked to catch in Lesson 9-7's De Moivre checkpoint: a tool that gets the arithmetic exactly right on the piece it computes, while silently skipping the one step — there, adding \(360^\circ k\) for every root; here, checking the quadrant — that a human is expected to supply from context. An AI tutor is not being deceptive when this happens; it is simply pattern-matching the arithmetic you gave it, and the quadrant is not arithmetic — it is a fact about where the original vector actually lives that has to be read off the signs of \(a\) and \(b\), not off the ratio between them.

💻 On Your Calculator: Catching a Quadrant Error by Eye

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), plot (-5, -12) and, on the same graph, plot (13*cos(67.38 deg), 13*sin(67.38 deg)) — the AI's uncorrected answer — next to (13*cos(247.38 deg), 13*sin(247.38 deg)), your corrected answer. The AI's point lands in Quadrant I, nowhere near \((-5,-12)\); your corrected point lands exactly on it. A single glance at the graph is often the fastest way to catch a quadrant error before you have even finished the algebra.

▶  Try it yourself: Convert \(\mathbf{v}=(0,6)\) to trigonometric form.
\(|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{0^2+6^2}=6\). The vector points straight up the positive \(y\)-axis, so no reference-angle triangle even applies here — \(\theta=90^\circ\) directly. \(\mathbf{v}=6(\cos90^\circ\,\mathbf{i}+\sin90^\circ\,\mathbf{j})\). Check: \(6\cos90^\circ=0\), \(6\sin90^\circ=6\). ✓
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Reversing which number is the magnitude and which is the angle when writing trigonometric form — for instance, writing \(\theta(\cos|\mathbf{v}|\,\mathbf{i}+\sin|\mathbf{v}|\,\mathbf{j})\) instead of \(|\mathbf{v}|(\cos\theta\,\mathbf{i}+\sin\theta\,\mathbf{j})\). The magnitude always sits outside the parentheses as the thing multiplying the whole expression; the angle only ever appears inside the cosine and sine. If your "trigonometric form" has an angle sitting outside the parentheses, the notation itself is telling you something has gone wrong.

Conclusion

Fluency across three forms means being able to start anywhere and land anywhere — component to trigonometric, trigonometric to linear, and back again with a check that proves it. The one place this fluency has real teeth is the quadrant step: it is invisible in the arithmetic, easy for a tool to skip, and the entire reason a direction angle can come out looking plausible while pointing nowhere near the original vector. Tomorrow, you put every form to work at once: given several vectors specified only by magnitude and direction, you will convert each to components, add them, and convert the sum back — the first time this unit's virtue, service, shows up as vectors literally combining forces toward one shared result.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does an inverse tangent alone never tell you which quadrant a vector is actually in, no matter how carefully you compute it?

2. In Example 3, what would it have meant if the linear form you ended with had not matched the component form you started with — where would you look first to find the error?

3. Why did the AI's quadrant error in this lesson's checkpoint produce a completely different point on the plane rather than just a slightly-off answer?

4. What habit could you build into every conversion you do — today, on a test, or with an AI tool — that would catch a quadrant error before you accepted the final answer?
