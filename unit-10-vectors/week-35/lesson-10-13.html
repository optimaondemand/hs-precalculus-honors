Projections and Work
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to compute the vector projection \(\text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}=\left(\dfrac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{v}|^2}\right)\mathbf{v}\) of one vector onto another, and interpret it as the piece of \(\mathbf{u}\) that lies along \(\mathbf{v}\)'s direction.

  • I will be able to decompose a vector into a component parallel to a chosen direction and a component perpendicular to it, and verify the decomposition using the dot product.

  • I will be able to apply the work formula \(W=\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{d}\) to compute the work a force does over a displacement, and explain why only the force's component along the displacement contributes.

📚 VOCABULARY

Vector projection \(\text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}\): the vector component of \(\mathbf{u}\) that lies along the direction of \(\mathbf{v}\), given by \(\text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}=\left(\dfrac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{v}|^2}\right)\mathbf{v}\).

Scalar projection (component): the signed length of \(\mathbf{u}\)'s shadow along \(\mathbf{v}\), \(\text{comp}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}=\dfrac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{v}|}\), which equals \(|\mathbf{u}|\cos\theta\) for \(\theta\) the angle between \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\).

Orthogonal decomposition: writing \(\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}_{\parallel}+\mathbf{u}_{\perp}\), where \(\mathbf{u}_{\parallel}=\text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}\) is parallel to \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\mathbf{u}_{\perp}=\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{\parallel}\) is perpendicular to \(\mathbf{v}\).

Work: in mechanics, the energy transferred when a constant force \(\mathbf{F}\) acts through a displacement \(\mathbf{d}\), computed as \(W=\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{d}=|\mathbf{F}||\mathbf{d}|\cos\theta\).

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In 1586, the Flemish engineer Simon Stevin settled a long-standing argument about forces on a slope with a single, almost playful thought experiment. Imagine a closed loop of beads — a “wreath of spheres” — draped over a triangular wedge, part of it hanging along the steep face and part along the shallow face. If the wreath ever slid on its own, it would slide forever, a perpetual motion with no cause — which Stevin took to be plainly false. So the wreath must hang in balance, and from that single fact he could read off exactly how much of a hanging weight's pull acts along a slope versus into it. Stevin had discovered, without a shred of trigonometry as we know it today, that a force can honestly be split into a piece that matters for a given direction and a piece that does not. That splitting is exactly what this lesson makes precise.

From Angle to Formula: The Vector Projection

Lessons 10-11 and 10-12 gave you the dot-product-angle formula \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\cos\theta\). Divide both sides by \(|\mathbf{v}|\) and you get the scalar projection, \(\text{comp}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}=\dfrac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{v}|}=|\mathbf{u}|\cos\theta\) — a single number telling you how far \(\mathbf{u}\) reaches in \(\mathbf{v}\)'s direction, positive if \(\mathbf{u}\) leans toward \(\mathbf{v}\) and negative if it leans away. To turn that number back into an actual vector pointing along \(\mathbf{v}\), multiply it by \(\mathbf{v}\)'s own unit vector, \(\dfrac{\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{v}|}\): \(\text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}=\left(\dfrac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{v}|}\right)\dfrac{\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{v}|}=\left(\dfrac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{v}|^2}\right)\mathbf{v}\). Nothing here is new machinery — it is the angle formula, divided through twice.

Example 1

Find \(\text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}\) for \(\mathbf{u}=\langle4,3\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{v}=\langle5,0\rangle\).

Solution:

\(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=4(5)+3(0)=20\). \(|\mathbf{v}|^2=5^2+0^2=25\).

\(\text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}=\dfrac{20}{25}\langle5,0\rangle=\dfrac{4}{5}\langle5,0\rangle=\langle4,0\rangle\)

Answer: \(\langle4,0\rangle\). Since \(\mathbf{v}\) points straight along the horizontal axis, the projection should simply recover \(\mathbf{u}\)'s horizontal component — and it does. This is a useful sanity check to run whenever \(\mathbf{v}\) is an axis vector.

Example 2 — A Full Decomposition

Decompose \(\mathbf{u}=\langle1,3\rangle\) into a component parallel to \(\mathbf{v}=\langle3,4\rangle\) and a component perpendicular to it. Verify both the perpendicularity and the Pythagorean relationship between the two pieces and \(\mathbf{u}\) itself.

Solution:

\(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=1(3)+3(4)=15\). \(|\mathbf{v}|^2=3^2+4^2=25\).

\(\mathbf{u}_{\parallel}=\text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}=\dfrac{15}{25}\langle3,4\rangle=\langle1.8,\,2.4\rangle \qquad \mathbf{u}_{\perp}=\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{\parallel}=\langle1-1.8,\,3-2.4\rangle=\langle-0.8,\,0.6\rangle\)

Perpendicularity check: \(\mathbf{u}_{\perp}\cdot\mathbf{v}=(-0.8)(3)+(0.6)(4)=-2.4+2.4=0\). ✓

Pythagorean check: \(|\mathbf{u}_{\parallel}|=\dfrac{15}{5}=3\), so \(|\mathbf{u}_{\parallel}|^2=9\); \(|\mathbf{u}_{\perp}|^2=(-0.8)^2+0.6^2=0.64+0.36=1\); and \(|\mathbf{u}|^2=1^2+3^2=10\). Indeed \(9+1=10\). ✓

Answer: \(\mathbf{u}_{\parallel}=\langle1.8,2.4\rangle\), \(\mathbf{u}_{\perp}=\langle-0.8,0.6\rangle\). Whenever \(\mathbf{u}\) is split into a piece parallel to \(\mathbf{v}\) and a piece perpendicular to it, the two pieces form the legs of a right triangle whose hypotenuse is \(\mathbf{u}\) itself — a genuinely new Pythagorean theorem, quietly hiding inside every projection.

Key Fact: Work Is a Dot Product

When a constant force \(\mathbf{F}\) acts on an object as it moves through a displacement \(\mathbf{d}\), the work done is \(W=\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{d}=|\mathbf{F}||\mathbf{d}|\cos\theta\), where \(\theta\) is the angle between the force and the displacement. Decompose \(\mathbf{F}\) into \(\mathbf{F}_{\parallel}\) (along \(\mathbf{d}\)) and \(\mathbf{F}_{\perp}\) (perpendicular to \(\mathbf{d}\)): only \(\mathbf{F}_{\parallel}\) ever does work, because \(\mathbf{F}_{\perp}\cdot\mathbf{d}=0\) exactly — a perpendicular push moves an object sideways to its own effort, never along it. A force applied entirely at a right angle to the motion, however large, does zero work.

Example 3 — Work from Magnitude and Angle

A worker pulls a loaded wagon by a rope held at \(30^\circ\) above the horizontal, applying a steady force of \(50\) lb, and pulls the wagon \(100\) ft along level ground. Find the work done.

Solution:

\(W=|\mathbf{F}||\mathbf{d}|\cos\theta = 50(100)\cos30^\circ = 5000\left(\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)=2500\sqrt3\)

Answer: \(W=2500\sqrt3\approx4330.1\) ft-lb. In component form, the same force is \(\mathbf{F}=\langle50\cos30^\circ,\,50\sin30^\circ\rangle=\langle25\sqrt3,\,25\rangle\), and the displacement is \(\mathbf{d}=\langle100,0\rangle\), giving \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{d}=25\sqrt3(100)+25(0)=2500\sqrt3\) — the identical answer, confirming that the vertical component of the pull, however real it feels to the worker's arm, contributes nothing while the wagon rolls along the ground.

Example 4 — Work from Components

A force \(\mathbf{F}=\langle30,40\rangle\) lb acts on a crate as it slides through a displacement \(\mathbf{d}=\langle50,0\rangle\) ft. Find the work done, and explain the role of each component of \(\mathbf{F}\).

Solution:

\(W=\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{d}=30(50)+40(0)=1500+0=1500\)

Answer: \(W=1500\) ft-lb. The horizontal component of the force, \(30\) lb, does all the work because it points exactly along the crate's horizontal path. The vertical component, \(40\) lb, is real — it may well be lifting part of the crate's weight off the floor — but because the crate's displacement has no vertical part, that \(40\) lb component is multiplied by zero and contributes nothing to the work total.

💻 On Your Calculator: Checking a Projection Numerically

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), you can check Example 2 directly. Type u=(1,3) and v=(3,4), then type p=(dot(u,v)/dot(v,v))v — Desmos will compute the projection vector for you. Plot u, v, and p together, and confirm by eye that \(p\) lands exactly on the line through \(v\), with \(u-p\) meeting that line at a right angle.

▶  Try it yourself: Find \(\text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}\) for \(\mathbf{u}=\langle6,-2\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{v}=\langle0,5\rangle\).
\(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=6(0)+(-2)(5)=-10\). \(|\mathbf{v}|^2=25\). \(\text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}=\dfrac{-10}{25}\langle0,5\rangle=\langle0,-2\rangle\) — since \(\mathbf{v}\) points straight up, this simply recovers \(\mathbf{u}\)'s vertical component, \(-2\), matching the axis check from Example 1.
▶  Try it yourself: A force of \(80\) lb pulls at \(45^\circ\) above the horizontal across a displacement of \(20\) ft. Find the work done.
\(W=80(20)\cos45^\circ=1600\left(\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)=800\sqrt2\approx1131.4\) ft-lb.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Writing the projection formula with \(|\mathbf{v}|\) in the denominator instead of \(|\mathbf{v}|^2\). \(\dfrac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{v}|}\) is the scalar projection — a single number, the length of \(\mathbf{u}\)'s shadow. To get the vector projection, that number still has to be multiplied by \(\mathbf{v}\)'s unit vector, \(\mathbf{v}/|\mathbf{v}|\), which is where the second factor of \(|\mathbf{v}|\) comes from. Confusing the two means either reporting a vector where a number was asked for, or a number scaled wrong by a whole factor of \(|\mathbf{v}|\).
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The word “work,” as a precise technical term in mechanics, is younger than you might expect. It was the French engineer Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis — better known today for the Coriolis effect in rotating systems — who gave the concept its modern definition in 1829, in a treatise on the efficiency of machines. Before Coriolis pinned it down, engineers had intuitions about “force times distance” but no single, universally agreed formula. \(W=\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{d}\) is the same idea Coriolis was chasing, expressed in language he never had available to him.

Conclusion

Every vector, it turns out, is quietly two vectors at once relative to any direction you choose: a piece that lies along that direction and a piece that is entirely indifferent to it. Projection makes that split exact, and work is simply that split applied to force and motion — only the parallel piece ever does anything. Tomorrow, this same idea of forces cooperating along and against each other stops being an abstract formula and becomes the whole point: a sign hangs from two cables, and neither cable succeeds alone.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does dividing the dot-product-angle formula by \(|\mathbf{v}|\) once give a number, but dividing by \(|\mathbf{v}|\) twice give a vector?

2. In Example 2, why must \(\mathbf{u}_{\perp}\cdot\mathbf{v}\) always come out to exactly zero, no matter which \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) you start with?

3. Why does a force applied exactly perpendicular to an object's motion do zero work, even if the force itself is very large?

4. Simon Stevin found the truth about forces on a slope without any of today's dot-product notation. What does having the formula \(\text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}\) give you that Stevin's wreath of spheres could not?
