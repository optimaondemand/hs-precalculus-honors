Vectors in Service: Applied Capstone

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to model a genuine multi-force equilibrium scenario using vector components, and set up the two equations that equilibrium requires.

  • I will be able to solve a system of equilibrium equations for unknown tensions or forces, and verify the solution numerically.

  • I will be able to build a recalculating Excel model of a force-equilibrium scenario, and interpret how the answer changes as the angles change.

  • I will be able to compose a balanced force system in a VR Session and verify, on a whiteboard, that my arrangement satisfies both equilibrium equations.

📚 VOCABULARY

Equilibrium: the condition of a system of forces whose vector sum equals the zero vector, \(\sum\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{0}\), so the object they act on experiences no net acceleration.

Tension: the pulling force transmitted through a cable, rope, or cord, always directed along the cable, away from the object it supports.

Resultant: the single vector sum of two or more vectors, representing their combined effect.

Statics: the branch of mechanics that studies bodies at rest, or moving at constant velocity — that is, bodies in equilibrium.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Every lesson in this unit has treated vectors as objects that add, subtract, and multiply against each other. Today they cooperate. A sign hanging from two cables is held up by neither cable alone — it is held up by the two of them together, each contributing exactly the share the other cannot. That is the mathematics of this lesson, and it is also, quite literally, the unit's virtue: magnitudes and directions cooperating toward a common resultant, the way any genuine service asks two or more parties to carry a load no one of them could carry alone.

Setting Up an Equilibrium Problem

Picture a sign of weight \(W\) hanging from a single point where two cables meet the ceiling: a left cable making angle \(\theta_1\) with the horizontal, and a right cable making angle \(\theta_2\) with the horizontal. Each cable pulls with tension \(T_1\) or \(T_2\) directed back up along its own length. For the knot where all three forces meet to sit in equilibrium, the horizontal components must cancel and the vertical components must support the full weight:

\(\sum F_x:\ -T_1\cos\theta_1+T_2\cos\theta_2=0 \qquad\qquad \sum F_y:\ T_1\sin\theta_1+T_2\sin\theta_2-W=0\)

Two equations, two unknowns — exactly the kind of system Unit 3 already trained you to solve.

Example 1 — Solving the General System

Solve the equilibrium system above for \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) in terms of \(W\), \(\theta_1\), and \(\theta_2\).

Solution:

From \(\sum F_x\): \(T_2=\dfrac{T_1\cos\theta_1}{\cos\theta_2}\). Substitute into \(\sum F_y\):

\(T_1\sin\theta_1+\dfrac{T_1\cos\theta_1}{\cos\theta_2}\sin\theta_2=W \ \ \Rightarrow\ \ T_1\cdot\dfrac{\sin\theta_1\cos\theta_2+\cos\theta_1\sin\theta_2}{\cos\theta_2}=W\)

The numerator is exactly the sum formula for sine from Unit 7: \(\sin\theta_1\cos\theta_2+\cos\theta_1\sin\theta_2=\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\). So:

\(T_1\cdot\dfrac{\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)}{\cos\theta_2}=W \ \ \Rightarrow\ \ T_1=\dfrac{W\cos\theta_2}{\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)}\)

Answer: \(T_1=\dfrac{W\cos\theta_2}{\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)}\), and back-substituting, \(T_2=\dfrac{W\cos\theta_1}{\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)}\). Neither formula was invented for this problem alone — the sum-of-angles identity you proved algebraically in Unit 7 is doing real structural work here, collapsing two equations into one clean pair of formulas.

Example 2 — A Sign in Balance

A \(100\)-lb sign hangs from two cables, the left cable at \(\theta_1=60^\circ\) and the right cable at \(\theta_2=30^\circ\) from the horizontal. Find each cable's tension, and verify the equilibrium numerically.

Solution:

\(\theta_1+\theta_2=90^\circ\), so \(\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)=1\).

\(T_1=\dfrac{100\cos30^\circ}{1}=100\left(\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)=50\sqrt3\approx86.60\text{ lb} \qquad T_2=\dfrac{100\cos60^\circ}{1}=100(0.5)=50\text{ lb}\)

Verify: \(T_1\cos60^\circ=86.60(0.5)=43.30\) and \(T_2\cos30^\circ=50(0.8660)=43.30\) — the horizontal components match and cancel. ✓ \(T_1\sin60^\circ+T_2\sin30^\circ=86.60(0.8660)+50(0.5)=75.00+25.00=100.00=W\). ✓

Answer: \(T_1\approx86.60\) lb, \(T_2=50\) lb. The steeper cable (\(60^\circ\)) carries more of the load, as intuition demands — but the formula makes that intuition exact rather than a guess.

📊 On Your Spreadsheet: A Recalculating Equilibrium Model

Because Example 1 solved the system symbolically, you can build a single Excel worksheet that never needs re-deriving: input cells for \(W\), \(\theta_1\), and \(\theta_2\) (in degrees, then converted with RADIANS()), and formula cells for \(T_1=\dfrac{W\cos\theta_2}{\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)}\) and \(T_2=\dfrac{W\cos\theta_1}{\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)}\), each written directly from the derivation above using =COS() and =SIN(). Change any input and both tensions recalculate instantly — exactly the situation a rigger or engineer actually faces when a sign's mounting angles are still being decided.

Try it: with \(W=200\) lb, \(\theta_1=70^\circ\), and \(\theta_2=20^\circ\), the same formulas give \(T_1\approx187.94\) lb and \(T_2\approx68.40\) lb — check that your spreadsheet reproduces both numbers before moving on.

🦥 Your VR Session: Forces in Three Dimensions 25 min

What you're doing. Example 2 balanced a sign algebraically, on paper. This Session lets you balance one with your own hands, at room scale, and feel what “equilibrium” actually means before you check it numerically. You will arrange force vectors on a suspended object, compose them tip-to-tail, and adjust them until the system genuinely holds still — then prove on a whiteboard that your arrangement satisfies the same two equations Example 2 solved by algebra.

What you'll need. Your VR headset (preferred) or the ENGAGE desktop app; complete Examples 1 and 2 above first, so the two equilibrium equations already mean something concrete before you build them in space.

How to join.

  • Session type: solo, persistent — your arrangement and whiteboard work remain if you return.
  • Session ID: posted beside this lesson in the Canvas module.
  • Location: The Rigging Loft.
  • On arrival: you spawn facing a sign suspended from two anchor points in the rafters. A Whiteboard IFX at your left holds today's instructions, and a supply of Force Arrow IFX waits on a nearby shelf.

What to do, in order.

  1. Read the Whiteboard's overview, then place a Force Arrow IFX along each cable, pointing away from the sign along the cable's actual angle, plus a third Force Arrow IFX pointing straight down from the sign to represent its weight.
  2. Set each cable's Force Arrow IFX to an initial guess for its length (representing tension), and compose the two cable arrows tip-to-tail to build their resultant.
  3. Compare that tip-to-tail resultant, by eye, to the downward weight arrow it must exactly cancel for the system to be in equilibrium.
  4. Adjust each cable arrow's length (without changing its angle) until the tip-to-tail resultant of the two tensions exactly cancels the weight arrow — the system balances, and the sign hangs still.
  5. On the Whiteboard IFX, write out \(\sum F_x=0\) and \(\sum F_y=0\) using your final arrow lengths as \(T_1\) and \(T_2\), and confirm your numbers actually satisfy both equations.
  6. Start a spatial recording and narrate, in thirty to sixty seconds, how you adjusted each component and why your balanced arrangement satisfies both equilibrium equations — then stop the recording.

What to submit. The shareable link to your narrated spatial recording, together with a written paragraph (typed or handwritten on the same page) restating your final \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) and the equilibrium check, included as a page in your Assignment 10-14 PDF. About 25 minutes in-headset.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Assuming the more steeply angled cable must always carry the smaller tension, since it looks like it is doing “less horizontal work.” The opposite is true: in Example 2, the steeper cable (\(60^\circ\)) carries \(86.60\) lb, more than the shallower cable's \(50\) lb. A nearly vertical cable is doing almost all of the lifting; a nearly horizontal one contributes very little vertical support and must be balanced by a large horizontal pull from the other side. Trust the algebra over the first-glance intuition, then let the algebra teach the intuition to catch up.

Conclusion

Two cables, one sign, and one system of equations is a small scenario — but it is the same mathematics that keeps suspension bridges standing, holds up cable-stayed roofs, and lets a crane operator trust a rigging plan before the load ever leaves the ground. Every tool this unit has built — components, magnitude and direction, resultants, the dot product's angle formula, and now equilibrium — was needed to get here. Tomorrow's lesson steps back from any one application and asks the largest question this unit can ask: what is a vector, really, and how does it relate to the other languages this course has spent two semesters building for describing the plane?
