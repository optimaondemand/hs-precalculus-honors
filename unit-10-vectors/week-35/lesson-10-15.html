One Geometry: Vectors, Complex Numbers, and Polar (Unit Synthesis)
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to translate a single plane quantity among its vector (component and trigonometric) form, its complex-number (rectangular and trigonometric) form, and its polar (magnitude-angle) description.

  • I will be able to articulate what each of the three languages — vectors, complex numbers, and polar form — is best suited to express, and choose the right one for a given task.

  • I will be able to explain how the Gibbs–Heaviside vector algebra this unit has taught is a deliberate simplification of Hamilton's quaternions, built to keep exactly the parts of quaternion algebra that physics and engineering actually needed.

📚 VOCABULARY

Trigonometric (magnitude-angle) form of a vector: writing \(\mathbf{v}=|\mathbf{v}|(\cos\theta\,\mathbf{i}+\sin\theta\,\mathbf{j})\), where \(|\mathbf{v}|\) is the vector's magnitude and \(\theta\) its direction angle — the exact vector counterpart of a complex number's trigonometric form.

Quaternion: a four-part number, one real part plus three imaginary units \(i,j,k\), invented by William Rowan Hamilton to extend complex-number rotation from the plane into three-dimensional space.

Vector algebra: the system of vector addition, scalar multiplication, the dot product, and (in three dimensions) the cross product, distilled by Josiah Willard Gibbs and Oliver Heaviside from the vector-like part of Hamilton's quaternions.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

You already know how William Rowan Hamilton's long search for a way to multiply points in three-dimensional space ended: on the morning of October 16, 1843, walking with his wife along Dublin's Royal Canal, the answer arrived to him all at once, and he could not resist carving it into the stone of Broom Bridge as he crossed it — \(i^2=j^2=k^2=ijk=-1\). That flash of insight gave the world quaternions, a four-part number system that could rotate a point through space the way multiplying by \(i\) rotates a point through the plane. What that earlier telling did not finish is what happened to quaternions next — and that ending is where this course's vector algebra actually comes from.

Quaternions worked, but they were heavier than physics and engineering wanted to carry. Multiplying two quaternions tangled together a scalar part and a vector part in one motion, and most working scientists found themselves reaching past the scalar part every time, using only the three imaginary units to track direction and magnitude in space. In the 1880s, the American physicist Josiah Willard Gibbs and the English engineer Oliver Heaviside, working independently, each made the same decision: keep the vector part, its addition, and two new products — the dot product and the cross product — and set the rest of Hamilton's four-part algebra aside. Quaternion loyalists, including Hamilton's own successor Peter Guthrie Tait, argued hard against the simplification. The simplification won anyway, because it did everything physics and engineering actually needed, with less to carry. Every dot product, every component, every equilibrium equation this unit has used is Gibbs and Heaviside's distillation, still doing exactly the job they built it for.

One Plane Quantity, Three Languages

Consider the vector \(\mathbf{v}=3\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j}\).

Language This quantity Best for…
Vector (component) \(3\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j}=\langle3,4\rangle\) Adding forces, velocities, and displacements — physical composition.
Complex number \(3+4i\) Rotating and scaling — multiplication does the rotating for you.
Polar / trigonometric \(5\left(\cos53.13^\circ+i\sin53.13^\circ\right)\) Reading off magnitude and direction directly — bearings, headings, distances.

Example 1 — One Vector, Two Trigonometric Forms

Write \(\mathbf{v}=3\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j}\) in vector trigonometric form, and write the corresponding complex number \(3+4i\) in complex trigonometric form. Compare.

Solution:

\(|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=\sqrt{25}=5\). Reference angle: \(\tan\theta=\dfrac{4}{3}\), and since both components are positive (Quadrant I), \(\theta=\tan^{-1}\left(\dfrac{4}{3}\right)\approx53.13^\circ\).

Vector trigonometric form: \(\mathbf{v}=5(\cos53.13^\circ\,\mathbf{i}+\sin53.13^\circ\,\mathbf{j})\)

Complex trigonometric form: \(3+4i=5(\cos53.13^\circ+i\sin53.13^\circ)\)

Answer: The magnitude (\(5\)) and the direction angle (\(53.13^\circ\)) are identical in both forms — the only difference is whether you write \(\mathbf{i}\) and \(\mathbf{j}\), or the real axis and \(i\). That is not a coincidence; it is the same computation, \(r\cos\theta\) and \(r\sin\theta\), computing the same two numbers either way.

Example 2 — Starting from Magnitude and Angle

A quantity has magnitude \(10\) at direction angle \(210^\circ\). Write it as a vector in component form and as a complex number in rectangular form.

Solution:

\(x=10\cos210^\circ=10\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)=-5\sqrt3 \qquad y=10\sin210^\circ=10\left(-\dfrac12\right)=-5\)

Answer: As a vector, \(\langle-5\sqrt3,-5\rangle\); as a complex number, \(-5\sqrt3-5i\). One pair of numbers, \(x=r\cos\theta\) and \(y=r\sin\theta\), feeds both descriptions at once — there was never a second computation to perform.

Example 3 — The Rotation You Already Proved

Back in Lesson 9-1, you verified that multiplying the complex number \(3+4i\) by \(i\) gives \(-4+3i\), and that this multiplication rotates the point \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise without changing its distance from the origin. Reinterpret that exact computation as a statement about the vector \(\mathbf{v}=\langle3,4\rangle\).

Solution:

\((3+4i)\cdot i=3i+4i^2=-4+3i\)

As vectors, this says: rotating \(\langle3,4\rangle\) by \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise produces \(\langle-4,3\rangle\). Check the magnitude is preserved: \(|\langle3,4\rangle|=5\) and \(|\langle-4,3\rangle|=\sqrt{16+9}=\sqrt{25}=5\). ✓

Answer: \(\langle-4,3\rangle\). Complex-number multiplication by \(i\) and rotating a vector \(90^\circ\) are not two related operations — they are the same operation, computed the same way, wearing two different notations. If you ever need to rotate a vector, writing it as a complex number and multiplying is often the fastest route there is.

Key Fact: Choosing Your Language

When two forces or velocities must be combined, reach for vector component form — addition is immediate, and it is the only one of the three languages built for physical composition (Lessons 10-2, 10-3, 10-9, 10-10). When a quantity must be rotated or scaled, reach for complex trigonometric form — multiplication does the rotating automatically (Unit 9; Example 3 above). When you only need to state or read off a magnitude and a direction — a compass bearing, a wind heading, a distance and angle — polar form already says exactly that, with nothing left to compute. No single form wins every job; a mathematician who has learned all three chooses whichever makes the problem in front of them simple.

💻 On Your Calculator: Watching the Three Languages Agree

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), plot \((3,4)\) as an ordered pair, and separately plot the point \((5\cos(53.13^\circ),5\sin(53.13^\circ))\). They land on top of each other — confirming that the vector, the complex number, and the polar description in Example 1 are, quite literally, the same point on the same plane.

▶  Try it yourself: Write \(\mathbf{w}=\langle-6,6\rangle\) in vector trigonometric form, and its complex counterpart \(-6+6i\) in complex trigonometric form.
\(|\mathbf{w}|=\sqrt{36+36}=6\sqrt2\). Reference angle \(45^\circ\); Quadrant II (\(x<0,y>0\)) gives \(\theta=135^\circ\). Both forms: magnitude \(6\sqrt2\), angle \(135^\circ\) — \(\mathbf{w}=6\sqrt2(\cos135^\circ\,\mathbf{i}+\sin135^\circ\,\mathbf{j})\) and \(-6+6i=6\sqrt2(\cos135^\circ+i\sin135^\circ)\).

Conclusion

Ten units ago, this course began with a function's rule for turning one number into another. It ends here, with the discovery that a single point in a plane can be addressed three honest ways — as a vector to be composed, as a complex number to be rotated, as a magnitude and an angle to be read directly — and that none of the three is the “real” one underneath the others. They are the same geometry, in three accents. Thursday's test asks you to prove, on paper, that you can hear all three. After that, one week remains before the year's work is gathered into the semester final: not new mathematics, but the standing invitation to notice, one more time, how much of what looked like separate subjects turned out to be a single plane, examined from every angle this course could offer.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why must a vector's magnitude and direction angle, and its corresponding complex number's modulus and argument, always come out identical for the same underlying pair of components?

2. What exactly did Gibbs and Heaviside remove from Hamilton's quaternions, and why did physics and engineering keep the simplified version rather than the original four-part algebra?

3. In Example 3, why is “multiply by \(i\)” and “rotate \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise” the same instruction rather than merely two operations that happen to agree?

4. How does having three cooperating languages for one geometric idea reflect this unit's virtue of service — magnitudes and directions, and now representations, cooperating toward a common result?
