📋 THE SCENARIO

Long before satellites could tell a pilot exactly where an airplane sat over the earth, dead reckoning was the only navigation there was: know your airspeed and the heading you intend to steer, know the wind aloft, and add the two as vectors to find out where you are actually going. Every piece of mathematics this unit has handed you — the parallelogram law Simon Stevin worked out for forces in the 1580s, the component method J. Willard Gibbs and Oliver Heaviside distilled out of Hamilton's quaternions two centuries later — was built, in part, to make this exact problem solvable without guesswork. Lesson 10-9 gave you the technique itself: an aircraft's own-velocity vector (its airspeed and the heading it is steered) plus the wind's velocity vector equals its ground-velocity vector — the speed and direction the aircraft actually travels over the earth. This unit's virtue, service, is not an afterthought bolted onto that mathematics; it is the whole reason the mathematics matters. A flight plan that gets the vector addition right is passengers who land where the pilot said they would, fuel reserves that were calculated honestly instead of hoped for, and a heading that tells the truth about where the wind is actually carrying the airplane.

Your task: plan a two-leg flight against wind forecasts you choose, build the vector-addition arithmetic as a live Excel worksheet — one that recomputes your ground speed and ground track the instant a wind number changes — and then write the paragraph a pilot would actually read before takeoff: the briefing.

🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
If the wind forecast for one of your legs changed overnight — a tailwind becoming a headwind, or a crosswind reversing — what in your worksheet would have to change, what would stay exactly the same, and how would your spreadsheet prove it got the new answer right without you recomputing anything by hand?
📝 THE TASK

Part 1 — Get the flight on record. On a sheet named Data/Plan, invent a two-leg flight: two legs with genuinely different distances and different headings (real airports and a real route you look up are welcome, but an invented route is just as acceptable). For each leg, record in labeled cells: the distance (nautical miles), the aircraft's true airspeed — TAS — in knots, the heading the pilot intends to steer (in degrees, aviation convention: measured clockwise from north, 000°–360°), and a wind forecast — a wind speed in knots and a wind direction. Label your wind direction as the compass heading the wind is blowing toward (for example, “wind toward 200° at 20 kt” means the air is moving from the northeast toward the southwest) — this keeps your wind vector built exactly the same way as your airspeed vector. Real aviation forecasts (METARs, TAFs, winds-aloft charts) report the direction the wind blows from; if you pull a real forecast, add 180° (mod 360°) to its reported direction before you enter it, and label that conversion in a cell so a reader can check your honesty at a glance. Two different, clearly labeled forecasts — real or self-invented — one per leg.

Part 2 — Build the vector-addition model, live. On a sheet named Model, build the worksheet that turns each leg's airspeed vector and wind vector into a ground-velocity vector — entirely with formulas that recalculate the instant a wind cell changes. No hard-coded intermediate values anywhere: if you change a wind speed or wind direction on the Data/Plan sheet, every downstream cell on the Model sheet must update itself.

The heading conversion, spelled out (a favorite place for sign errors). Aviation headings are measured clockwise from north; the angle convention you learned in trigonometry class is measured counterclockwise from due east. The two are related by \( \theta_{\text{math}} = 90^\circ - H_{\text{heading}} \) (add 360° if the result is negative). You almost never need to make that conversion by hand, because it is already built into the pair of identities you should type straight into Excel:

  • East component = speed × sin(heading)
  • North component = speed × cos(heading)

Notice that sine pairs with East and cosine pairs with North here — the opposite of the standard math-class pairing (cosine with the horizontal axis, sine with the vertical). That swap is exactly the \(90^\circ - H\) relationship above, worked through the co-function identities \(\sin(90^\circ-H)=\cos H\) and \(\cos(90^\circ-H)=\sin H\). Mixing up which trig function goes with which axis is the single most common way a wind-triangle problem goes wrong — say the swap out loud to a partner before you build a single formula, the way you practiced it in Lesson 10-9.

For each leg, with TAS in column B, heading in column C, wind speed in column F, and wind direction (toward) in column G, build (row 2 shown; copy down for row 3):

  • Airspeed East, D2: =B2*SIN(RADIANS(C2))  ·  Airspeed North, E2: =B2*COS(RADIANS(C2))
  • Wind East, H2: =F2*SIN(RADIANS(G2))  ·  Wind North, I2: =F2*COS(RADIANS(G2))
  • Ground East (sum), J2: =D2+H2  ·  Ground North (sum), K2: =E2+I2
  • Ground speed (the magnitude of the ground-velocity vector), L2: =SQRT(J2^2+K2^2)
  • Ground track (the direction the aircraft actually travels, 0°–360°), M2: =MOD(DEGREES(ATAN2(K2,J2)),360) — Excel's ATAN2(x,y) wants the North component first and the East component second to hand back a heading in this clockwise-from-north convention, which is the reverse order from how you likely built ATAN2 formulas in earlier units.

Worked illustration (not your data — build your own two legs): TAS 150 kt, heading 065°, wind toward 200° at 20 kt, over a 220 nmi leg, resolves to a ground speed of 136.6 kt on a ground track of 070.9° — an estimated time en route of about 1 hr 37 min. Notice the wind has bent the airplane's actual path a few degrees north of the 065° it is pointed, and slowed it down: a partial headwind. Confirm your own worksheet behaves the same way — a tailwind component should raise your ground speed above your TAS, a headwind component should lower it, and a crosswind component should bend your ground track away from your heading without changing your ground speed by much.

Part 3 — Write it up like a pilot. On a sheet named Answers, state each leg's ground speed and ground track in full sentences that cite your own Model-sheet cells by reference (for example: “On Leg 1, cell L2 shows a ground speed of 136.6 kt, and cell M2 shows a ground track of 070.9°, given a heading of 065° and a 20 kt wind toward 200°”). Then write the pilot's briefing paragraph — four to six sentences, in plain, professional language an actual pilot could read before departure: each leg's heading, ground speed, ground track, and estimated time en route, plus one sentence naming what the wind is doing to the flight (helping, hurting, or pushing it off course) on each leg.

Part 4 — Put your AI tutor to the test. Before you touch a single cell, describe one leg's numbers to your AI tutor and ask it to predict, without computing anything, how that leg's ground speed and ground track would change if the wind shifted — for instance, if a tailwind became a headwind, or a crosswind reversed direction. Record the AI's prediction on the Answers sheet, in its own words. Then actually change that leg's wind speed and/or wind direction cells on the Data/Plan sheet to the shifted values, let the Model sheet recalculate itself, and record the new ground speed and ground track it produces. Grade the AI's prediction against what the worksheet actually did — did it get the direction of the change right? The rough size of the change? Did it hedge, hallucinate a number it had no way of knowing, or reason soundly from the geometry? Write your verdict, defended with the actual before-and-after numbers, and sign it. Once you have recorded the comparison, revert the wind cells to your original forecast so the workbook you submit reflects your real flight plan.

Honors stretch (optional): So far your worksheet has solved the forward problem — given a heading, find where the wind actually takes you. Pilots more often need the problem run in reverse: given a wind, what heading do I steer to hold an exact desired ground track? This is the wind correction angle (WCA) problem, and it is solvable with the Law of Sines applied to the wind triangle: if \(\theta\) is the angle between the wind's from-direction and your desired ground track, then \(\sin(\text{WCA}) = \dfrac{V_w}{\text{TAS}}\sin\theta\). For example, to hold a ground track of due east (090°) in a 150 kt airplane against a 30 kt wind from 070°: here \(\theta = 090^\circ-070^\circ=20^\circ\), so \(\sin(\text{WCA}) = \frac{30}{150}\sin 20^\circ \approx 0.0684\), giving \(\text{WCA}\approx 3.9^\circ\). Because the wind is coming from the north side of the course, you correct into it, steering a heading of \(090^\circ - 3.9^\circ \approx 086^\circ\) — a few degrees north of the track you actually want, so the wind pushes you back onto it. Checking that heading the forward way (the same component method as Part 2) confirms it: TAS 150 kt at 086° plus a 30 kt wind toward 250° (070° + 180°) sums to a ground track of exactly 090.0°, at a reduced ground speed of about 121.5 kt — the price this wind exacts for holding an exact course. Add a small block to your Model sheet that solves this reverse problem for one of your own legs: pick a desired ground track, and use the Law-of-Sines relation above (built as a live formula, not typed as a number) to find the heading you would actually have to steer, then verify it forward.
