Exponential Models: Growth, Decay, and Interest
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to build and use doubling-time models \(A(t) = A_0 \cdot 2^{t/d}\) and half-life models \(A(t) = A_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t/h}\).

  • I will be able to apply the compound interest formula to compare frequencies and account offers.

  • I will be able to view one growth story through both lenses — geometric sequence and exponential function — and translate between them.

📚 VOCABULARY

Doubling time: The fixed span \(d\) in which a growing quantity multiplies by 2 — the same span from any starting moment, a property only exponentials have.

Half-life: The fixed span \(h\) in which a decaying quantity falls to half — a coinage of early atomic physics (originally “half-life period”): not half of a lifetime, but the lifetime of each successive half.

Exponential decay: An exponential model with base below 1 — a constant fraction lost per unit time, so the loss is steep at first and gentle forever after.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Every living thing carries a clock it did not wind. Cosmic rays striking the upper atmosphere continually create carbon-14, a radioactive cousin of ordinary carbon, and every plant and animal absorbs it through food and air for as long as it lives. At death the intake stops, and the carbon-14 begins its patient exit: half of it gone in 5,730 years, half of what remains in the next 5,730, and so on — a decay so regular that in the 1940s the chemist Willard Libby realized it could be read backward. Measure the fraction of carbon-14 remaining in a parchment, a bone, a charred hearth, and you can tell when its clock stopped. Radiocarbon dating won Libby the Nobel Prize and rewrote archaeology — and its entire mechanism is a function you learned to write on Monday: an exponential with base \(\frac{1}{2}\).

Today is the toolkit lesson. This week built the exponential function (3-1), drew it (3-2), and found its natural base (3-3); now we shape it to the three stories it tells most often — doubling, halving, and interest. The craft is in the exponent. If a quantity doubles every \(d\) units of time, then after time \(t\) it has doubled \(\frac{t}{d}\) times, so:

The Two Chassis: Doubling and Halving

  • Doubling time \(d\): \(A(t) = A_0 \cdot 2^{t/d}\) — the exponent \(\frac{t}{d}\) counts how many doublings have elapsed.
  • Half-life \(h\): \(A(t) = A_0 \cdot \left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{t/h}\) — the exponent counts how many halvings have elapsed.
  • Sanity check, always: at \(t = d\) the first gives \(A_0 \cdot 2^1 = 2A_0\) ✓; at \(t = h\) the second gives \(\frac{1}{2}A_0\) ✓. One substitution proves your model tells the story.
  • Units must match: \(t\) and \(d\) (or \(h\)) in the same unit — both hours, both years — or the exponent counts nonsense.

These are ordinary exponentials \(ab^t\) in tailored clothing: base \(2^{1/d}\) or \(\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{1/h}\) per unit time. The tailoring just puts the story's natural rhythm — the doubling, the halving — where you can see it.

Example 1

Carbon-14 has a half-life of 5,730 years. What fraction of a sample's original carbon-14 remains 1,000 years after death — and what does that mean for dating?

Solution:

With \(A_0 = 1\) (a whole sample) and \(h = 5730\):

\(A(1000) = \left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{1000/5730} = \left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{0.1745\ldots} \approx 0.886\)

The exponent is the number of half-lives elapsed — about 0.17 of one — so the sample has taken only a fraction of its first halving step.

Answer: About 88.6% remains. Run it backward and you have Libby's whole science: an archaeologist who measures 88.6% of the expected carbon-14 concludes the organism died roughly 1,000 years ago. Notice the model needed a fractional exponent — 0.1745 half-lives — and Monday's smooth curve, defined at every real input, is precisely what makes “partway through a halving” meaningful.

Example 2

A bacteria culture starts at 200 cells and doubles every 3 hours. Write the model; find the population after 12 hours, and after 4.5 hours.

Solution:

Chassis: \(A(t) = 200 \cdot 2^{t/3}\), with \(t\) in hours. Sanity check: \(A(3) = 200 \cdot 2 = 400\) ✓ — one doubling after one doubling time.

\(A(12) = 200 \cdot 2^{12/3} = 200 \cdot 2^4 = 200 \cdot 16 = 3200\)

\(A(4.5) = 200 \cdot 2^{4.5/3} = 200 \cdot 2^{1.5} = 200 \cdot 2\sqrt{2} \approx 566\)

Answer: After 12 hours, 3,200 cells — four clean doublings. After 4.5 hours, about 566 cells: one-and-a-half doublings, where \(2^{1.5} = 2\sqrt{2}\) puts an exact value under the decimal. The model runs between the doublings as smoothly as at them.

Example 3

You deposit \(\$3000\) at 6% for 8 years. Compare annual and monthly compounding — how much does the finer frequency earn?

Solution:

Both rungs use \(A = P\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}\):

Annual: \(3000(1.06)^{8} \approx 3000(1.593848) \approx \$4781.54\)

Monthly: \(3000\left(1 + \tfrac{0.06}{12}\right)^{96} = 3000(1.005)^{96} \approx \$4842.43\)

Answer: Monthly compounding yields about $60.89 more — real money, though yesterday's ceiling is already in sight: even continuous compounding could only add a few dollars beyond monthly here. When you compare account offers, frequency matters — but a higher rate matters far more than a finer frequency. Compute both before believing either.

Example 4

\(\$1000\) is deposited at 6% compounded monthly. Tell the story twice: once in Unit 1's sequence language, once in this week's function language — and find the balance after 24 months both ways.

Solution:

Sequence lens (Unit 1): each month multiplies the balance by \(1 + \frac{0.06}{12} = 1.005\). That is a recursion, \(b_n = 1.005 \, b_{n-1}\) with \(b_0 = 1000\) — a geometric sequence with ratio 1.005 — whose explicit form is \(b_n = 1000(1.005)^n\).

Function lens (this week): \(f(x) = 1000(1.005)^x\), the smooth exponential with initial value 1000 and base 1.005, defined at every real \(x\).

The formulas are identical; only the domains differ. The sequence is the function sampled at the integers — the statement months, dotted along the curve. At \(n = 24\):

\(b_{24} = 1000(1.005)^{24} \approx \$1127.16\)

Answer: $1,127.16, by either lens — necessarily, since the lenses share one formula. This is Unit 1's promise cashed out: the common ratio became a base, the term index became a variable, and everything you proved about geometric sequences — ratio tests, explicit forms — carried into the theory of exponential functions intact. Two languages; one mathematics.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Units that do not match, and numbers in the wrong seats. If the doubling time is 3 hours, the exponent \(\frac{t}{3}\) requires \(t\) in hours — feed it \(t = 2\) days and the model reports two-thirds of a doubling instead of sixteen doublings. Likewise, “doubles every 3 hours” is a doubling time, not a rate: writing \(200(1.03)^t\) (as if 3 were a percent) or \(200 \cdot 2^{3t}\) (doubling time in the numerator) both produce confident nonsense. Before trusting any model, run the sanity check: substitute \(t = d\) (or \(t = h\)) and confirm the output is exactly \(2A_0\) (or \(\frac{1}{2}A_0\)). One line of arithmetic guards the whole solution — the same guard-then-judge habit you built on rational equations in Unit 2.

💻 On Your Calculator: Reading Doubling and Halving off the Graph 10 min

At desmos.com/calculator, make the models confess their rhythms:

  1. Graph Example 2's culture: y = 200 · 2^(x/3), and the horizon line y = 400. Click the intersection: \(x = 3\) — the doubling time, read straight off the graph. Move the line to \(y = 800\), then \(y = 1600\): the crossings land at \(x = 6\) and \(x = 9\). Equal vertical ratios, equal horizontal steps — that staircase is what “constant doubling time” looks like.
  2. Now the clock: graph y = (1/2)^(x/5730) with the window \(0 \le x \le 20000\), and y = 0.5. The intersection sits at \(x = 5730\), the half-life. Slide the line to \(y = 0.886\) and read the crossing near \(x = 1000\) — Example 1's date, measured graphically.
  3. Prefer Excel? Column A: years 0, 500, 1000, … (fill series); B2: =(1/2)^(A2/5730), fill down — a printed decay table an archaeologist could use in the field.
  4. Every readout in steps 1–2 is an intersection you clicked, not an equation you solved. The “when” questions still await their exact tool — one lesson more. The calculator confirms; your algebra convinces.
▶  Try it yourself: Caffeine leaves the body with a half-life of about 5 hours. You drink a 200 mg coffee at noon. Write the model and find how much caffeine remains at 10 p.m. Work it on paper before you peek.
\(A(t) = 200\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t/5}\), \(t\) in hours. At 10 p.m., \(t = 10\): \(A(10) = 200\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2} = 200 \cdot \frac{1}{4} = 50\) mg — two clean half-lives, a quarter of the dose still working at bedtime.
▶  Try it yourself: An investment doubles every 9 years. Starting from \(\$5000\), write the model and find the balance after 27 years — no calculator needed.
\(A(t) = 5000 \cdot 2^{t/9}\). At \(t = 27\): \(5000 \cdot 2^{27/9} = 5000 \cdot 2^3 = \$40{,}000\). Three doublings in 27 years — when the exponent comes out whole, the arithmetic is mental.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
In 1991, hikers in the Alps found a body melting out of a glacier — so well preserved that police initially treated it as a modern case. Radiocarbon dating told a different story: about 53% of the man's carbon-14 remained, placing his death around 5,300 years ago, before Egypt raised its pyramids. “Ötzi the Iceman” is now the most studied human body of the Copper Age — his tools, his tattoos, even his last meal reconstructed — and the single number that placed him in time came from the model in Example 1: \(\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t/5730}\), read backward.
