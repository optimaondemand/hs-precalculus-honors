🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to tell linear growth from exponential growth in a table or a description, using differences and ratios as evidence.

  • I will be able to evaluate exponential functions and interpret the results in context.

  • I will be able to construct \(f(x) = ab^x\) from an initial value and a rate, or from two points.

  • I will be able to classify an exponential function as growth or decay by reading its base.

📚 VOCABULARY

Exponential function: A function of the form \(f(x) = ab^x\) with \(a \neq 0\), \(b > 0\), \(b \neq 1\) — the variable lives in the exponent, from the Latin exponere, “to set forth,” the number set up above the line.

Base (growth factor): The number \(b\) — the fixed amount each one-unit step multiplies the output by.

Growth rate vs. growth factor: The rate \(r\) is the percent change per step; the factor is \(b = 1 + r\). A 5% rate is the factor 1.05 — two names that must never be confused.

Initial value: The number \(a = f(0)\) — the output before any growing or decaying has happened.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

An old legend says the inventor of chess presented the game to his king, who offered any reward. The inventor asked for something apparently humble: one grain of wheat on the first square of the board, two on the second, four on the third — doubling, square by square, to the sixty-fourth. The king laughed at the modesty of the request and ordered it filled. Then his treasurers began to count. The final square alone calls for \(2^{63} \approx 9.2 \times 10^{18}\) grains — more wheat than the kingdom, or the world, has ever held. Nothing about “doubling” sounds alarming; sixty-three doublings is a number beyond imagining. Today begins a new unit, and its through-line is exactly this kind of astonishment: wonder at how much shape a simple rule can hide.

You already know the quiet version of growth. A linear function adds the same amount each step: start at 100, add 50 per year, and the totals march 100, 150, 200, 250 — steady, predictable, tame. The chessboard runs on a different engine. An exponential function multiplies by the same amount each step: start at 100, multiply by 1.5 per year, and the totals go 100, 150, 225, 337.5 — gentle at first, then ungovernable. Addition versus multiplication: that single difference separates the two most important growth stories in mathematics.

The Exponential Function

An exponential function has the form

\(f(x) = ab^x, \qquad a \neq 0, \quad b > 0, \quad b \neq 1\)

  • \(a\) is the initial value: \(f(0) = ab^0 = a\).
  • \(b\) is the growth factor: each one-unit increase in \(x\) multiplies the output by \(b\), because \(f(x+1) = ab^{x+1} = b \cdot ab^x\).
  • \(b > 1\) means growth; \(0 < b < 1\) means decay. Multiplying by 1.05 enlarges; multiplying by 0.88 shrinks.

Why the restrictions on \(b\)? If \(b = 1\) the function is the constant \(a\) — nothing grows. If \(b\) were negative, the outputs would flip sign at every step and the smooth curve would shatter. Positive base, not 1: those are the conditions for a genuine growth-or-decay story.

Here is the fingerprint that lets you diagnose a table at a glance. Over equal steps in \(x\), a linear function shows a constant difference and an exponential function shows a constant ratio. Subtract consecutive outputs: all the same? Linear. Divide consecutive outputs: all the same? Exponential. The chessboard's ratio is 2; a savings account's might be 1.045; a fading medication's might be 0.75 — but in each case, divide neighbor by neighbor and the same number falls out every time.

If “constant ratio” sounds familiar, it should — you spent Unit 1 with it. A geometric sequence is an exponential function sampled at the integers. Same engine, two languages:

Two Languages, One Idea: Geometric Sequence ↔ Exponential Function

Sequence language (Unit 1):
\(a_n = a_1 r^{n-1}\)
defined at \(n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots\)		 Function language (today):
\(f(x) = ab^x\)
defined for every real \(x\)
common ratio \(r\) base \(b\) — the same number
Example: \(a_n = 6 \cdot 2^{n-1}\): terms 6, 12, 24, 48, … Same values: \(f(x) = 3 \cdot 2^x\), since \(6 \cdot 2^{n-1} = 3 \cdot 2^n\). Check \(f(1) = 6\), \(f(2) = 12\) ✓

The sequence is dots; the function is the smooth curve threaded through them — and now the in-between inputs (\(x = 2.5\) years, \(x = 0.1\) seconds) have meaning too. Unit 1's ratio has become a curve.

Example 1

Each table shows outputs at equally spaced inputs. Diagnose each as linear or exponential, and write its rule.

Table A: \(x = 0, 1, 2, 3\);   \(y = 12,\ 17,\ 22,\ 27\)    Table B: \(x = 0, 1, 2, 3\);   \(y = 12,\ 18,\ 27,\ 40.5\)

Solution:

Run both tests on both tables. Table A — differences: \(17 - 12 = 5\), \(22 - 17 = 5\), \(27 - 22 = 5\). Constant difference: linear, slope 5, intercept 12.

Table B — differences: \(6, 9, 13.5\) — not constant, so not linear. Ratios: \(\frac{18}{12} = 1.5\), \(\frac{27}{18} = 1.5\), \(\frac{40.5}{27} = 1.5\). Constant ratio: exponential, initial value 12, base 1.5.

Answer: Table A: \(y = 12 + 5x\). Table B: \(y = 12(1.5)^x\). One diagnosis by subtraction, one by division — and note that Table B's differences (6, 9, 13.5) are themselves growing by the ratio 1.5. Exponential growth accelerates; that is the chessboard's secret.

Example 2

An account is modeled by \(f(x) = 1200(1.045)^x\), where \(x\) is measured in years. Interpret the two constants, then find and interpret \(f(10)\).

Solution:

Read the anatomy first: \(a = 1200\) is the initial deposit, \(f(0) = \$1200\); the base \(1.045 = 1 + 0.045\) says the balance is multiplied by 1.045 each year — a 4.5% annual growth rate. For \(f(10)\):

\(f(10) = 1200(1.045)^{10} \approx 1200(1.552969) \approx 1863.56\)

Answer: After 10 years the account holds about $1,863.56. Notice what a decade of 4.5% did: the balance grew by more than half, though no single year grew it by more than a twentieth. Small factor, many applications — the multiplications compound.

Example 3

Find the exponential function through \((0, 3)\) and \((2, 12)\).

Solution:

Write \(f(x) = ab^x\) and feed it both points. The first is a gift: \(f(0) = a = 3\). The second gives

\(3b^2 = 12 \quad\Rightarrow\quad b^2 = 4 \quad\Rightarrow\quad b = 2\)

(We take the positive root — the base of an exponential must be positive.)

Answer: \(f(x) = 3 \cdot 2^x\). Verify against both points: \(f(0) = 3\) ✓ and \(f(2) = 3 \cdot 4 = 12\) ✓. Two points determine a line; two points (with positive outputs) also determine an exponential — but the second unknown is dug out by a root, not a slope formula, because the base sits inside a power.

Example 4

Translate between rates and factors: ① a quantity grows 5% per year — what is the factor? ② a car loses 12% of its value per year — what is the factor? ③ a population follows \(P(t) = 40000(0.88)^t\) — state the rate, and find \(P(3)\).

Solution:

① Growth adds the rate to 1: keeping 100% and gaining 5% means multiplying by \(1 + 0.05 = 1.05\).

② Decay subtracts the rate from 1: losing 12% means keeping 88%, so the factor is \(1 - 0.12 = 0.88\). The factor is always the fraction that remains.

③ The base 0.88 is below 1: decay at \(1 - 0.88 = 0.12\), a 12% annual loss. Then

\(P(3) = 40000(0.88)^3 = 40000(0.681472) \approx 27{,}259\)

Answer: ① 1.05   ② 0.88   ③ 12% annual decay; \(P(3) \approx 27{,}259\). After three years of losing 12%, about 68% remains — not \(100\% - 3 \times 12\% = 64\%\), because each year's loss is taken from a smaller amount. Percent change compounds; it never simply adds.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Confusing the growth rate with the growth factor. “Grows 5% per year” means the base is \(1.05\) — never \(0.05\). Write \(f(x) = 300(0.05)^x\) and you have built a function that destroys 95% of its value every year, the opposite of the story. The same trap runs in reverse for decay: a 12% loss is the base \(0.88\), not \(0.12\). Before you trust any model you write, test one step of it by hand: does multiplying the initial value by your base once produce the value the story predicts after one step? The factor is what remains: \(1 + r\) for growth, \(1 - r\) for decay.

💻 On Your Calculator: Watching the Exponential Win 10 min

At desmos.com/calculator, race a generous line against a modest exponential:

  1. Graph y = 100 + 50x and y = 100(1.5)^x. The line hands out $50 every step; the curve grants only 50% of a starting 100.
  2. Click the + and add a table with \(x = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5\) and both formulas as columns. Read the race: at \(x = 0\) they tie at 100; at \(x = 1\) they tie again, both exactly 150. Between the ties the line is (barely) ahead — check \(x = 0.5\): the line reads 125, the curve about 122.47.
  3. Past \(x = 1\) the curve takes the lead and never returns it: at \(x = 2\) it is 225 to the line's 200; by \(x = 5\) it is 759.375 to 350 — more than double.
  4. Now steepen the line: change it to \(y = 100 + 500x\), even \(y = 100 + 5000x\), and zoom out. The crossing point retreats to the right — but it always exists. Every exponential growth curve eventually overtakes every line, because multiplication compounds and addition cannot. The calculator confirms; your algebra convinces — the ratio test from Example 1 is the proof the pixels can only illustrate.
▶  Try it yourself: A table reads \(x = 0, 1, 2, 3\); \(y = 80,\ 60,\ 45,\ 33.75\). Classify it, write the rule, and name the percent rate. Work it on paper before you peek.
Differences (\(-20, -15, -11.25\)) are not constant; ratios are: \(\frac{60}{80} = \frac{45}{60} = \frac{33.75}{45} = 0.75\). Exponential decay: \(y = 80(0.75)^x\), a 25% loss per step — the factor 0.75 is the 75% that remains.
▶  Try it yourself: Find the exponential function through \((0, 5)\) and \((3, 40)\), and verify it against both points.
\(a = f(0) = 5\); then \(5b^3 = 40\) gives \(b^3 = 8\), so \(b = 2\). Thus \(f(x) = 5 \cdot 2^x\). Check: \(f(0) = 5\) ✓ and \(f(3) = 5 \cdot 8 = 40\) ✓.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The whole chessboard — all sixty-four squares summed — comes to \(2^{64} - 1 \approx 1.8 \times 10^{19}\) grains of wheat. At roughly 25 milligrams a grain, that is on the order of 460 billion tonnes: several centuries of the entire modern world's annual wheat harvest, promised in a sentence that sounded like pocket change. The legend survives because the mathematics does — human intuition is built for adding, and exponential growth quietly refuses to add.

Conclusion

Today you met the multiplication engine: \(f(x) = ab^x\), with its initial value out front and its growth factor in the base, diagnosed in tables by the constant-ratio fingerprint, built from a rate or from two points, and revealed as Unit 1's geometric sequence grown up into a smooth curve defined at every real input. You also learned the one translation this family demands: rate to factor, \(1 + r\) or \(1 - r\), always the fraction that remains. But a formula is only half a function's identity. Tomorrow we draw this family — the sweeping curve, the anchor points every graph shares, and a horizontal line the graph chases forever without touching. You met that idea in Unit 2 at the far ends of rational functions; tomorrow it returns as a permanent feature of every exponential. Bring your transformation instincts: shifts, stretches, and reflections all still apply.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. What single arithmetic fingerprint separates exponential data from linear data over equal steps, and why does it work?

2. In what sense are a geometric sequence and an exponential function the same idea in two languages — and what does the function lens add?

3. Why is the growth factor \(1 + r\) for growth but \(1 - r\) for decay — what does the factor actually measure?

4. Why must every exponential growth curve eventually overtake every line, no matter how steep the line?
