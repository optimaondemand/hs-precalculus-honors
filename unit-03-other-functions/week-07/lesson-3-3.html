The Number e and Continuous Growth
Lesson · This week's Focus assignment draws on this material
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to explain where the number \(e\) comes from, as the ceiling of the compounding expression \(\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n\).

  • I will be able to compare compounding frequencies with the compound interest formula and describe how the gains shrink.

  • I will be able to model continuous growth with \(A = Pe^{rt}\).

  • I will be able to evaluate natural exponential expressions with Excel and Desmos, and estimate a “when” question graphically.

📚 VOCABULARY

e: The number \(2.718281828\ldots\) — irrational, unending, unrepeating — that \(\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n\) approaches as \(n\) grows without bound. The base of the natural exponential function.

Compound interest: Interest computed on the balance including previously earned interest — interest earning interest. Formula: \(A = P\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}\), with principal \(P\), annual rate \(r\), and \(n\) compoundings per year for \(t\) years.

Continuous compounding: The limiting case — compounding at every instant, \(n\) pushed past all bounds — giving \(A = Pe^{rt}\).

Natural exponential function: \(f(x) = e^x\), the exponential whose base is \(e\); the standard model for growth that happens continuously rather than in steps.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In 1683 the Swiss mathematician Jacob Bernoulli asked a banker's question and found a philosopher's answer. Suppose a preposterously generous bank pays 100% interest per year, and you deposit $1. Compounded once, at year's end you have $2. But what if the bank compounds twice — 50% at midyear, then 50% on the new balance? You get \(1.5 \times 1.5 = \$2.25\). Compound quarterly and it is \((1.25)^4 \approx \$2.44\). More compounding, more money — always. So Bernoulli pressed the question to its edge: compound monthly, daily, hourly, every instant — does the dollar grow without limit?

It does not. The answer creeps upward toward a ceiling it never passes, a number that turned out to be one of the most important constants in all of mathematics. Half a century later Leonhard Euler named it \(e\) and computed its digits: \(e \approx 2.718281828\ldots\), irrational, its decimal expansion never repeating. Here is the unit's virtue of wonder in its purest form: nobody chose this number. It was not designed, like a coinage system, or measured, like a planet's mass. It was found hiding inside the act of compounding itself — and it has been turning up ever since, in population biology, radioactive decay, probability, and the geometry of curves, wherever growth happens continuously.

Watch the cornering happen. One dollar, 100% annual interest, compounded \(n\) times, yields \(\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n\) dollars — each of the \(n\) periods multiplies the balance by \(1 + \frac{1}{n}\). Now let \(n\) climb:

Compounding \(n\) \(\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n\)
Annually12
Semiannually22.25
Quarterly42.44140625
Monthly122.613035

Hourly8,7602.718127
The ceiling\(n \to \infty\)\(e \approx 2.718282\)

From daily to hourly — 24 times more compounding — buys about a third of a cent. The expression is not marching to infinity; it is stalling, and the wall it stalls against is \(e\). Why? Each refinement adds interest-on-interest for ever-shorter stretches — but the interest those stretches could earn is itself already nearly captured by the previous refinement. The leftovers shrink faster than the refinements multiply.

Compound and Continuous Growth

  • Compounded \(n\) times per year: \(A = P\left(1 + \dfrac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}\) — each of the \(nt\) periods multiplies the balance by \(1 + \frac{r}{n}\).
  • Compounded continuously: \(A = Pe^{rt}\) — the ceiling of the formula above as \(n \to \infty\), because \(\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{n}\) closes in on \(e^{r}\) just as \(\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n\) closes in on \(e\).
  • \(r\) enters as a decimal: 5% is \(r = 0.05\) — always.

One formula for growth in steps, one for growth as flow — and \(e\) is the bridge between them.

📊 In Excel: Corner e Yourself 15 min

Do not take the table above on faith — rebuild it. Open a blank Excel workbook:

  1. In column A, starting at A2, enter the compounding counts: 1, 2, 4, 12, 52, 365, 8760, 525600 (that last one is every minute of the year).
  2. In B2 type =(1+1/A2)^A2 and fill down. Format column B to six decimal places.
  3. Read down the column and watch the stall: the jump from row to row collapses — whole cents, then tenths, then \(2.718127 \to 2.718279\) as you go from every hour to every minute. A 60-fold refinement moves the fourth decimal place.
  4. In D2 type =EXP(1) — Excel's built-in \(e\) — and compare. Your minute-by-minute value agrees with \(e = 2.718282\ldots\) to five decimal places. The ceiling is real, and you have just measured it.
  5. Keep the workbook: swap the formula to =(1+0.05/A2)^A2 and watch the same stall against \(e^{0.05}\). The construction generalizes — that is the assignment's honors stretch, half-built.

Example 1

You invest \(\$1000\) at 5% annual interest for 10 years. Climb the compounding ladder: find the balance with annual, quarterly, monthly, daily, and continuous compounding.

Solution:

Each rung uses \(A = 1000\left(1 + \frac{0.05}{n}\right)^{10n}\), and the top rung uses \(A = 1000e^{0.05 \cdot 10} = 1000e^{0.5}\):

Annual: \(1000(1.05)^{10} \approx \$1628.89\)

Quarterly: \(1000(1.0125)^{40} \approx \$1643.62\)

Monthly: \(1000\left(1 + \tfrac{0.05}{12}\right)^{120} \approx \$1647.01\)

Daily: \(1000\left(1 + \tfrac{0.05}{365}\right)^{3650} \approx \$1648.66\)

Continuous: \(1000e^{0.5} \approx \$1648.72\)

Answer: The ladder climbs $1628.89, $1643.62, $1647.01, $1648.66, $1648.72 — and look at the gains: +$14.73, +$3.39, +$1.65, +$0.06. Moving from monthly to infinitely many compoundings — the entire rest of the ladder — buys $1.71, less than two dollars. Bernoulli's discovery, priced in your own account: compounding more helps, but toward a ceiling, never toward the sky.

Example 2

A town of 38,000 grows continuously at 2.4% per year. Write the model and find the population after 6 years.

Solution:

Continuous growth is \(A(t) = Pe^{rt}\) with \(P = 38000\) and \(r = 0.024\), so \(A(t) = 38000e^{0.024t}\). At \(t = 6\):

\(A(6) = 38000e^{0.024 \cdot 6} = 38000e^{0.144} \approx 38000(1.154884) \approx 43{,}886\)

Answer: About 43,900 people. Populations suit the continuous model for a good reason: a town does not grow in an annual burst on December 31 — births and arrivals flow through every day of the year. Where growth is a flow, \(e\) is the natural base.

Example 3

You want an account to hold \(\$5000\) eight years from now. It earns 3% compounded continuously. How much must you deposit today?

Solution:

Here the unknown is the principal. Set up \(5000 = Pe^{0.03 \cdot 8} = Pe^{0.24}\) and divide both sides by \(e^{0.24}\):

\(P = \dfrac{5000}{e^{0.24}} = 5000e^{-0.24} \approx 5000(0.786628) \approx \$3933.14\)

Answer: Deposit about $3,933.14. Check forward: \(3933.14 \, e^{0.24} \approx 5000\) ✓. Note the move: dividing by \(e^{0.24}\) is multiplying by \(e^{-0.24}\) — a negative exponent runs the growth film backward, and solving for \(P\) needed only that.

But now try the question everyone actually asks: how long until a deposit doubles? For \(\$1000\) at 5% continuous, that demands solving \(1000e^{0.05t} = 2000\) — and the unknown \(t\) is locked in the exponent, exactly where Lesson 3-2's intercepts hid. Estimate it honestly in Desmos: graph \(y = 1000e^{0.05x}\) and \(y = 2000\), click the intersection, and read \(t \approx 13.9\) years. That is a measurement, not a solution. The tool that solves it — that undoes an exponential exactly — arrives next week, and it was the greatest labor-saving invention in the history of calculation.

⚠️ WHERE AI HELPS — AND WHERE IT DOESN'T
Ask your AI tutor the question Bernoulli asked: “Why doesn't \(\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n\) grow to infinity as \(n\) grows? Explain without calculus.” Then hold its answer up against your own Excel table. A good explanation must account for the stall you measured — each refinement adds interest-on-interest over shorter stretches, but that interest was already nearly captured by the refinement before, so the additions shrink faster than they multiply. A weak or hallucinated answer waves at “limits” without explaining why the additions shrink, or worse, asserts the expression grows forever (your spreadsheet refutes that in eight rows). You hold the evidence; the AI holds only words. Make the words answer to the evidence — that is the supervising habit this course is building.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Two errors, one small and one philosophical. First: plugging the percent in whole — \(r = 5\) instead of \(r = 0.05\). Then \(1000e^{5 \cdot 10} = 1000e^{50}\) is about \(5 \times 10^{24}\) dollars — more than all the money on Earth, from a savings account. If your answer could buy the planet, recheck \(r\). Second: believing continuous compounding makes money grow without bound in finite time. It never does — continuous compounding is the ceiling of the ladder, not an escape from it. \(Pe^{rt}\) is a perfectly finite number for every finite \(t\); in Example 1 it beat daily compounding by six cents.
▶  Try it yourself: Compute \(\left(1 + \frac{1}{100}\right)^{100}\) to six decimal places. How far below \(e\) does it still sit? Work it (with a calculator) before you peek.
\((1.01)^{100} \approx 2.704814\). Against \(e \approx 2.718282\), that is still about \(0.0135\) short — a hundred compoundings and the third decimal place is still wrong. The climb toward \(e\) is real but unhurried; that slowness is exactly why the stall in your Excel table is so convincing.
▶  Try it yourself: \(\$2500\) is invested at 3.6% compounded continuously. Write the model and find the balance after 5 years.
\(A(t) = 2500e^{0.036t}\); then \(A(5) = 2500e^{0.18} \approx 2500(1.197217) \approx \$2993.04\). (If you got an astronomical number, you used \(r = 3.6\) — the decimal matters.)
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The digits of \(e\) begin \(2.7\,1828\,1828\,45\,90\,45\ldots\) — and generations of students have gratefully memorized the “1828, 1828” repetition. But \(e\) is irrational (Euler proved it in 1737), so its decimal expansion can never truly repeat: the pattern is pure coincidence, and it breaks immediately after. Even the number's name is quietly humble — Euler simply used the letter \(e\) in his notes, and it stuck. A constant discovered rather than designed, wearing a pattern that dissolves the moment you trust it: \(e\) rewards wonder and punishes assumption, which makes it very good company for a mathematician.

Conclusion

Today you watched a number get cornered: Bernoulli's compounding question, pressed refinement by refinement, stalled against a ceiling that no amount of compounding can breach, and that ceiling — \(e \approx 2.718282\), irrational, unchosen, everywhere — became a new base for a new function, \(e^x\), with its own formula \(A = Pe^{rt}\) for growth that flows instead of steps. You climbed the ladder in dollars and found the rungs shrinking; you rebuilt the stall in Excel with your own formula; and you ran into the locked door again — how long until…? — which graphs can estimate but not yet solve. Tomorrow assembles the full modeling toolkit around what you now own: doubling times, half-lives, radiocarbon clocks, and interest in all its frequencies — every exponential story the world actually tells, run forward. And remember: this week's Focus assignment is today's — the compounding ladder and the continuous model are the two method problems your teacher reads closely. Build them like arguments.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does more frequent compounding always help, yet never help past a ceiling — what shrinks faster than the refinements multiply?

2. In what sense was \(e\) discovered rather than invented, and why does that distinction feed wonder?

3. When does a continuous model \(Pe^{rt}\) suit a situation better than a stepped model \(P\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}\)?

4. What can a graphical estimate of “how long until…?” deliver, and what must it leave for an exact tool?
