📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
This is Week 8's Focus assignment — the one set this week your teacher grades for method. The two gold-badged ⭐ METHOD PROBLEMS are the lesson's two crafts: a proof (the quotient rule, from the exponent laws, every step of the machinery labeled) and a condensation (a three-term expression collapsed to a single logarithm and evaluated exactly, each rule cited by name as it acts). Your teacher reads them as logical chains — every link justified, nothing asserted because it “looks right.” Around them: expansion and condensation drills, change-of-base evaluations with their verification lines, and an examiner's problem where five claimed identities — real and counterfeit mixed — face your judgment.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
Every logarithm rule is an exponent law read in the mirror. If you truly believe that, what should you do when you meet a “rule” you cannot trace back to an exponent law?
📝 PROBLEM SET

Show your work for every problem in neat vertical form — one step per line, equals signs aligned, final answers labeled. On every expansion or condensation, cite the rule by name at the step where it acts. Fractions stay exact.

1
Expand completely, evaluating any piece that has an exact value (all variables positive):   ① \(\ln\!\left(\dfrac{x}{y^3 z}\right)\)   ② \(\log\!\left(\dfrac{1000\,x^2}{y^5}\right)\).
2
Condense to a single logarithm; if it has an exact value, find it:   ① \(\log 6 + \log 5 - \log 3\)   ② \(2\ln x + \tfrac{1}{2}\ln y - 3\ln z\)   ③ \(\tfrac{1}{2}\log_3 81\).
⭐ METHOD PROBLEM — your teacher grades this one for method
3
Prove the quotient rule \(\log_b\!\left(\dfrac{M}{N}\right) = \log_b M - \log_b N\) from the laws of exponents, using the lesson's machinery with every step labeled: ① name the exponents (\(m = \log_b M\), \(n = \log_b N\)); ② translate both to exponential form; ③ apply the exponent law for quotients of like bases; ④ translate back, stating in words why the final equation says exactly what the rule claims. Then put your theorem to work: expand \(\log_b\!\left(\dfrac{x^4\sqrt{y}}{z^3}\right)\) completely, citing the quotient, product, and power rules by name at the steps where they act. (Write \(\sqrt{y}\) as a power first.)
4
Evaluate by change of base, to four decimal places, with a before-you-compute estimate (between which two integers?) and an exponentiation check afterward (e.g., \(5^{\text{your answer}} \approx 30\)):   ① \(\log_5 30\)   ② \(\log_2 7\).
⭐ METHOD PROBLEM — your teacher grades this one for method
5
Condense and evaluate exactly: \(3\log_2 4 + \log_2 5 - \log_2 10\). Collapse the expression to a single logarithm one rule at a time — cite each rule by name at the step where it acts (power rule before the terms combine, then product, then quotient) — and finish with the exponent question that produces the exact value. End with the verification line: raise 2 to your answer and confirm it returns the argument of your single logarithm.
6
The examiner's desk: Five claimed identities follow. Mark each VALID (cite the rule it instantiates) or INVALID (supply a numeric counterexample — specific numbers, both sides computed):   ① \(\log_b(MN) = \log_b M \cdot \log_b N\)   ② \(\ln(x^5) = 5\ln x\)   ③ \(\log(a + b) = \log a + \log b\)   ④ \((\ln x)^3 = 3\ln x\)   ⑤ \(\dfrac{\log a}{\log b} = \log_b a\).   (Fair warning: at least one is valid, at least two are counterfeit, and one of the five is the AI Checkpoint's impostor back for a second opinion.)
7
Explain why: Why does the product rule work? Restate the lesson's proof in your own words, one paragraph — no symbols required beyond \(b^m \cdot b^n = b^{m+n}\) — making clear where the exponent law enters and why “logs turn multiplication into addition” was already true before logarithms were invented.
8
Honors stretch: Prove that \(\log_b a \cdot \log_a b = 1\) for any two legal bases \(a\) and \(b\), using the change-of-base formula. Then say in one sentence what this identity means about the two logarithms as a pair.
💡 CHECK YOURSELF WITH DESMOS
After working on paper, audit yourself in Desmos (desmos.com/calculator): for problems 1–3, assign test values (try \(x = 3\), \(y = 4\), \(z = 5\)) and type your original expression on one line and your expanded or condensed form on the next — equal identities return equal numbers, every time. For problem 6, run each claimed identity at two different sets of numbers before writing your verdict. For problems 4 and 5, exponentiate your answers and watch the arguments come back. The calculator confirms; your algebra convinces.
