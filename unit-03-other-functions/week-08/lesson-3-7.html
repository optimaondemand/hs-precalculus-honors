Properties of Logarithms
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to prove the product, quotient, and power rules for logarithms directly from the laws of exponents.

  • I will be able to expand a logarithm of a product, quotient, or power into a sum of simple logarithms — and condense such a sum back into a single logarithm.

  • I will be able to derive and use the change-of-base formula to evaluate a logarithm of any base with any calculator.

📚 VOCABULARY

Product rule: \(\log_b(MN) = \log_b M + \log_b N\) — the logarithm of a product is the sum of the logarithms.

Quotient rule: \(\log_b\!\left(\dfrac{M}{N}\right) = \log_b M - \log_b N\) — the logarithm of a quotient is the difference.

Power rule: \(\log_b(M^p) = p \log_b M\) — an exponent inside the logarithm becomes a multiplier outside it.

Change-of-base formula: \(\log_b x = \dfrac{\log_a x}{\log_a b}\) — any logarithm, rewritten as a ratio of logarithms in a base of your choosing.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

On Monday you learned what Napier invented; today you learn why it worked. The three rules of this lesson are the entire labor-saving engine. To multiply two enormous numbers, an astronomer looked up their logarithms in a table, added the two entries — addition being cheap where multiplication is dear — and looked up which number owns that logarithm. Multiplication had become a lookup and a sum. Fold the same trick into a pair of sliding rulers marked on logarithmic scales and you get the slide rule, invented within a decade of Napier's book — and for the next three and a half centuries, that sliding stick was calculation: every bridge, ship, and engine of the industrial age, the Empire State Building, and the machines of the early space program were figured on logarithmic scales. Slide rules were still riding in engineers' pockets during the Apollo missions. All of it rests on three identities you can prove in the next ten minutes — because each one is an exponent law from Lesson 3-1, wearing its logarithmic grammar.

The whole strategy of proof is one move: name the exponents, then let the exponent laws speak. A logarithm is an exponent — so give the exponents letters and watch each rule fall out.

Example 1

Prove the product rule: \(\log_b(MN) = \log_b M + \log_b N\) for all positive \(M, N\) and any legal base \(b\).

Solution:

Name the exponents. Let

\(m = \log_b M \quad \text{and} \quad n = \log_b N\)

Translate to exponential grammar — by Monday's definition, these say exactly:

\(M = b^m \quad \text{and} \quad N = b^n\)

Let the exponent law speak. Multiply the two equations; same base, so exponents add:

\(MN = b^m \cdot b^n = b^{m+n}\)

Translate back. The statement \(MN = b^{m+n}\) says, in logarithmic grammar: \(m + n\) is the exponent that turns \(b\) into \(MN\). That is,

\(\log_b(MN) = m + n = \log_b M + \log_b N \quad \blacksquare\)

Answer: Proved — and notice what the proof reveals. The product rule is not a new fact about logarithms; it is the old fact \(b^m \cdot b^n = b^{m+n}\), read in the mirror. Logs turn multiplication into addition because exponents already did.

The Three Rules — Each an Exponent Law in the Mirror

For positive \(M, N\), any real \(p\), and legal base \(b\):

  • Product rule: \(\log_b(MN) = \log_b M + \log_b N\) — the mirror of \(b^m b^n = b^{m+n}\).
  • Quotient rule: \(\log_b\!\left(\dfrac{M}{N}\right) = \log_b M - \log_b N\) — the mirror of \(\dfrac{b^m}{b^n} = b^{m-n}\).
  • Power rule: \(\log_b(M^p) = p\log_b M\) — the mirror of \((b^m)^p = b^{mp}\); run the machinery: \(M^p = (b^m)^p = b^{mp}\), so \(\log_b(M^p) = mp\).

All three proofs use the identical machinery: name the exponents, translate, apply the exponent law, translate back. Example 1 wrote out the first; the power rule is sketched above; the quotient rule is yours — writing that proof, every step labeled, is Method Problem A on this week's Focus assignment.

Example 2

Expand completely: \(\log_b\!\left(\dfrac{x^3 y^2}{z^5}\right)\), for positive \(x, y, z\).

Solution:

Work from the outside in, citing each rule as it acts:

\(\log_b\!\left(\dfrac{x^3 y^2}{z^5}\right) = \log_b(x^3 y^2) - \log_b(z^5)\)   (quotient rule)

\(= \log_b(x^3) + \log_b(y^2) - \log_b(z^5)\)   (product rule)

\(= 3\log_b x + 2\log_b y - 5\log_b z\)   (power rule, three times)

Answer: \(3\log_b x + 2\log_b y - 5\log_b z\). One compound logarithm has become an inventory of simple ones — exactly the form a table-user (or, next lesson, an equation-solver) needs.

Example 3

Condense to a single logarithm and evaluate exactly: \(2\log_6 4 + \log_6 27 - \log_6 12\).

Solution:

Reverse the expansion process, citing each rule:

\(2\log_6 4 = \log_6(4^2) = \log_6 16\)   (power rule — coefficients climb inside first)

\(\log_6 16 + \log_6 27 = \log_6(16 \cdot 27) = \log_6 432\)   (product rule)

\(\log_6 432 - \log_6 12 = \log_6\!\left(\dfrac{432}{12}\right) = \log_6 36\)   (quotient rule)

Now the exponent question: 6 to what exponent gives 36? Since \(6^2 = 36\),

\(\log_6 36 = 2\)

Answer: \(2\). Three messy-looking logarithms, none of them individually a nice number, collapse to an integer — the rules see structure the eye does not.

One tool remains. Your calculator offers \(\log\) (base 10) and \(\ln\) (base \(e\)) — but Example 3 lived in base 6. What if a base-6 logarithm doesn't collapse to an integer? Derive the escape hatch. Set \(y = \log_b x\), so \(b^y = x\). Take the logarithm of both sides in any base you like — say base \(a\) — and apply the power rule:

\(\log_a(b^y) = \log_a x \;\; \Rightarrow \;\; y \log_a b = \log_a x \;\; \Rightarrow \;\; y = \dfrac{\log_a x}{\log_a b}\)

Key Fact: Change of Base

\(\log_b x = \dfrac{\log_a x}{\log_a b} \qquad \text{in particular} \qquad \log_b x = \dfrac{\log x}{\log b} = \dfrac{\ln x}{\ln b}\)

Any logarithm in any base is a ratio of logarithms in a base your calculator speaks — Napier's “ratio-number” earning its name one more time. Note carefully what this formula is and is not: \(\frac{\log x}{\log b}\) is a quotient of two logarithms, which is not the same creature as the logarithm of a quotient. The Common Mistake box below has more to say about that.

Example 4

Evaluate \(\log_7 100\) to four decimal places, and verify the answer.

Solution:

Estimate first: \(7^2 = 49 < 100 < 343 = 7^3\), so the answer lies between 2 and 3, nearer 2. Change to base 10, where \(\log 100 = 2\) exactly:

\(\log_7 100 = \dfrac{\log 100}{\log 7} = \dfrac{2}{0.8451} \approx 2.3666\)

Verify by exponentiating — the confirm-by-inverting habit from Monday: \(7^{2.3666} \approx 100.0\) ✓.

Answer: \(\log_7 100 \approx 2.3666\) — between 2 and 3, nearer 2, exactly as estimated.

⚠️ AI CHECKPOINT: THE PLAUSIBLE IMPOSTOR

A student asked an AI to simplify \(\log(x + 10) + \log x\). It answered:

“First, distribute the logarithm over the addition: log(x + 10) = log x + log 10 = log x + 1. So the whole expression becomes log x + 1 + log x = 2 log x + 1. By the power rule this is log(x²) + 1, so the simplified form is log(10x²).”

Every step after the first is genuine — the power rule is quoted correctly, the final condensation is clean. But the first move is counterfeit: there is no rule that distributes a logarithm over a sum. One line of arithmetic convicts it:

\(\log(10 + 10) = \log 20 \approx 1.301, \qquad \text{but} \qquad \log 10 + \log 10 = 1 + 1 = 2\)

Not equal — the “identity” fails at the first number you try. The true rule turns products into sums: \(\log(MN) = \log M + \log N\). Nothing turns sums into sums; \(\log(M + N)\) simply does not decompose.

Diagnose the slip: the machine pattern-matched the shape of the product rule — “log of two things combined = logs, combined” — onto the wrong operation, then executed flawlessly from the false premise. This is the unit's standing AI lesson: fluent execution, missing discipline. The machine knows what the rules look like; you must know what they are — which is exactly why today's proofs matter. On this week's Focus set, you will sit as examiner over five claimed identities, real and counterfeit mixed.

▶  Try it yourself: Expand completely: \(\log\!\left(\dfrac{100\,x^3}{y}\right)\), for positive \(x, y\) — one term should come out as a plain number.
Quotient rule, then product rule, then power rule: \(\log(100x^3) - \log y = \log 100 + \log(x^3) - \log y = 2 + 3\log x - \log y\). The 100 dissolved into the exact value \(\log 100 = 2\) — always evaluate the pieces that can be evaluated.
▶  Try it yourself: Condense and evaluate exactly: \(2\log 5 + \log 4\).
Power rule: \(2\log 5 = \log 25\). Product rule: \(\log 25 + \log 4 = \log 100 = 2\). Two “calculator numbers” — \(\log 5\) and \(\log 4\) are both irrational decimals — combine into a clean integer.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Three illegal moves, each a real rule worn by the wrong expression. ① \(\log(x + y) \neq \log x + \log y\) — the product rule dresses up as a “sum rule”; the AI Checkpoint's counterexample (\(\log 20\) vs. \(2\)) kills it. ② \((\log x)^3 \neq \log(x^3)\) — the right side is the power rule's \(3\log x\); the left side cubes the output, a different act entirely. Test \(x = 1000\): \((\log 1000)^3 = 27\) but \(\log(1000^3) = 9\). ③ \(\dfrac{\log a}{\log b} \neq \log\!\left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right)\) — the left side is a perfectly good number, but its name is change of base: it equals \(\log_b a\), not the log of a quotient. When in doubt, run one numeric test case — a true identity survives every number you throw at it; a fake rarely survives the first.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
A slide rule is the product rule made of wood: two rulers whose markings are spaced by logarithm, so that sliding one along the other physically adds the logs — and adding logs, as you proved today, multiplies the numbers. When engineers carried slide rules through the Apollo era, they were carrying Example 1 in their pockets, machined to three significant figures.

Conclusion

Three rules, one proof strategy: name the exponents, translate, let the exponent laws speak, translate back. The product, quotient, and power rules are the exponent laws in logarithmic mirror-writing, and change of base makes every logarithm computable with the two buttons any calculator offers — always with the exponentiation check standing verification. You also sat as examiner for the first time: a fluent argument fell to a one-line counterexample, and the difference between knowing a rule's shape and knowing the rule was the whole verdict. Tomorrow the rules go to work on the problem they were sharpened for: a variable trapped in an exponent. Growth questions this unit could only estimate — when does the account double, when does the sample decay to half — get exact answers at last. This lesson is the heart of this week's Focus set: the quotient-rule proof and a condensation, method problems both, are waiting for your best hand.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. In what sense is each logarithm rule “an exponent law in the mirror,” and why does the machinery \(M = b^m, N = b^n\) prove all three?

2. Why did turning multiplication into addition change the working lives of astronomers and engineers for three and a half centuries?

3. How does one numeric counterexample outweigh any amount of fluent, confident derivation — and what does that teach about auditing an AI's algebra?

4. The change-of-base formula writes one logarithm as a ratio of two others. Why is that not the same as the quotient rule, and how do you keep the two straight?
