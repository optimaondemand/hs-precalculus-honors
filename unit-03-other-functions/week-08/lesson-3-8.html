Exponential and Logarithmic Equations
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to solve exponential equations exactly — by matching bases when possible, and by taking logarithms of both sides when not.

  • I will be able to solve logarithmic equations by rewriting in exponential form and by the one-to-one property.

  • I will be able to apply the guard-and-judge discipline to logarithmic equations: post the forbidden list first, and reject every extraneous candidate.

📚 VOCABULARY

One-to-one property: Exponentials and logarithms never repeat an output, so equal outputs force equal inputs: if \(b^m = b^n\) then \(m = n\); if \(\log_b M = \log_b N\) (with \(M, N > 0\)) then \(M = N\).

Extraneous solution (returning from Unit 2 — same discipline, new equations): A candidate produced by legal algebra that fails the original equation. In Unit 2 the culprit was a cleared denominator; this week it is a logarithm whose argument goes zero or negative.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

This lesson pays a debt. Back in Lesson 3-3, continuous growth left you holding “when” questions you could only answer approximately — when does the account double, when does the culture reach a million — by zooming a graph or running a table. The promissory note said: wait for logarithms. Today it comes due. A logarithm extracts an exponent; an equation with the variable trapped in an exponent is precisely a request for that extraction. Every tool from this week — the definition, the graphs, and especially yesterday's three rules — assembles now into an exact answer machine.

Start with the case that needs no new machinery at all: when both sides can wear the same base, the one-to-one property finishes the job. An exponential function never repeats an output — you saw that in its always-climbing graph — so \(b^{\text{this}} = b^{\text{that}}\) forces \(\text{this} = \text{that}\).

Example 1

Solve \(2^{3x - 1} = 16\).

Solution:

Both sides speak base 2, since \(16 = 2^4\):

\(2^{3x-1} = 2^4\)

\(3x - 1 = 4\)   (one-to-one property)

\(x = \dfrac{5}{3}\)

Verify: \(3 \cdot \frac{5}{3} - 1 = 4\), and \(2^4 = 16\) ✓.

Answer: \(x = \dfrac{5}{3}\). One honest rewriting, one appeal to one-to-one, done.

But 16 was a gift. Solve \(3^x = 20\) and no integer power of 3 comes to the rescue — 20 is not on base 3's list. This is where the logarithm earns its keep: take the logarithm of both sides — both sides whole, never term by term — and let yesterday's power rule pull the variable down out of the exponent.

Example 2

Solve \(3^x = 20\) exactly, then approximate to three decimal places.

Solution:

Take the natural log of both sides, then apply the power rule:

\(\ln(3^x) = \ln 20\)

\(x \ln 3 = \ln 20\)   (power rule — the exponent steps outside)

\(x = \dfrac{\ln 20}{\ln 3} \approx \dfrac{2.9957}{1.0986} \approx 2.727\)

Sanity-check the size: \(3^2 = 9 < 20 < 27 = 3^3\), so an answer between 2 and 3 — nearer 3 — is right where it should be. Verify: \(3^{2.727} \approx 20.0\) ✓.

Answer: \(x = \dfrac{\ln 20}{\ln 3} \approx 2.727\). The exact form is the answer; the decimal is its report. (You may recognize \(\frac{\ln 20}{\ln 3}\) as \(\log_3 20\) by change of base — the two roads meet.)

Example 3

Solve \(5e^{2t} = 60\) exactly, then approximate.

Solution:

Isolate the exponential first — the logarithm should meet \(e^{2t}\) alone:

\(e^{2t} = 12\)

\(2t = \ln 12\)   (the definition: \(\ln 12\) is the exponent that turns \(e\) into 12)

\(t = \dfrac{\ln 12}{2} \approx \dfrac{2.4849}{2} \approx 1.242\)

Verify: \(5e^{2(1.242)} = 5e^{2.484} \approx 5 \cdot 12.0 = 60\) ✓.

Answer: \(t = \dfrac{\ln 12}{2} \approx 1.242\). Had you taken logs before dividing by 5, you would face \(\ln(5e^{2t})\) — legal, but now needing the product rule to untangle. Isolate first; it is the shorter road.

Logarithmic Equations: Guard, Work, Judge

Equations with the variable inside logarithms revive Unit 2's discipline for rational equations, word for word:

  • Guard — post the forbidden list FIRST. Every argument of every logarithm must be positive. Write the conditions before touching the equation; together they fence the only territory where solutions may live.
  • Work — condense and convert. Use yesterday's rules to collapse to a single logarithm, then rewrite in exponential form (or, if both sides are single logs of the same base, apply the one-to-one property directly).
  • Judge — try every candidate against the guard and the original equation. The converted equation is usually polynomial, and polynomials have no memory of the logarithm's domain. Any candidate outside the fence is extraneous: reject it by name.

Cleared denominators, squared radicals, condensed logarithms — different operations, same side effect: the new equation is more permissive than the old. The forbidden-list-first habit is the one discipline that covers them all.

Example 4

Solve \(\log_2 x + \log_2(x - 2) = 3\).

Solution:

Guard: \(x > 0\) and \(x - 2 > 0\) — together, \(x > 2\). Posted.

Work: condense by the product rule, then convert by the definition:

\(\log_2\big(x(x-2)\big) = 3\)

\(x(x - 2) = 2^3 = 8\)

\(x^2 - 2x - 8 = 0\)

\((x - 4)(x + 2) = 0\)

\(x = 4 \;\; \text{or} \;\; x = -2\)

Judge: \(x = 4\) clears the fence (\(4 > 2\)); confirm in the original: \(\log_2 4 + \log_2 2 = 2 + 1 = 3\) ✓. But \(x = -2\) is forbidden — it would ask for \(\log_2(-2)\), which does not exist. Reject it by name: extraneous.

Answer: \(x = 4\). The quadratic honestly produced two candidates; only the guard knew that one of them was a ghost. Where did the ghost come from? The condensed equation \(x(x-2) = 8\) is a plain polynomial with no memory that its parents were logarithms — it happily accepts \(-2\); the original equation cannot.

Example 5

Solve \(\log(x + 4) = \log(2x - 1)\).

Solution:

Guard: \(x + 4 > 0\) and \(2x - 1 > 0\) — together, \(x > \frac{1}{2}\).

Work: two single logarithms, same base, equal — the one-to-one property applies directly:

\(x + 4 = 2x - 1 \;\; \Rightarrow \;\; x = 5\)

Judge: \(5 > \frac{1}{2}\) ✓, and in the original both sides read \(\log 9\) ✓.

Answer: \(x = 5\). Even when the algebra is one line, the guard and the judge still take their turns — the habit is cheap, and the one time it catches a ghost pays for every time it didn't.

Now the debt from Lesson 3-3, paid in full. An account holds $500 growing continuously at 4% per year: \(A(t) = 500e^{0.04t}\). When does it double? Set \(A(t) = 1000\):

\(500e^{0.04t} = 1000 \;\; \Rightarrow \;\; e^{0.04t} = 2 \;\; \Rightarrow \;\; 0.04t = \ln 2 \;\; \Rightarrow \;\; t = \dfrac{\ln 2}{0.04} \approx 17.3 \text{ years}\)

Not “about 17, by the table” — exactly \(\frac{\ln 2}{0.04}\), with the decimal as its report. Every doubling time, half-life, and “when” question in the sciences resolves by this move; notice too that the answer is \(\ln 2\) divided by the growth rate, which is why doubling times barely care about the starting amount — the 500 cancelled before the logarithm ever arrived.

💻 On Your Calculator: Solving by Intersection 12 min

Today's flagship calculator skill — learn it once, use it all year. Any equation is a meeting of two graphs: left side and right side, each a curve, solutions where they cross. At desmos.com/calculator (or below):

  1. Enter Example 2 as two functions: y = 3^x on one line, y = 20 on the next. Click the intersection point — Desmos marks it and reports \((2.727, 20)\). The \(x\)-coordinate is your solution, to calculator precision; your \(\frac{\ln 20}{\ln 3}\) is the same number, exact.
  2. Do the same for Example 4: y = log_2(x) + log_2(x - 2) and y = 3. Look closely: the curves cross once, at \(x = 4\) — and nothing whatsoever happens at \(x = -2\). The graph never even visits there; the left side's domain ends at \(x = 2\). The algebra's second candidate was always a ghost, and the picture shows you a world in which it never existed.
  3. Verify Example 1 (\(y = 2^{3x-1}\) meets \(y = 16\) at \(x = 1.667 \approx \frac{5}{3}\)), Example 3 (\(y = 5e^{2x}\) meets \(y = 60\) at \(x \approx 1.242\)), and the doubling problem (\(y = 500e^{0.04x}\) meets \(y = 1000\) near \(x \approx 17.3\) — widen the window to see it).
  4. Division of labor, as always: the intersection click gives a decimal and a picture; only the algebra gives \(\frac{\ln 20}{\ln 3}\), \(\frac{\ln 12}{2}\), \(\frac{\ln 2}{0.04}\) — the exact forms that later mathematics builds on. The calculator confirms; your algebra convinces.

▶  Try it yourself: Solve \(5^{2x} = 125\) by matching bases, and verify.
\(125 = 5^3\), so \(5^{2x} = 5^3\); one-to-one gives \(2x = 3\), so \(x = \frac{3}{2}\). Verify: \(5^{2 \cdot 3/2} = 5^3 = 125\) ✓.
▶  Try it yourself: Solve \(\log_3 x + \log_3(x + 6) = 3\) with the full guard-and-judge discipline. Expect two candidates.
Guard: \(x > 0\) and \(x + 6 > 0\), so \(x > 0\). Work: \(\log_3\big(x(x+6)\big) = 3\), so \(x(x+6) = 27\), giving \(x^2 + 6x - 27 = 0 = (x+9)(x-3)\): candidates \(x = 3\) and \(x = -9\). Judge: \(x = 3\): \(\log_3 3 + \log_3 9 = 1 + 2 = 3\) ✓. \(x = -9\): forbidden — extraneous, rejected. Answer: \(x = 3\).
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Two ways to lose tonight's equations. First: taking the logarithm of a sum term by term. Facing \(2^x + 7 = 20\), the temptation is to write “\(\ln(2^x) + \ln 7 = \ln 20\)” — but that is yesterday's impostor, \(\log(a+b) = \log a + \log b\), sneaking back in work clothes. The logarithm acts on each whole side, once: isolate first (\(2^x = 13\)), then take logs. Second: skipping the guard on logarithmic equations. The condensed polynomial will happily hand you candidates the original equation cannot accept — post the forbidden list before the algebra, and judge every candidate against it after. A ghost caught at the guard post costs one line; a ghost that reaches your answer costs the problem.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
When archaeologists date a wooden tool or a bone, they are solving Example 3. Living things hold a known fraction of radioactive carbon-14, which decays exponentially after death with a half-life of about 5,730 years; measure how much remains, set up \(Ae^{kt} = (\text{measured amount})\), and the dig site's age is a logarithm away. The “when” question you learned to answer exactly today is, quite literally, how we know how old the past is.

Conclusion

The week's arc is complete. Monday defined the logarithm as an exponent; Tuesday drew its portrait in the mirror; Wednesday proved the rules that move it; and today the whole toolkit freed variables from exponents and from inside logarithms — matching bases where possible, taking logs of whole sides where not, and holding every logarithmic equation to the guard-and-judge discipline that Unit 2's rational equations first taught you. Doubling times and half-lives now have exact answers: the promissory note from Lesson 3-3 is paid. ❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. When does matching bases settle an exponential equation, and what exactly does taking the logarithm of both sides accomplish when it cannot?

2. Where do extraneous solutions of logarithmic equations come from — and why does the converted polynomial “forget” what the original equation knew?

3. How is the guard-and-judge discipline for logarithmic equations the same discipline you used on rational equations, and what side effect does it guard against in each case?

4. Solving by intersection shows one crossing where the algebra produced two candidates. What does each representation know that the other does not?
