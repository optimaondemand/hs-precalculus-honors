Graphs of Logarithmic Functions
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to graph logarithmic parent functions from their anchor points, with the vertical asymptote drawn dashed and labeled.

  • I will be able to apply shifts, stretches, and reflections to logarithmic graphs, and read off the new asymptote, domain, and range.

  • I will be able to find the domain of any logarithmic expression by solving the argument-positive inequality.

  • I will be able to explain how the exponential and logarithmic graphs mirror each other across the line \(y = x\), feature for feature.

📚 VOCABULARY

Logarithmic parent function: \(f(x) = \log_b x\) for a legal base \(b\) — domain \((0, \infty)\), range all reals, passing through \((1, 0)\) and \((b, 1)\).

Vertical asymptote (returning from Unit 2 — new address): A vertical line the graph approaches without bound. Rational functions dug theirs at zeros of the denominator; the logarithm posts its own at the edge of its domain, where the argument reaches 0.

Reflection across \(y = x\): The mirror flip that swaps every point's coordinates, sending \((a, b)\) to \((b, a)\) — the geometric relationship between \(y = b^x\) and \(y = \log_b x\).

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Napier published the idea; Henry Briggs spent the rest of his life tabulating it, and his 1624 Arithmetica Logarithmica ran to tens of thousands of common logarithms computed to fourteen decimal places. Here is the wonder hiding in that dusty ledger: a table of values is a graph, read one row at a time. Briggs was plotting a curve, point by laborious point, generations before mathematicians routinely drew “the graph of a function” at all. Today you will draw in thirty seconds the shape he computed for years — and you will find you already know it, because you met its mirror image in Lesson 3-2.

Start where the last lesson ended: \(\log_b x\) answers the question \(b^y = x\). So the pairs that satisfy \(y = \log_2 x\) are exactly the pairs of \(y = 2^x\) with the coordinates swapped. The exponential owns \((1, 2)\), so the logarithm owns \((2, 1)\); the exponential owns \((3, 8)\), so the logarithm owns \((8, 3)\). Geometrically, swapping coordinates reflects a point across the line \(y = x\) — and a whole curve of swapped points is the whole curve, reflected. Build the parent \(y = \log_2 x\) honestly from its anchors:

\(\left(\tfrac{1}{2}, -1\right), \qquad (1, 0), \qquad (2, 1), \qquad (4, 2)\)

Check each against the exponent question: \(2^{-1} = \frac{1}{2}\), \(2^0 = 1\), \(2^1 = 2\), \(2^2 = 4\) ✓. Now watch the left end. To push the output down to \(-2, -3, -4, \ldots\) the input must fall to \(\frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{8}, \frac{1}{16}, \ldots\) — the curve dives as \(x\) slips toward 0, and it can never touch \(x = 0\) itself, because \(\log_2 0\) does not exist. The exponential's horizontal asymptote \(y = 0\) has passed the torch: reflected across \(y = x\), it becomes the logarithm's vertical asymptote, the \(y\)-axis. Unit 2's asymptote discipline — dashed line, labeled equation — applies at the new address unchanged.

The Mirror Inventory: Everything the Exponential Owns, the Log Owns Reflected

For \(y = b^x\) and \(y = \log_b x\) with \(b > 1\):

  • Domain ↔ Range: the exponential's domain (all reals) is the log's range; the exponential's range \((0, \infty)\) is the log's domain. The mirror swaps the axes' jobs.
  • Asymptote: the exponential's horizontal asymptote \(y = 0\) reflects to the log's vertical asymptote \(x = 0\).
  • Intercept swap: the exponential's \(y\)-intercept \((0, 1)\) reflects to the log's \(x\)-intercept \((1, 0)\); the exponential's \((1, b)\) reflects to the log's \((b, 1)\).
  • Growth temperament: the exponential rises ever faster; its mirror rises ever slower. Both increase forever — the log merely takes its time.

One reflection, four inheritances. If you ever forget a fact about the logarithm's graph, reflect the exponential fact you do remember.

Example 1

Graph the parent \(f(x) = \log_2 x\) from its anchor points, and state its domain, range, and asymptote.

Solution:

Plot the anchors \(\left(\frac{1}{2}, -1\right)\), \((1, 0)\), \((2, 1)\), \((4, 2)\), each certified by the exponent question. Draw the \(y\)-axis's ghost: a dashed vertical line at \(x = 0\), labeled. The curve dives along the asymptote at the left, crosses the \(x\)-axis at \((1, 0)\), and climbs — ever more slowly, never leveling — through \((2, 1)\) and \((4, 2)\).

Answer: Domain \((0, \infty)\); range all real numbers; vertical asymptote \(x = 0\). The parent exponential's whole biography, read in the mirror.

Example 2

Graph \(f(x) = \log_2(x - 3) + 1\), and state its vertical asymptote, domain, and range.

Solution:

This is the parent shifted right 3 and up 1 — the same transformation language you have used all course, applying to this family without modification. Everything the parent owns moves with it. The asymptote \(x = 0\) slides to \(x = 3\); the domain slides to \((3, \infty)\); the range, all reals, is unbothered by shifts. Slide the anchors: \((1, 0) \to (4, 1)\), \((2, 1) \to (5, 2)\), \((4, 2) \to (7, 3)\). Certify each in the formula:

\(f(4) = \log_2 1 + 1 = 0 + 1 = 1 \; \checkmark \qquad f(5) = \log_2 2 + 1 = 2 \; \checkmark \qquad f(7) = \log_2 4 + 1 = 3 \; \checkmark\)

Answer: Vertical asymptote \(x = 3\); domain \((3, \infty)\); range all real numbers — with the graph diving along \(x = 3\) and climbing through the three verified anchors.

Example 3

Find the domain of \(g(x) = \log(5 - 2x)\).

Solution:

The argument must be positive — that is the guard, posted first:

\(5 - 2x > 0\)

\(-2x > -5\)

\(x < \dfrac{5}{2}\)   (dividing by \(-2\) flips the inequality)

Answer: Domain \(\left(-\infty, \frac{5}{2}\right)\), with vertical asymptote \(x = \frac{5}{2}\). Spot-check one value on each side: \(g(0) = \log 5\) exists ✓; \(g(3) = \log(-1)\) does not ✓. The negative coefficient also reflects the graph — this logarithm opens leftward, diving along its asymptote from the left.

Example 4

A logarithmic graph has a dashed vertical asymptote at \(x = -2\) and passes through \((-1, 0)\), \((0, 1)\), and \((2, 2)\). Write its equation.

Solution:

The asymptote sits 2 units left of the parent's, so the argument is \(x + 2\): try \(y = \log_b(x + 2)\). The point \((-1, 0)\) obliges any base: \(\log_b 1 = 0\) ✓. Now let \((0, 1)\) choose the base: \(\log_b 2 = 1\) forces \(b = 2\). Judge the third point, which took no part in the fitting:

\(\log_2(2 + 2) = \log_2 4 = 2 \; \checkmark\)

Answer: \(y = \log_2(x + 2)\). Note the craft: two points determined the equation, and the third point — unused, unsuborned — delivered the verdict on it.

💻 On Your Calculator: Watching the Mirror Work 10 min

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), put the reflection on screen:

  1. Graph y = 2^x, then y = log_2(x) — type log, then an underscore, and Desmos drops you into the base slot — then the mirror itself, y = x.
  2. Zoom so the window is square (equal scales on both axes — drag or use the wrench; a stretched window bends the mirror and hides the symmetry).
  3. Click the exponential at \((2, 4)\); now click the logarithm at \((4, 2)\). Coordinates swapped — one point reflected across \(y = x\). Find one more mirror pair yourself, and check the pair \((0,1) \leftrightarrow (1,0)\), the intercept swap from the concept box.
  4. Follow the log rightward. It leaves the top of any window you set — slowly, but it leaves. Now add y = log_2(x - 3) + 1 from Example 2 and watch the whole apparatus — asymptote, anchors, dive — slide right 3 and up 1, exactly as the algebra promised.

▶  Try it yourself: Graph \(g(x) = \log_3(x + 1) - 2\): state the vertical asymptote and domain, and find three anchor points by sliding the parent's. Work it on paper before you peek.
Parent \(\log_3 x\) shifted left 1, down 2. Vertical asymptote \(x = -1\); domain \((-1, \infty)\); range all reals. Anchors: \((1, 0) \to (0, -2)\), \((3, 1) \to (2, -1)\), \((9, 2) \to (8, 0)\). Certify: \(g(0) = \log_3 1 - 2 = -2\) ✓, \(g(2) = \log_3 3 - 2 = -1\) ✓, \(g(8) = \log_3 9 - 2 = 0\) ✓.
▶  Try it yourself: Find the domain and vertical asymptote of \(h(x) = \log_2(6 - x)\), and say which direction the graph opens.
Guard: \(6 - x > 0\), so \(x < 6\) — domain \((-\infty, 6)\), vertical asymptote \(x = 6\). The negative coefficient on \(x\) reflects the graph: it opens leftward, diving along \(x = 6\) and climbing slowly toward the left. Check: \(h(4) = \log_2 2 = 1\) exists ✓; \(h(7) = \log_2(-1)\) does not ✓.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Two traps tonight. First, the flipped sign: solving \(5 - 2x > 0\) requires dividing by \(-2\), and the inequality flips — forget that, and you will report the domain on the wrong side of the asymptote. Spot-check one value from your claimed domain in the original argument; if it comes out negative, you flipped wrong. Second, the imaginary ceiling: the logarithmic graph looks like it is leveling off, and it is not. It has no horizontal asymptote — slow is not stopped. Claim a ceiling and the function will exceed it; see the box below for how far you would have to walk to watch it happen.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
How slowly does \(y = \log_2 x\) climb? It reaches \(y = 100\) only when \(x = 2^{100} \approx 1.27 \times 10^{30}\) — a 31-digit number, roughly a million trillion trillion. And yet it does reach 100, and 1000, and every height ever named, because \(2^y\) has an answer for each one. The exponential's honesty about the long run — Unit 2's virtue — has a mirror image: the logarithm never lies about leveling off, because it never levels off. Patience without limit, in the shape of a curve.

Conclusion

The logarithm's graph is the exponential's graph reflected across \(y = x\): domain and range trade places, the horizontal asymptote turns vertical, the intercepts swap seats, and runaway growth becomes unhurried — but unbounded — climbing. You graphed the parents from anchor points, slid them with the same transformation grammar the whole course speaks, found domains by posting the argument-positive guard, and fitted an equation to a graph with an unused point standing as judge. So far, though, you can only state logarithms; you cannot yet compute with them. Tomorrow that changes: three rules — product, quotient, power — that convert multiplication into addition, division into subtraction, and powers into multiples. They are the engine inside Napier's tables, the reason a slide rule works, and the machinery that ran the world's arithmetic for 350 years. ❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does reflecting \(y = b^x\) across the line \(y = x\) produce exactly the graph of \(y = \log_b x\), point for point?

2. Where does a transformed logarithm's vertical asymptote come from, and why does the domain always begin exactly there?

3. The logarithm increases forever yet has no horizontal asymptote — how can both be true, and how would you convince a skeptic who trusts the picture?

4. Which features of a logarithmic graph can Desmos display convincingly, and which — the asymptote's exact location, the absence of a ceiling — must the algebra certify?
