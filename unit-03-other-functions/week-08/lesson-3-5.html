🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to define \(\log_b x\) as the answer to an exponent question, and state the conditions on the base and the argument.

  • I will be able to convert fluently between exponential form and logarithmic form — two sentences saying one fact.

  • I will be able to evaluate logarithms exactly, without a calculator, by asking “to what exponent?”

  • I will be able to evaluate common and natural logarithms with technology and verify each answer by exponentiating.

📚 VOCABULARY

Logarithm: The exponent to which a base must be raised to produce a given number: \(\log_b x\) is the exponent \(y\) with \(b^y = x\). From the Greek logos (ratio) + arithmos (number) — “ratio-number” — a word coined by John Napier for his invention.

Base (of a logarithm): The number \(b\) being raised to the exponent — the same base as in the matching exponential statement. Legal bases: \(b > 0\) and \(b \neq 1\).

Argument: The input of the logarithm — the number whose exponent is being asked for. In \(\log_b x\), the argument is \(x\), and it must be positive.

Common logarithm: The base-10 logarithm, written \(\log x\) with the base omitted: \(\log x = \log_{10} x\).

Natural logarithm: The base-\(e\) logarithm, written \(\ln x\): the exponent question asked of the number \(e\) from Lesson 3-3.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In 1614 a Scottish laird named John Napier published a slim book with an immodest title: A Description of the Marvelous Canon of Logarithms. He had spent roughly twenty years computing its tables by hand — two decades of arithmetic, performed so that everyone after him could skip theirs. The marvel he was selling: a table that converts every multiplication into an addition. Word traveled fast. Henry Briggs, London's leading mathematician, rode all the way to Edinburgh to meet Napier; by their second summer together the two had agreed to rebuild the tables on base 10, the base our common logarithm still uses. Kepler seized the new tool to compute his planetary tables. Two centuries later the astronomer Laplace looked back and said that logarithms, “by shortening the labors, doubled the life of the astronomer.” Almost no invention in the history of mathematics has been so plainly, so immediately, a gift — and it begins with a question you can already answer.

Here is the question. You know what \(2^5\) is. Now run it backwards: 2 to what exponent gives 32? You said “5” before you finished reading the sentence. That act — recovering the exponent from the base and the result — is the logarithm. Nothing more mysterious is being defined today:

\(\log_2 32 = 5\)   because   \(2^5 = 32\)

The Definition: A Logarithm Is an Exponent

For \(b > 0\), \(b \neq 1\), and \(x > 0\):

\(\log_b x = y \iff b^y = x\)

The two statements are one fact in two grammars. The exponential sentence \(b^y = x\) leads with the exponent and announces the result; the logarithmic sentence \(\log_b x = y\) leads with the result and asks for the exponent. Reading \(\log_b x\), always hear the question: “b to what exponent gives x?”

In this informal sense the logarithm undoes the exponential — each reverses the other's question. Unit 4 will build the full theory of inverse functions and this pair will be its star witness; for now, the plain language is enough.

Example 1

Convert each statement into the other form.

① \(2^6 = 64\)   ② \(10^{-3} = 0.001\)   ③ \(9^{1/2} = 3\)   ④ \(\log_5 125 = 3\)   ⑤ \(\log_2 \frac{1}{16} = -4\)   ⑥ \(\ln 14 = t\)

Solution:

Each conversion is the same move: identify base, exponent, result, and re-seat them in the other grammar.

① \(\log_2 64 = 6\)   ② \(\log 0.001 = -3\)   ③ \(\log_9 3 = \frac{1}{2}\)

④ \(5^3 = 125\)   ⑤ \(2^{-4} = \frac{1}{16}\)   ⑥ \(e^t = 14\)

Answer: As above. Notice ② and ⑤: exponents may be negative — that is legal and common. It is the argument (0.001, \(\frac{1}{16}\)) that stays positive, never the logarithm's output.

Example 2

Evaluate each logarithm exactly, without a calculator.

① \(\log_2 32\)   ② \(\log_9 3\)   ③ \(\log 1000\)   ④ \(\ln e^2\)   ⑤ \(\log_5 1\)   ⑥ \(\log_7 7\)

Solution:

Ask the exponent question each time, and verify by exponentiating:

① 2 to what exponent gives 32?   \(2^5 = 32\), so \(\log_2 32 = 5\).

② 9 to what exponent gives 3?   \(9^{1/2} = \sqrt{9} = 3\), so \(\log_9 3 = \frac{1}{2}\).

③ 10 to what exponent gives 1000?   \(10^3 = 1000\), so \(\log 1000 = 3\).

④ \(e\) to what exponent gives \(e^2\)?   The question answers itself: \(\ln e^2 = 2\).

⑤ 5 to what exponent gives 1?   \(5^0 = 1\), so \(\log_5 1 = 0\).

⑥ 7 to what exponent gives 7?   \(7^1 = 7\), so \(\log_7 7 = 1\).

Answer: \(5, \; \frac{1}{2}, \; 3, \; 2, \; 0, \; 1\). The last two are worth memorizing as patterns, not facts: they hold for every legal base.

Key Fact: Two Values Every Base Agrees On

\(\log_b 1 = 0\)   (because \(b^0 = 1\))    \(\log_b b = 1\)   (because \(b^1 = b\))

Every logarithm, whatever its base, passes through these two facts — and tomorrow you will see them become two anchor points, \((1, 0)\) and \((b, 1)\), shared by every logarithmic graph.

Example 3

Solve each equation by rewriting it in exponential form:   ① \(\log_x 49 = 2\)   ② \(\log_3 x = 4\)

Solution:

① The statement says: \(x\) to the exponent 2 gives 49.

\(x^2 = 49 \;\; \Rightarrow \;\; x = 7 \;\; \text{or} \;\; x = -7\)

Judge the candidates against the definition: a logarithm's base must be positive (and not 1). Reject \(x = -7\).

② The statement says: 3 to the exponent 4 gives \(x\).

\(x = 3^4 = 81\)

Answer: ① \(x = 7\)   ② \(x = 81\). Notice the discipline in ①: the algebra produced two candidates and the definition sat as judge — the same candidates-then-verdict habit you built on rational equations in Unit 2. It returns in force in Lesson 3-8.

Example 4

Most logarithms are not whole numbers or tidy fractions. Evaluate \(\log 47\) and \(\ln 20\) with a calculator, to four decimal places — and verify each answer.

Solution:

Before computing, estimate: \(10^1 = 10 < 47 < 100 = 10^2\), so \(\log 47\) is between 1 and 2. The calculator gives

\(\log 47 \approx 1.6721\)

Verify by exponentiating — ask the calculator for \(10^{1.6721}\) and get \(\approx 47.0\) ✓. Likewise \(e^2 \approx 7.39 < 20 < e^3 \approx 20.1\), so \(\ln 20\) is just under 3:

\(\ln 20 \approx 2.9957, \qquad e^{2.9957} \approx 20.0 \; \checkmark\)

Answer: \(\log 47 \approx 1.6721\); \(\ln 20 \approx 2.9957\). The verification step is not decoration — exponentiating your answer re-asks the original question, and only the right exponent answers it.

💻 On Your Calculator: Ask the Question, Then Check the Answer 8 min

At desmos.com/calculator, build the confirm-by-inverting habit:

  1. Type log(47) — in Desmos, log is the common (base-10) logarithm — and read \(1.6721\ldots\). On the next line type 10^1.6721 and watch 47.0 come back. One line asks the exponent question; the next line checks the answer by re-raising the base.
  2. Do the same with ln(20) and e^2.9957.
  3. For other bases, type log_2(32) (keyboard: log, then an underscore to drop into the base slot) and confirm the 5 with 2^5.
  4. Now try to break it: ask for log(-5) and log(0). Desmos returns undefined for both — and by the end of this lesson you will be able to say exactly why the machine refuses.

Every logarithm you compute this week, confirm by exponentiating. The calculator confirms; your algebra convinces.

▶  Try it yourself: Evaluate exactly, then verify by exponentiating: \(\log_4 64\), \(\;\log_2 \frac{1}{8}\), \(\;\log 0.01\), \(\;\ln \sqrt{e}\).
\(\log_4 64 = 3\) since \(4^3 = 64\).   \(\log_2 \frac{1}{8} = -3\) since \(2^{-3} = \frac{1}{8}\).   \(\log 0.01 = -2\) since \(10^{-2} = 0.01\).   \(\ln \sqrt{e} = \frac{1}{2}\) since \(e^{1/2} = \sqrt{e}\).
▶  Try it yourself: Solve by rewriting in exponential form: ① \(\log_x 8 = 3\)   ② \(\log_4 x = \frac{3}{2}\).
① \(x^3 = 8\), so \(x = 2\) (a cube root has only one real candidate, and it is a legal base).   ② \(x = 4^{3/2} = \left(\sqrt{4}\right)^3 = 2^3 = 8\).
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Two errors account for most early logarithm trouble. First: reading \(\log_b x\) as a multiplication — “log times x.” It is nothing of the kind: \(\log_b x\) is a single number, an exponent, and “log” by itself has no value any more than a lone exponent does. Second: forgetting the domain. \(\log 0\) and \(\log(-5)\) do not exist — because \(\log x\) asks “10 to what exponent gives \(x\)?” and \(10^y\) is positive for every real exponent \(y\). No exponent produces 0; no exponent produces a negative. The question has no answer, so the logarithm has no value. The argument of a logarithm must be positive — post it like a guard, today and every day this week.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Napier's twenty-year project left more than one mark on your page. In the same body of work he championed a small dot to separate the whole part of a number from its fractional part — and his advocacy helped make the decimal point standard. Every time you write 1.6721, you are using Napier's punctuation to record Napier's invention.

Conclusion

A logarithm is an exponent: \(\log_b x\) is the answer to “\(b\) to what exponent gives \(x\)?”, and the equivalence \(\log_b x = y \iff b^y = x\) lets you convert any statement about exponents into a statement about logarithms and back. With it you evaluated logarithms exactly, solved simple equations by re-seating them in exponential form, and computed common and natural logs by machine — always confirming by exponentiating. And you know why the argument must stay positive: exponentials only ever output positive numbers, so only positive numbers have logarithms. That last fact is really a fact about a graph — and tomorrow you will meet that graph: the exponential curve's mirror image, reflected across the line \(y = x\), inheriting everything the exponential owns in reversed order. Napier built the table; tomorrow we draw its portrait.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. In what precise sense is a logarithm “just an exponent,” and how does the equivalence \(\log_b x = y \iff b^y = x\) capture that?

2. Why do \(\log_b 1 = 0\) and \(\log_b b = 1\) hold for every legal base, and why must the base be positive and different from 1?

3. Why does \(\log(-5)\) have no value — and what does that refusal have to do with the outputs of exponential functions?

4. What made Napier's invention “double the life of the astronomer,” and what does that suggest about what logarithms do to multiplication?
