📋 BEFORE YOU BEGIN

This test gathers the whole four-week arc into one sitting: exponential growth and the strange constancy of \(e\), the logarithm and its hard-won properties, radical functions with their guards and ghosts, piecewise laws and their borders, functions compared across representations, and systems — where everything met. It has three parts: Part A (fluency), Part B (reasoning — write these as arguments), and Part C (one synthesis problem reaching back to Unit 2).

Ground rules. Set aside a quiet block of about 90 minutes and complete the test in one honest sitting. Work alone: no classmates, no AI tools, no solution websites — this is the one place where the mathematics must be entirely yours. A calculator may be used for arithmetic only (evaluating powers, quotients, and logarithms as numbers); every setup, conversion, property, guard, and step of reasoning must appear on your page. Lesson pages and notes stay closed — the properties this test needs are ones you have proved yourself.

Write the honor line at the top of your first page and sign it: “I completed this test on my own, without help from people, notes, or AI tools.” Intellectual honesty is a quarter of the rubric — and in the unit that taught you to reject answers your own algebra manufactured, it is also the method.

📝 PART A — FLUENCY (Lessons 3-1 through 3-14)

Neat vertical work for every problem — one step per line, equals signs aligned, exact values before decimals, labeled answers.

1
An exponential function passes through \((0, 5)\) and \((3, 40)\). Find its rule \(f(x) = a \cdot b^x\) (show the solving for \(b\), not just the answer), state its percent growth rate per unit, and give its horizontal asymptote.
2
Sketch \(f(x) = 2^{x-1} - 4\) by hand: name the transformations of the parent \(2^x\), draw the asymptote dashed, and find exactly the \(y\)-intercept and the \(x\)-intercept (the \(x\)-intercept requires solving; show it). State domain and range in interval notation.
3
Evaluate exactly, no calculator, each in one clean line with its reason:   ① \(\log_2 32\)   ② \(\log_9 3\)   ③ \(\ln e^7\)   ④ \(\log 0.001\). Then convert \(5^3 = 125\) to logarithmic form.
4
① Expand completely: \(\log_3\!\left(\dfrac{27x^5}{y^2}\right)\).   ② Condense to a single logarithm: \(2\ln x + \tfrac{1}{2}\ln y - 3\ln z\). Name each property as you use it.
5
A \$2{,}000 deposit earns 6% annual interest for 5 years. Compute the balance ① compounded quarterly and ② compounded continuously — formulas set up by hand, calculator for the arithmetic — then ③ state the difference the compounding style makes, to the cent.
6
Solve each exponential equation, exact answer first:   ① \(5 \cdot 3^{2x} = 45\) (no logarithm needed — say why)   ② \(4^{x+1} = 9\) (exact form as a ratio of logarithms, then a decimal to three places).
7
① Graph \(f(x) = \sqrt{x-1} + 2\) with three labeled anchor points; state domain and range. ② Solve \(\sqrt{2x+3} = x\) with the full guard-and-judge discipline — guards posted with reasons, every candidate judged against the original, verdict in a sentence.
8
For \(f(x) = \begin{cases} x + 4 & \text{if } x < -1 \\ x^2 & \text{if } -1 \leq x < 2 \\ 6 - x & \text{if } x \geq 2 \end{cases}\):   ① evaluate \(f(-3)\), \(f(-1)\), and \(f(2)\), naming which piece owns each boundary input; ② graph the function with every open and closed dot deliberate; ③ at each boundary, state whether the graph jumps or is a sewn seam — the two boundaries behave differently.
9
The equation \(f(x) = 3x + 2\) meets the table \(g\): \((0, 2), (1, 4), (2, 8), (3, 16)\). ① Diagnose \(g\)'s family from its ratios and write its rule. ② Find both inputs where the table proves \(f\) and \(g\) are equal. ③ Compare their average rates of change on \([0, 3]\). ④ State who leads between the two meeting points, and who leads forever after the second — with the feature that certifies each claim.
10
Solve the system \(y = x^2 - 4x + 5\) and \(y = 2x - 3\) algebraically. Write both solutions as ordered pairs and verify each in both equations, arithmetic shown.
📝 PART B — REASONING (write these as arguments)

These problems are graded primarily on the quality of your reasoning. Full sentences where explanation is asked; every claim earned, not asserted.

11
Prove the product rule for logarithms — \(\log_b(MN) = \log_b M + \log_b N\) — from the exponent rules, as you did in Lesson 3-7: set \(M = b^m\) and \(N = b^n\), and write every line, explaining the key step in a sentence. Then use your theorem to expose the impostor: a student claims \(\log_b(M + N) = \log_b M + \log_b N\). Refute it two ways — a numerical counterexample computed exactly, and one sentence explaining why the true rule pairs multiplication with addition (what do logarithms convert into what?).
12
Solve \(\log_3(x+6) + \log_3(x-2) = 2\) with the full discipline: guards posted first (each with its reason), the condensed equation solved, every candidate judged against the original. State your conclusion in a full sentence — and then explain, in two or three more sentences, exactly where the counterfeit candidate was manufactured: which property enlarged the equation's domain, and why does the rejected value satisfy the condensed equation but not the original?
13
For which values of \(k\) does the line \(y = 2x + k\) meet the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 5\) in exactly one point? Substitute, and let the discriminant of the resulting quadratic carry the argument — show its three cases (two meetings, one, none) as an inequality story in \(k\), not just the boundary values. For the positive \(k\), find the point of tangency and verify it lies on both graphs. Close with one sentence connecting the discriminant's sign to the picture.
📝 PART C — SYNTHESIS (this unit meets Unit 2)
14
On the Unit 2 test you watched the sequence \(2^n\) overtake \(n^3\). Here is the continuous version, as a system: two consulting firms forecast a client's monthly demand with dueling models — Firm P (polynomial): \(y = x^2\); Firm E (exponential): \(y = 2^x\) (both in thousands of orders, \(x\) in months). Put the system \(y = x^2\), \(y = 2^x\) on trial:
① Find the meetings. Verify by exact computation that \(x = 2\) and \(x = 4\) both solve the system, and give the two intersection points.
② Prove there is a third. Compute both functions at \(x = -1\) and at \(x = 0\), observe which is larger at each, and explain in two sentences why a continuous pair that trades the lead must cross somewhere between — locating the third meeting inside \((-1, 0)\) without finding it exactly. (Label this fact honestly: proved to exist, not computed.)
③ Referee the intervals. Using test values, state who leads on each interval the three crossings create — and notice the polynomial's brief comeback on \((2, 4)\).
④ The verdict, as a four-line model defense. The client asks which forecast to trust for the long run. Answer in exactly four labeled lines: claim (which model wins, and from when); structure (compare the growth ratios — what does \(\frac{2^{x+1}}{2^x}\) equal always, and what happens to \(\frac{(x+1)^2}{x^2}\) as \(x\) grows?); long-run (the end-behavior honesty argument: why no coefficient rescues a polynomial against a surviving exponential); domain of honesty (what real-world ceiling — capacity, market size — eventually makes both models dishonest, and what shape the truth then demands).
📊 GRADING RUBRIC

The whole test is graded against the standing four-criterion rubric, each criterion scored out of 10. Part A supplies most of the evidence for understanding and precision; Parts B and C supply most of the evidence for reasoning.

Criteria What excellent looks like Pts
Understanding of the material The right tool reaches every problem: logarithms unlock exponents, guards precede every squaring and every log condensation, boundary dots are deliberate, systems finish with verified ordered pairs, notation exact throughout. 10 pts
Quality of reasoning The proof in Part B reads as a real argument; counterfeit candidates are traced to their manufacturing step; the discriminant story covers all three cases; the synthesis verdict reasons about growth ratios and end behavior, not one data point. 10 pts
Presentation & precision Clean vertical work throughout, aligned equals signs, exact values before decimals, dashed asymptotes and deliberate endpoint dots on every graph, pages in order — forty points of work a stranger could follow. 10 pts
Intellectual honesty Honor line signed and honored; work shown as actually done; proved and approximated facts labeled as such (Part C demands it); any problem you could not finish carries an honest note of where and why you stopped — which earns respect here, never punishment. 10 pts
