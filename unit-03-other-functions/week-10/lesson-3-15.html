🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to translate an intersection question from the world — equilibrium, break-even, passing trajectories, catch-up — into a system of equations.

  • I will be able to choose the right solving tool for the families in the system: elimination for shared terms, substitution with guards for radicals, logarithms for exponentials.

  • I will be able to interpret each solution in context, with units, and judge every candidate against the model's domain of honesty before reporting it.

📚 VOCABULARY

Demand curve: The price buyers will pay as a function of quantity — falling, because plenty cheapens. Supply curve: the price sellers require as a function of quantity — rising, because scarcity of effort costs.

Equilibrium point: The intersection of supply and demand — the one quantity and price at which buyers and sellers agree, so the market clears with neither shortage nor surplus.

Break-even point: The intersection of cost and revenue — the output at which a venture stops losing money and starts making it.

Catch-up time: The moment two growing (or shrinking) quantities are equal — the intersection of two models of change, found where their equations are set face to face.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In 1890 the economist Alfred Marshall drew two curves crossing — supply rising, demand falling — and gave economics its most famous picture. Asked whether it is really demand (what buyers want) or supply (what production costs) that sets a price, Marshall refused the question: you might as well ask, he said, whether it is the upper or the under blade of a pair of scissors that cuts the paper. It takes both blades, and the cut happens where they meet. That is a system of equations wearing a waistcoat. Today, in the closing lesson of this unit, every function family you have gathered — the lines and polynomials of the fall, the exponentials, logarithms, radicals, and piecewise laws of the last four weeks — walks onto the same plane, and we ask of each pair the scissors question: where do they meet, and what does the meeting mean? This is also where Napier's four-hundred-year-old gift gets its payoff: some of today's meetings simply cannot be found without a logarithm.

Example 1

A farmers' market prices honey by the jar. Market research gives the demand curve \(p = 120 - 2q\) and the supply curve \(p = 30 + 4q\), where \(q\) is jars per week and \(p\) is dollars... per jar in cents of attention — dollars. Find the equilibrium and interpret it.

Solution:

Both expressions equal the same price \(p\), so set them face to face:

\(120 - 2q = 30 + 4q\)

\(90 = 6q \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; q = 15, \qquad p = 120 - 2(15) = 90\)

Verify in the supply curve: \(30 + 4(15) = 90\) ✓.

Answer: Equilibrium at \((15, 90)\): 15 jars per week at \$90 per jar (evidently remarkable honey). The interpretation is the point: price the honey above \$90 and sellers offer more jars than buyers take — surplus; price below \$90 and buyers want more than sellers bring — shortage. Only at the intersection do the two blades of the scissors agree. An intersection in context is never just a point; it is a sentence about the world, and you must write it.

Example 2

Now a market with a curved blade. Suppose supply follows a radical law, \(p = 2\sqrt{q}\) (early jars are cheap to add; later jars strain the hives), while demand is linear: \(p = 15 - q\). Find the equilibrium — with full discipline.

Solution:

Set the prices equal: \(2\sqrt{q} = 15 - q\). A radical equation — so Lesson 3-10's discipline reports for duty. Substitute \(u = \sqrt{q}\), with its guard posted: \(u \geq 0\). Then \(q = u^2\):

\(2u = 15 - u^2\)

\(u^2 + 2u - 15 = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; (u + 5)(u - 3) = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; u = 3 \;\text{ or }\; u = -5\)

Judge: \(u = -5\) violates the guard \(u = \sqrt{q} \geq 0\) — rejected, and doubly so: it is both algebraically impossible (no square root is negative) and economically meaningless. \(u = 3\) gives \(q = 9\), and \(p = 2\sqrt{9} = 6\).

Verify in the demand curve: \(15 - 9 = 6\) ✓.

Answer: Equilibrium at 9 jars per week, \$6 per jar. Notice what happened: a context problem walked in, and inside it was a radical equation with a ghost candidate — the same ghost, the same guard, the same judgment as two weeks ago. Context changes the costume, never the discipline.

Example 3

Two fireworks shells are fired vertically. The first launches from the ground: \(h_1(t) = -16t^2 + 64t\). The second launches one second's rig away on a 40-foot platform: \(h_2(t) = -16t^2 + 48t + 40\) (heights in feet, \(t\) in seconds). When are the shells at the same height, and how high is that?

Solution:

Set the heights equal — and watch the structure before you compute. Both models carry the identical gravity term \(-16t^2\), so elimination annihilates it:

\(-16t^2 + 64t = -16t^2 + 48t + 40\)

\(64t = 48t + 40\)

\(16t = 40 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; t = 2.5\)

Height at that moment: \(h_1(2.5) = -16(6.25) + 64(2.5) = -100 + 160 = 60\).

Verify in the second model: \(h_2(2.5) = -100 + 120 + 40 = 60\) ✓. Judge against reality: is \(t = 2.5\) inside both flights? \(h_1\) is airborne until \(t = 4\); \(h_2\) until \(t \approx 5.4\). Both aloft ✓.

Answer: The shells pass at the same height 60 feet, at \(t = 2.5\) seconds. The honors observation: two parabolas met in only one point, because gravity pulls both with the same \(-16t^2\) — their difference is a straight line, and a line crosses zero once. When two models share a term, the shared term is not information about their difference; elimination knows this before you do.

Example 4

A catch-up problem that only a logarithm can finish. Rivertown has 5{,}000 residents and grows 8% per year; Lakeside has 8{,}000 residents and grows 2% per year. When are the towns the same size, and how big are they then?

Solution:

Models: \(R(t) = 5000(1.08)^t\) and \(L(t) = 8000(1.02)^t\). Set them equal and herd the exponentials to one side:

\(5000(1.08)^t = 8000(1.02)^t\)

\(\left(\dfrac{1.08}{1.02}\right)^{t} = \dfrac{8000}{5000} = \dfrac{8}{5}\)

The unknown is captive in the exponent; only the logarithm can free it. Take \(\ln\) of both sides and apply the power rule:

\(t \,\ln\!\left(\dfrac{1.08}{1.02}\right) = \ln\!\left(\dfrac{8}{5}\right)\)

\(t = \dfrac{\ln(8/5)}{\ln(1.08/1.02)} = \dfrac{0.47000\ldots}{0.05716\ldots} \approx 8.22 \text{ years}\)

Verify by computing both populations at \(t = 8.22\): \(R \approx 5000(1.88) \approx 9{,}414\) and \(L \approx 8000(1.177) \approx 9{,}415\) — agreement to the rounding digit ✓.

Answer: The towns draw level after about 8.2 years, at roughly 9{,}400 residents each — and from that day Rivertown leads forever, because 8% compounds past 2% with no further argument needed. Before 1614, this problem had no exact method — merchants and astronomers ground through such questions by trial. Napier's logarithm turned it into four lines. That is the wonder this unit has been pointing at all along: an invention of pure thought that hands you the key to every locked exponent, forever.

One Plane, Every Family: The Synthesis

Step back and see what you now hold. Every family this course has met lives on the same plane, and a system may pair any two of them. The translation is always the same — two quantities, one moment of agreement, set the expressions face to face. Only the finishing tool changes, and the families themselves tell you which to draw:

  • Line meets line (Example 1, break-even): substitution or elimination — Algebra I, now with interpretation duties.
  • Polynomial meets polynomial (Example 3): eliminate shared terms first; what remains is simpler than either model.
  • Radical in the system (Example 2): guard, substitute, square if you must — and judge every candidate. Ghosts do not respect word problems.
  • Exponential meets exponential (Example 4): isolate the ratio, then logarithms — the inverse that unlocks the exponent.
  • Families that refuse exact algebra (\(2^x = x + 3\), \(\ln x = 5 - x\)): reasoning counts the solutions; Desmos locates them. Honesty requires saying which tool certified what.

And after every solve, two judgments before you report: units (an intersection in context is a sentence — write it) and the domain of honesty (negative jars, negative seconds, and populations beyond the model's warrant are rejected even when the arithmetic is flawless).

  2. Click the intersection: \((15, 90)\), confirming your algebra.
  3. Now make demand adjustable: replace it with y = a - 2x and click to create a slider for a. Drag \(a\) upward — a demand surge (honey goes viral) — and watch the equilibrium point slide up the supply curve: more jars, higher price. Drag \(a\) down and watch the market cool.
  4. Say what you saw in one sentence of economics: the equilibrium is not a fixed fact but the moving intersection of two live curves. Then, for Example 3, plot both shell heights with y = -16x^2 + 64x and y = -16x^2 + 48x + 40 and click the single crossing at \((2.5, 60)\). The calculator confirms; your algebra convinces.

⚠️ AI CHECKPOINT: THE SUPERVISED TUTOR

Today your AI works for you — as a tutor generating practice, under your supervision. Three tasks:

① Commission practice. Ask your AI: “Write two market-equilibrium problems for a precalculus student: one with a radical supply curve and linear demand, one where two exponentially growing quantities catch up. Use numbers that work out cleanly. Do not show solutions.” Solve both by hand, full discipline.

② Then audit the tutor. Ask the AI to solve its own radical problem — and read its work like an examiner. Does it post the guard? Does it judge both candidates from the squared equation? You know from Lesson 3-10's checkpoint that the middle of a method is where AI shines and the edges are where it slips. If it kept a ghost, tell it precisely what it skipped, and watch whether its correction is real or cosmetic.

③ Close the loop. Ask it to explain why the equilibrium price is the only stable one, then compress its paragraph into one honest sentence of your own. The mathematician supervises the tool: it drafts, you verify; it explains, you decide what is true. That habit — not any single answer — is what this strand of the course is training.

▶  Try it yourself: A print shop's costs are \(C(x) = 450 + 3.5x\) dollars for \(x\) posters; revenue is \(R(x) = 8x\). Find the break-even point and interpret both coordinates. Work it on paper before you peek.
Set \(450 + 3.5x = 8x\): \(450 = 4.5x\), so \(x = 100\), and \(R(100) = \$800\). Verify: \(C(100) = 450 + 350 = 800\) ✓. Break-even at \((100, 800)\): the 100th poster is where the shop stops paying to print and starts printing to profit — below 100 posters every sale still owes the setup cost; beyond it, each poster clears \(\$4.50\).
▶  Try it yourself: An investment of \$1{,}000 grows 10% per year. Exactly when does it reach \$2{,}000? Solve with logarithms, exactly and then as a decimal.
\(1000(1.10)^t = 2000 \Rightarrow (1.10)^t = 2 \Rightarrow t = \dfrac{\ln 2}{\ln 1.10} \approx \dfrac{0.6931}{0.0953} \approx 7.27\) years. Verify: \(1.1^{7.27} \approx 2.00\) ✓. (The banker's “Rule of 72” estimates \(72/10 = 7.2\) — your logarithm just certified the folklore.)
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Reporting the naked number. A student solves Example 1 and writes “\(q = 15\)” — and has answered nothing, because the question was about a market. An intersection in context has two coordinates and a meaning: 15 jars per week at \$90 per jar, the only price at which the market clears. The reverse error is just as common: accepting a solution the world forbids — a negative time, a negative quantity, the \(u = -5\) of Example 2 — because the algebra produced it. Every applied answer passes two gates: arithmetic truth (it satisfies both equations) and honest meaning (it lives inside the model's domain of honesty, in units, in a sentence).
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The number \(e\) — this unit's strange constant — was first sighted inside a money problem. In 1683 Jacob Bernoulli asked what happens to compound interest as compounding becomes continuous, and found the limit \(2.718\ldots\) waiting there. It was Leonhard Euler who tamed it, named it \(e\) (first in a 1731 letter, then in print in his Mechanica of 1736), and revealed it standing quietly at the center of all growth, decay, and — as you will one day see in calculus — nearly everything else. A constant discovered in a bank vault turned out to be a law of nature. If that does not produce wonder, check your pulse.

Today's four intersections — a market clearing, a ghost rejected in context, two shells passing at 60 feet, two towns drawing level by logarithm — used every discipline the unit taught, which is exactly why Thursday's slot carries the Unit 3 test: one sitting, everything from lesson 3-1 to this page, and the same four-part standard your work is always held to. Tonight's Focus assignment is the dress rehearsal — the one set this week your teacher grades for method. And when the test is behind you, notice the door this lesson leaves open. A system asks where two functions meet — the answer is a point. Unit 4 asks something deeper: how functions relate — how they combine, how one feeds its output into another, how a function can be undone. You have already seen the first case: the exponential and the logarithm, each reversing the other. Unit 4 makes that mutual courtesy a theory. From meetings between functions to relationships between them — that is the road from here.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. What must be true at an intersection point twice over — once in the algebra and once in the world — before it may be reported as an answer?

2. Given a system, how do the function families themselves announce which finishing tool to draw — elimination, guarded substitution, or the logarithm?

3. Why can a solution pass every arithmetic check and still be rejected? Who or what has the authority to reject it, and where in Example 2 was that authority exercised twice?

4. Systems answer where two functions meet. What questions about how functions relate to one another remain unasked — and which pair from this unit already hints at the answer?
