Systems of Equations: Linear and Nonlinear
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to solve two-variable linear systems by substitution and by elimination, and verify the solution in every original equation.

  • I will be able to solve nonlinear systems — a line meeting a parabola, a line meeting a circle, a curve meeting a curve — algebraically, judging every candidate pair against both originals.

  • I will be able to predict how many solutions a system can have from the shapes of its graphs, and solve graphically when exact algebra cannot finish the job.

📚 VOCABULARY

System of equations: Two or more equations considered together, sharing the same variables. The word is honest: the equations form a system — a set of simultaneous demands.

Solution of a system: An ordered pair \((x, y)\) that satisfies every equation at once. Geometrically: a point lying on every graph — an intersection. A solution is a point, never a lone number.

Substitution: Solving one equation for a variable and substituting that expression into the other — collapsing two equations in two unknowns into one equation in one unknown.

Elimination: Adding or subtracting (multiples of) the equations so that a variable cancels. Fastest when the same term appears in both equations — and that includes nonlinear terms like \(x^2\).

Nonlinear system: A system in which at least one equation graphs as something other than a line. New shapes, new possibilities: curves can meet 0, 1, 2, or more times.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Around 350 BC, long before algebra had symbols, the Greek geometer Menaechmus faced the most famous construction problem of antiquity: double the cube — build a cube with exactly twice the volume of a given one. That demands a length of \(\sqrt[3]{2}\), which no ruler and compass can produce. Menaechmus's astonishing move: he drew two curves — in our language, the parabola \(y = x^2\) and the hyperbola \(xy = 2\) — and observed that where they cross, the impossible number stands: substitute \(y = x^2\) into \(xy = 2\) and you get \(x \cdot x^2 = 2\), so \(x^3 = 2\). The intersection point solves the equation. That is the whole idea of today's lesson, twenty-three centuries old: to solve simultaneously is to find where curves meet, and to find where curves meet is to solve simultaneously. When Descartes fused algebra and geometry in 1637, he was industrializing Menaechmus's insight — and yesterday's lesson, where two functions kept tying, was the setup. Today we pin the ties down exactly.

Example 1

Solve the linear system, twice — once by substitution, once by elimination: \(\;2x + 3y = 12\) and \(x - y = 1\).

Solution:

Substitution. The second equation gives \(x = y + 1\). Substitute into the first:

\(2(y + 1) + 3y = 12\)

\(5y + 2 = 12 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; y = 2, \quad x = y + 1 = 3\)

Elimination. Multiply the second equation by 3 and add, so the \(y\)-terms cancel:

\(2x + 3y = 12\)

\(3x - 3y = 3\)

\(5x \phantom{{}-3y} = 15 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x = 3, \quad y = x - 1 = 2\)

Verify in both originals — always both: \(2(3) + 3(2) = 12\) ✓ and \(3 - 2 = 1\) ✓.

Answer: \((3, 2)\) — the single point where the two lines cross. Two roads to the same point: substitution is universal; elimination is faster when matching terms line up. Keep both in the kit, because nonlinear systems will demand a choice.

Example 2

A line meets a parabola: solve \(\;y = x^2 - 2x - 3\) and \(y = x + 1\).

Solution:

Both right-hand sides equal the same \(y\), so set them equal — substitution in its cleanest form:

\(x^2 - 2x - 3 = x + 1\)

\(x^2 - 3x - 4 = 0\)

\((x - 4)(x + 1) = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x = 4 \;\text{ or }\; x = -1\)

Each \(x\) needs its partner. Use the simpler equation, the line: \(x = 4 \Rightarrow y = 5\); \(x = -1 \Rightarrow y = 0\).

Verify in the parabola: \(16 - 8 - 3 = 5\) ✓ and \(1 + 2 - 3 = 0\) ✓.

Answer: \((4, 5)\) and \((-1, 0)\). Notice what the algebra became: a quadratic — so a line can meet a parabola in at most two points, and the discriminant of that quadratic counts the meetings before you ever find them: positive means two, zero means tangent (one), negative means the line misses entirely.

Example 3

A line meets a circle: solve \(\;x^2 + y^2 = 25\) and \(y = x + 1\).

Solution:

Substitute the line into the circle:

\(x^2 + (x + 1)^2 = 25\)

\(x^2 + x^2 + 2x + 1 = 25\)

\(2x^2 + 2x - 24 = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x^2 + x - 12 = 0\)

\((x + 4)(x - 3) = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x = -4 \;\text{ or }\; x = 3\)

Partners from the line: \(x = -4 \Rightarrow y = -3\); \(x = 3 \Rightarrow y = 4\).

Verify in the circle: \((-4)^2 + (-3)^2 = 16 + 9 = 25\) ✓ and \(3^2 + 4^2 = 9 + 16 = 25\) ✓.

Answer: \((-4, -3)\) and \((3, 4)\) — the line is a secant, cutting the circle twice. And a warning hides here: the pair \((3, -4)\) also satisfies the circle (\(9 + 16 = 25\)) but not the line (\(3 + 1 \neq -4\)). The circle, being generous, accepts impostors; only checking both equations exposes them. Pair each \(x\) with the \(y\) the line assigns it — never with every \(y\) the curve would allow.

Example 4

Curve meets curve, and elimination shines: solve \(\;x^2 + y^2 = 20\) and \(x^2 - y = 8\).

Solution:

Both equations carry the same term \(x^2\) — so subtract the second from the first and it vanishes:

\((x^2 + y^2) - (x^2 - y) = 20 - 8\)

\(y^2 + y = 12 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; y^2 + y - 12 = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; (y + 4)(y - 3) = 0\)

\(y = -4 \;\text{ or }\; y = 3\)

Recover \(x\) from \(x^2 = y + 8\), remembering both square roots:

\(y = -4: \;\; x^2 = 4 \;\Rightarrow\; x = \pm 2 \qquad\qquad y = 3: \;\; x^2 = 11 \;\Rightarrow\; x = \pm\sqrt{11}\)

Verify (one of each pair shown; check all four yourself): \((2, -4)\): \(4 + 16 = 20\) ✓, \(4 - (-4) = 8\) ✓. \((\sqrt{11}, 3)\): \(11 + 9 = 20\) ✓, \(11 - 3 = 8\) ✓.

Answer: Four solutions: \((\pm 2, -4)\) and \((\pm\sqrt{11}, 3)\) — a circle and a parabola embracing at four points. Had a candidate produced \(x^2 = \) a negative number, we would have rejected it: the algebra proposes, the judge disposes. The guard-and-judge discipline from radical week never left; it just changed courtrooms.

Counting Meetings Before Finding Them

The shapes announce the possibilities before the algebra begins — and the honors habit is to predict the count first, so a wrong answer announces itself:

  • Line and line: 0 (parallel), 1 (crossing), or infinitely many (same line).
  • Line and parabola, line and circle: 0, 1 (tangent), or 2 — because substitution produces a quadratic, and a quadratic has at most two real roots. The discriminant is the meeting-counter.
  • Circle and parabola: up to 4 — substitution produces a quartic in disguise (Example 4's quadratic in \(y\), each root splitting into \(\pm x\)).
  • Increasing curve and decreasing curve: exactly 1 — no algebra needed for the count; one rises, one falls, they can cross only once.

Choosing the tool: substitution when one equation hands you a variable (a line always does); elimination when the same nonlinear term appears in both equations. Either way, every candidate pair faces both original equations before it may call itself a solution.

At desmos.com/calculator (or below):

  1. Type Example 3's system exactly as written: x^2 + y^2 = 25 on one line, y = x + 1 on the next. Desmos graphs implicit equations directly — no solving for \(y\) needed. Click the two gray intersection dots: \((-4, -3)\) and \((3, 4)\), just as your algebra proved.
  2. Add Example 4's pair — x^2 + y^2 = 20 and y = x^2 - 8 — and count four meetings. Click the irrational ones and compare \(\pm 3.317\) against your exact \(\pm\sqrt{11}\).
  3. Now the humbling one: solve \(2^x = x + 3\). Type y = 2^x and y = x + 3. Your exact tools — factoring, logarithms — cannot isolate this \(x\); no algebraic form exists. But reasoning still counts the meetings: a line can cut an upward-bending exponential at most twice, and the graphs deliver exactly two: \(x \approx -2.86\) and \(x \approx 2.44\).
  4. Note the division of labor. Algebra tells you how many and why; the calculator finds where, to any precision you like — but only approximately. The calculator confirms; your algebra convinces — and when algebra cannot finish, it still referees.

▶  Try it yourself: Solve \(y = x^2\) and \(y = 2x + 8\), and verify both solutions in both equations. Work it on paper before you peek.
Set equal: \(x^2 = 2x + 8 \Rightarrow x^2 - 2x - 8 = 0 \Rightarrow (x-4)(x+2) = 0\), so \(x = 4\) or \(x = -2\). From the line: \(x = 4 \Rightarrow y = 16\); \(x = -2 \Rightarrow y = 4\). Verify in the parabola: \(4^2 = 16\) ✓, \((-2)^2 = 4\) ✓. Solutions: \((4, 16)\) and \((-2, 4)\).
▶  Try it yourself: Solve \(x^2 + y^2 = 10\) and \(y = 3x\). Predict the number of solutions from the shapes before you compute.
Prediction: a line through the origin must pierce a circle centered at the origin exactly twice. Substitute: \(x^2 + 9x^2 = 10 \Rightarrow 10x^2 = 10 \Rightarrow x = \pm 1\). Partners from the line: \((1, 3)\) and \((-1, -3)\). Verify: \(1 + 9 = 10\) ✓ both times. Two solutions, as the geometry promised.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Two classic system slips, both about partners. First: finding the \(x\)-values and then pairing them with the wrong \(y\)-values — recover each partner from the simplest original equation (usually the line), because the curvier equation happily accepts impostor pairs like \((3, -4)\) in Example 3. Second: solving \(x^2 = 4\) and writing only \(x = 2\) — the moment a square is unwrapped, both roots \(\pm 2\) must appear, or half the intersection points silently vanish from your answer. A system's solution is a complete set of points: every pair present, every pair verified in every equation.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The tool you used today — treating a curve and an equation as the same object — entered the world as an appendix. Descartes attached La Géométrie to the back of his 1637 Discourse on Method, almost as a demonstration piece, and in it every equation became a curve and every curve an equation. Every graph you have ever clicked in Desmos runs on that appendix. Menaechmus found the bridge; Descartes paved it; you now commute across it daily without feeling the river underneath.

Conclusion

A system asks two demands at once, and its solutions are the points loyal to both — the intersections. You now own the full toolkit: substitution when an equation offers up a variable, elimination when a shared term can be made to vanish, the discriminant to count meetings before finding them, the both-equations check that unmasks impostor pairs, and the graphical method for equations — like \(2^x = x + 3\) — that exact algebra honestly cannot finish. The shapes were the story today: lines, parabolas, and circles meeting 0, 1, 2, even 4 ways (and systems will return in earnest when the conic sections arrive in Unit 5). But so far every system has been bare mathematics. Tomorrow the intersections acquire meaning: the point where supply meets demand, where two projectiles pass, where a growing town catches a shrinking one — the closing lesson of the unit, where every function family you have met since August shows up to the same rendezvous.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why is a solution of a system an ordered pair rather than a number — and what does that pair mean in the picture?

2. Substitution turns “where do two curves meet?” into an equation in one variable — a kind you already know how to solve. Why does that collapse work, and what information must you restore afterward?

3. How can you know a line meets a circle zero, one, or two times — from the algebra and from the geometry — before finding any coordinates?

4. For \(2^x = x + 3\), exact algebra fails but the solution count is still provable. What exactly can be known for certain there, what can only be approximated, and which tool certifies which?
