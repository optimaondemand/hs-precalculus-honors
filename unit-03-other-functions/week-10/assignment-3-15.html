📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
This is Week 10's Focus assignment — the one set this week your teacher grades for method, and the dress rehearsal for Thursday's unit test. The two gold-badged ⭐ METHOD PROBLEMS are the unit in miniature: a market equilibrium whose radical supply curve demands the full guard-and-judge discipline inside a word problem, and an exponential catch-up that only a logarithm can unlock, finished with a four-line model defense. Your teacher reads them as arguments — models translated faithfully, tools chosen for the families present, every candidate judged, every answer wearing units in a sentence. Give every problem honest work, and give the method problems the full procedure with nothing skipped because it felt obvious.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
Two models, one moment of agreement. What must be true at an intersection point — in the algebra and in the world it describes — and who has the authority to reject a solution the arithmetic accepts?
📝 PROBLEM SET

Show your work for every problem in neat vertical form — one step per line, equals signs aligned, exact values before decimals, every applied answer stated in a sentence with units.

1
Warm-up equilibrium: A market has demand \(p = 100 - 3q\) and supply \(p = 20 + 5q\) (\(q\) in units per day, \(p\) in dollars). Find the equilibrium quantity and price, verify in both curves, and interpret the point in one sentence: what happens at prices above it, and below it?
2
Break-even: A startup's costs run \(C(x) = 1200 + 4x\) dollars for \(x\) units; revenue is \(R(x) = 10x\). Find the break-even point, verify it, and state in a sentence what each additional unit contributes beyond it.
⭐ METHOD PROBLEM — your teacher grades this one for method
3
A specialty coffee market has supply curve \(p = 2\sqrt{q}\) and demand curve \(p = 24 - q\) (\(q\) in bags per day, \(p\) in dollars per bag). Find the equilibrium with the complete discipline, every step labeled: ① set up — state the equation whose solution is the equilibrium, and why; ② guard — substitute \(u = \sqrt{q}\) with its condition \(u \geq 0\) posted and justified; ③ solve — the quadratic in \(u\), factored, both candidates shown; ④ judge — test each candidate against the guard and confirm the survivor numerically in both original curves; ⑤ verdict sentence — the equilibrium quantity and price, in units; ⑥ the rejected candidate — state why it fails twice over: once by algebra (what a square root can never be) and once by economics (what a negative value would have to mean).
4
Passing in the air: Two firework shells rise over the same field: \(h_1(t) = -16t^2 + 96t\) (ground launch) and \(h_2(t) = -16t^2 + 80t + 32\) (platform launch), heights in feet, \(t\) in seconds. ① Find when and at what height the shells pass each other, and verify in both models. ② Confirm both shells are still airborne at that moment. ③ One sentence: why did two parabolas meet only once — what did elimination do to the shared \(-16t^2\), and what does that term's cancellation say about gravity's role in the difference between the flights?
⭐ METHOD PROBLEM — your teacher grades this one for method
5
Account A holds \$3{,}000 growing at 7% compounded annually; Account B holds \$5{,}000 growing at 3% compounded annually. ① Model both balances as functions of \(t\). ② Set up the catch-up equation and isolate the ratio \(\left(\frac{1.07}{1.03}\right)^t\) on one side, showing the algebra. ③ Unlock the exponent — take a logarithm of both sides, naming the property that lets \(t\) come down. ④ Answer exactly, then numerically: \(t\) as a ratio of logarithms first, then as a decimal (about two decimal places). ⑤ Verify by computing both balances at your \(t\) — they should agree to within rounding — and state the common balance. ⑥ Four-line model defense: a friend with a 20-year horizon asks which account to choose. Answer in exactly four labeled lines — claim (which account, and the crossover time); structure (what in the two formulas drives the outcome); long-run (why the verdict can never reverse after the crossing); domain of honesty (one real-world assumption — constant rates, no deposits or fees — that could bend the conclusion, and how).
6
The one algebra can't finish: Solve \(e^{-x} = x\). ① Argue from shapes that exactly one solution exists (one side falls, the other rises — say it precisely). ② Find it in Desmos to three decimal places. ③ One sentence of honest bookkeeping: state exactly which of your two facts is proved and which is approximated, and by which tool.
7
Explain why: “Every equation you have ever solved was secretly a system.” Defend this claim in three or four sentences: what two graphs does the single equation \(f(x) = g(x)\) hide, what do its solutions become in the picture, and which example from this unit — your choice — shows the disguise most clearly?
8
Honors stretch — a duel with an exact tie: Consider the system \(y = 3^x\) and \(y = x^3\). ① Verify by exact computation that \(x = 3\) is a solution. ② Show a second positive solution exists between \(x = 2\) and \(x = 2.5\) by computing both functions at those inputs and citing the sign change; then locate it to two decimals in Desmos. ③ Argue that no negative solution exists (compare the signs of \(3^x\) and \(x^3\) for \(x < 0\)). ④ One closing sentence: who leads on each of the three intervals the two crossings create, and who wins the long run — with the growth-ratio reason.
