Logarithmic Scales and Models
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to explain why logarithmic scales are the right tool for quantities that span many orders of magnitude.

  • I will be able to compute with the pH, decibel, and Richter definitions in both directions — from quantity to scale reading, and from scale reading back to quantity.

  • I will be able to interpret the parameters of a logarithmic model \(f(x) = a + b\ln x\) in a real context, and justify the interpretation algebraically.

📚 VOCABULARY

Logarithmic scale: A scale on which each step of fixed size represents multiplication by a fixed factor — equal steps stand for equal ratios, not equal amounts.

Order of magnitude: A factor of 10. Two quantities differ by \(n\) orders of magnitude when one is about \(10^n\) times the other.

pH: A measure of acidity, \(\text{pH} = -\log[\text{H}^+]\), where \([\text{H}^+]\) is the hydrogen-ion concentration in moles per liter.

Decibel (dB): A measure of sound level, \(\text{dB} = 10\log\!\left(\dfrac{I}{I_0}\right)\), where \(I\) is the sound's intensity and \(I_0 = 10^{-12}\) W/m² is the faintest intensity a human ear can detect. From Latin decem, ten, plus bel — a tenth of a bel, the unit named for Alexander Graham Bell.

Magnitude (of an earthquake): A logarithmic rating of an earthquake's size: each whole step of magnitude means 10 times the ground-shaking amplitude, and roughly \(10^{1.5} \approx 31.6\) times the energy released.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Here is a strange fact about you: your own senses keep logarithmic books. Nineteenth-century scientists studying perception (Ernst Weber and Gustav Fechner among them) noticed that what feels like one equal step of loudness or brightness is not one equal amount more — it is one equal ratio more. Doubling a sound's intensity feels like roughly the same-sized step whether you start from a whisper or from a shout. Your ear, in other words, hears ratios; it was taking logarithms centuries before Napier invented them. So when scientists needed scales for quantities of absurd range — hydrogen ions in 1909, when the Danish chemist Søren Sørensen defined pH; earthquakes in 1935, when Charles Richter rated California tremors — they built the scales the way the ear was already built. That is today's wonder: one short axis that holds both a rustling leaf and a jet engine, with room to spare.

Why is such an axis needed at all? A quiet conversation carries about \(10^{-6}\) W/m² of sound intensity; a jet engine at close range, about \(10\) W/m². That is a factor of ten million. Plot both on an ordinary axis and the conversation vanishes into the thickness of the ink. The logarithm rescues the plot by recording each quantity's exponent instead of the quantity itself — and exponents of these intensities run politely from about \(-12\) to \(1\).

How Every Logarithmic Scale Works

A logarithmic scale trades multiplication for addition — the same trade Napier's logarithms have made all unit long:

  • Equal steps on the scale = equal ratios in the quantity. Going up 1 pH unit divides \([\text{H}^+]\) by 10; going up 10 dB multiplies intensity by 10; going up 1 magnitude multiplies ground amplitude by 10.
  • To compare two readings, subtract them — then exponentiate. A difference of \(d\) steps means a ratio of \(10^{d}\) (in whatever the scale's step-factor is).
  • Both directions matter. Quantity \(\to\) reading is a logarithm; reading \(\to\) quantity is an exponential. Every scale problem in this lesson is one of these two trips.

The scale adds steps; the world multiplies quantities. Keep that sentence — it is the whole lesson, and the common mistake below is exactly what happens when it is forgotten.

Example 1

(a) Black coffee has a pH of about 5; pure water has a pH of 7. How do their hydrogen-ion concentrations compare? (b) A soft drink has a pH of 3.4. Find its hydrogen-ion concentration.

Solution:

(a) From \(\text{pH} = -\log[\text{H}^+]\), a pH of 5 means \([\text{H}^+] = 10^{-5}\) mol/L, and a pH of 7 means \(10^{-7}\) mol/L. The ratio is

\(\dfrac{10^{-5}}{10^{-7}} = 10^{2} = 100\)

(b) Run the definition backward: \(3.4 = -\log[\text{H}^+]\) means \([\text{H}^+] = 10^{-3.4}\). The calculator gives

\(10^{-3.4} \approx 3.98 \times 10^{-4} \text{ mol/L}\)

Answer: (a) Coffee has \(100\) times the hydrogen-ion concentration of pure water — two pH steps, two factors of 10. (b) About \(3.98 \times 10^{-4}\) mol/L. Notice both directions in one problem: reading \(\to\) quantity is an exponential, comparison of readings is a subtraction that becomes a ratio.

Example 2

(a) A conversation measures about 60 dB and a rock concert about 110 dB. How do the sound intensities compare? (b) Find the decibel level of a sound with intensity \(I = 10^{-5}\) W/m².

Solution:

(a) Each 10 dB is one factor of 10 in intensity. The difference is \(110 - 60 = 50\) dB — five factors of ten:

\(10^{(110-60)/10} = 10^{5} = 100{,}000\)

(b) Apply the definition directly:

\(10\log\!\left(\dfrac{10^{-5}}{10^{-12}}\right) = 10\log\!\left(10^{7}\right) = 10 \cdot 7 = 70 \text{ dB}\)

Answer: (a) The concert is \(100{,}000\) times as intense as the conversation — yet only 50 dB louder on the scale, which is precisely the scale's job. (b) 70 dB. Notice that the log properties from Lesson 3-7 did all the work: \(\log 10^{7} = 7\), no calculator required.

Example 3

An earthquake of magnitude 7.9 strikes; a second quake measures 5.9. Compare the two in ground-shaking amplitude and in energy released.

Solution:

The magnitude difference is \(7.9 - 5.9 = 2\). Each whole magnitude step is 10 times the ground amplitude, so:

amplitude ratio \(= 10^{2} = 100\)

Energy grows faster — each magnitude step multiplies energy by about \(10^{1.5} \approx 31.6\), so:

energy ratio \(\approx 10^{1.5 \times 2} = 10^{3} = 1000\)

Answer: The magnitude-7.9 quake shakes the ground with \(100\) times the amplitude and releases about \(1000\) times the energy of the magnitude-5.9 quake. Two scale steps; three orders of magnitude in energy. This is why "only two points higher" is never a small difference in seismology.

Example 4

A study of new typists models words-per-minute after \(x\) hours of practice (for \(x \geq 1\)) as \(S(x) = 12 + 9.5\ln x\). Interpret the parameters 12 and 9.5, and prove the model's signature claim: every doubling of practice time adds the same fixed gain.

Solution:

At \(x = 1\): \(S(1) = 12 + 9.5\ln 1 = 12 + 0 = 12\), so 12 is the starting level — the speed after the first hour. The coefficient \(9.5\) controls how fast the gains come. But its cleanest meaning appears through a doubling. Compare \(S\) at \(2x\) and at \(x\):

\(S(2x) - S(x) = \big(12 + 9.5\ln 2x\big) - \big(12 + 9.5\ln x\big) = 9.5\big(\ln 2x - \ln x\big) = 9.5\ln\!\dfrac{2x}{x} = 9.5\ln 2\)

The \(x\) cancels completely — the gain from doubling is the same whether you double 1 hour into 2 or 40 hours into 80:

\(9.5 \ln 2 \approx 9.5 \times 0.6931 \approx 6.6\) words per minute

Answer: 12 is the level after one hour of practice; each doubling of practice time adds a constant \(9.5\ln 2 \approx 6.6\) wpm. This is the shape of diminishing returns: equal gains cost ever-doubling effort. Any model \(f(x) = a + b\ln x\) obeys the same law with increment \(b\ln 2\) — the proof above never used the particular numbers.

📊 In Excel: Watch a Chart Fail, Then Fix It with Logarithms 10 min

Open a blank workbook and build the case for log scales with your own hands:

  1. In column A, list five sounds with their approximate intensities in W/m²: rustling leaves 1E-11, whisper 1E-9, conversation 1E-6, rock concert 0.1, jet engine at close range 10. (Type the labels in a neighboring column.)
  2. Chart the raw intensities as a bar chart. Behold the failure: the jet engine's bar swallows the entire chart, and the first three sounds are invisibly flat — a factor of \(10^{12}\) cannot share one linear axis.
  3. Now add a decibel column with =10*LOG10(A2/1E-12) and fill down. You should get exactly 10, 30, 60, 110, 130 — verify the conversation row against Example 2's method.
  4. Chart the dB column. Every sound is now visible, spaced by meaning rather than crushed by magnitude. Same five numbers, two charts: one useless, one eloquent. That contrast is the reason logarithmic scales exist — you have just seen the why, not merely been told it.

⚠️ WHERE AI HELPS — AND WHERE IT DOESN'T
Ask your AI tutor: “Give me three real quantities that each span at least six orders of magnitude — and build me a table placing several examples of each on a logarithmic scale.” This is work AI does brilliantly: it knows more star brightnesses, animal masses, and computer speeds than any of us. But before you trust the table, audit it: pick two rows per quantity and run your own \(10\log(I/I_0)\)-style computation, exactly as in Examples 1–3. A fluent, well-formatted table can hide a slipped exponent — and on a log scale a slipped exponent is not a small typo but a factor of ten. The AI supplies breadth; you supply verification. That division of labor is the habit this course is building.
▶  Try it yourself: Lemon juice has a pH of about 2; milk has a pH of about 6. How many times more concentrated are the hydrogen ions in lemon juice? Work it on paper before you peek.
The pH difference is \(6 - 2 = 4\) steps, and each step is a factor of 10 in concentration: \(\dfrac{10^{-2}}{10^{-6}} = 10^{4} = 10{,}000\). Lemon juice carries ten thousand times the hydrogen-ion concentration of milk — four steps on the scale, four orders of magnitude in the chemistry.
▶  Try it yourself: City traffic produces a sound intensity of about \(10^{-4}\) W/m². Find its decibel level — then say how many times more intense it is than a 50 dB refrigerator hum.
\(10\log\!\left(\dfrac{10^{-4}}{10^{-12}}\right) = 10\log 10^{8} = 80\) dB. Against the 50 dB hum, the difference is 30 dB — three factors of ten — so traffic is \(10^{3} = 1000\) times as intense.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Reading a difference of 2 pH units as “twice as acidic.” It is not twice — it is \(10^2 = 100\) times, as Example 1 showed. The error comes from treating a logarithmic scale as if it were linear: on a log scale, steps add, but quantities multiply. A difference of \(d\) steps always means a ratio of \(10^d\) (times the scale's step size), never an amount of \(d\). When in doubt, translate the readings back to raw quantities before comparing — the definition is always the safe road home.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The oldest logarithmic scale in science predates logarithms by seventeen centuries. The Greek astronomer Hipparchus sorted stars into six “magnitudes” of brightness by eye — and because the eye judges ratios, his equal-feeling steps turned out to be equal factors. When astronomers formalized the scale in the 1850s, they fixed it so that 5 magnitude steps equal a brightness ratio of exactly 100. Hipparchus was measuring logarithmically without knowing it; his instrument — the human retina — was logarithmic all along.

Conclusion

Today the logarithm left the classroom and measured the world: acid in a glass, sound in a stadium, the shudder of the earth itself — each tamed by a scale on which equal steps mean equal ratios, and a whisper and a jet engine fit on one axis. You computed in both directions, and you proved the logarithmic model's signature: doubling the input always buys the same increment, \(b\ln 2\). ❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. What exactly does one step on a logarithmic scale mean, and why does that make such scales the right tool for quantities spanning many orders of magnitude?

2. Every scale problem is a trip in one of two directions — quantity to reading, or reading to quantity. Which function makes each trip, and how are the two trips related?

3. Why does a difference of \(d\) units on pH, decibel, or magnitude scales correspond to a ratio rather than an amount — and what goes wrong when people forget this?

4. What does the parameter \(b\) in \(f(x) = a + b\ln x\) tell you about a real situation, and why is “each doubling adds \(b\ln 2\)” the model's honest signature?
