Modeling with Piecewise Functions
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to build a piecewise model from a verbal policy description — tax brackets, shipping tiers, service plans.

  • I will be able to interpret breakpoints, continuity, and jumps in context, and verify continuity at a seam by computing both sides.

  • I will be able to distinguish a marginal rate from an effective (average) rate, and settle a policy argument with the distinction.

📚 VOCABULARY

Breakpoint: An input where a piecewise model changes rules — the top of a tax bracket, the edge of a shipping tier. The interesting behavior of a policy lives entirely at its breakpoints.

Marginal rate: The rate charged on the next dollar (or ounce, or minute) — the slope of the piece you are currently standing on.

Effective (average) rate: Total charge divided by total amount, \(T(x)/x\) — what you actually paid, per dollar, over everything.

Continuity (informal): No gap at the seam — the two pieces meeting at a breakpoint agree on its value, so the graph can be drawn across it without lifting the pencil.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Yesterday's piecewise functions were exercises; today's run countries. A progressive income tax — higher rates on higher portions of income — is nothing more or less than a piecewise-linear function, and entire elections are fought over where its breakpoints sit and how steep its pieces climb. Strangely, the most common civic math error in public life is not about the rates at all: it is a misreading of the function's shape — the widespread belief that crossing into a higher bracket can shrink your take-home pay. Today you will build the function, compute both sides of every seam, and retire that myth with arithmetic. To keep every claim honest, we will work inside an invented model republic with a deliberately simple tax code — the mathematics is exactly that of real codes, but no real country's figures are asserted here.

The model republic taxes annual income like this: 10% on income up to \$20,000; 20% on the portion above \$20,000 up to \$50,000; 30% on the portion above \$50,000. The phrase to underline twice is on the portion — it is the whole lesson.

Example 1

Build the tax function \(T(x)\) for the model republic as a piecewise function of income \(x\).

Solution:

On the first bracket, tax is simply 10%: \(T(x) = 0.10x\). By the top of that bracket the taxpayer owes \(0.10 \times 20{,}000 = 2000\).

On the second bracket, the first \$20,000 has already been taxed — \$2,000, settled and final — and only the portion above it pays 20%: \(T(x) = 2000 + 0.20(x - 20{,}000)\). By the top of this bracket the bill is \(2000 + 0.20 \times 30{,}000 = 2000 + 6000 = 8000\).

On the third bracket, the first \$50,000 owes its settled \$8,000, and the portion above pays 30%:

\(T(x) = \begin{cases} 0.10x & \text{if } 0 \leq x \leq 20{,}000 \\ 2000 + 0.20(x - 20{,}000) & \text{if } 20{,}000 < x \leq 50{,}000 \\ 8000 + 0.30(x - 50{,}000) & \text{if } x > 50{,}000 \end{cases}\)

Answer: As boxed. Check the seams before trusting it: at \(x = 20{,}000\), piece one gives \(2000\) and piece two gives \(2000 + 0.20(0) = 2000\) ✓; at \(x = 50{,}000\), piece two gives \(2000 + 6000 = 8000\) and piece three gives \(8000 + 0.30(0) = 8000\) ✓. The constants 2000 and 8000 are not decoration — they are the sewn seams, each one the accumulated tax of all lower brackets.

Example 2

Compute the tax on an income of \$80,000.

Solution:

\(80{,}000 > 50{,}000\), so the third law governs:

\(T(80{,}000) = 8000 + 0.30(80{,}000 - 50{,}000) = 8000 + 0.30 \times 30{,}000 = 8000 + 9000 = 17{,}000\)

Answer: \$17,000. Read the anatomy of the bill: \$2,000 from the first bracket, \$6,000 from the second, \$9,000 from the third — three different rates, each applied only to its own portion. At no point was the whole \$80,000 taxed at 30%.

Example 3

A worker in the model republic earning \$49,000 fears a raise to \$51,000: “It pushes me into the 30% bracket — I'll take home less!” Settle the matter: show \(T\) is continuous at its breakpoints, then compare the worker's marginal and effective rates at \$80,000.

Solution:

Continuity, computed at both seams (from Example 1): both pieces give \(2000\) at \(x = 20{,}000\), and both give \(8000\) at \(x = 50{,}000\). No gap, no cliff: an extra dollar of income can never cause a jump in tax, because only that dollar gets the new rate.

The raise, directly: \(T(49{,}000) = 2000 + 0.20 \times 29{,}000 = 7800\), leaving take-home \(49{,}000 - 7800 = 41{,}200\). And \(T(51{,}000) = 8000 + 0.30 \times 1000 = 8300\), leaving \(51{,}000 - 8300 = 42{,}700\). The raise adds \$1,500 of take-home pay. Only the \$1,000 above the breakpoint met the 30% rate.

Marginal versus effective at \$80,000: the marginal rate — the slope of the current piece — is 30%: the next dollar earned loses 30 cents. But the effective rate is

\(\dfrac{T(80{,}000)}{80{,}000} = \dfrac{17{,}000}{80{,}000} = 0.2125 = 21.25\%\)

Answer: The fear is refuted by continuity: a raise always raises take-home pay in a portion-based code. And one taxpayer honestly wears two rates at once — a 30% marginal rate on the next dollar, a 21.25% effective rate over all dollars. Most public arguments about “what people pay” are two sides quoting these two different numbers at each other.

Seams Sewn Shut, Seams Left Open — a Modeler's Choice

A breakpoint can be built two ways, and each is sometimes the right policy:

  • Continuous at the seam (the tax code): the “portion above” language forces the pieces to agree at every breakpoint. Use this when a hair's difference of input must never cause a leap in output — fairness demands it.
  • Jump at the seam (the shipping tier, yesterday's parking garage): one extra ounce, whole new price. The output leaps at the breakpoint by design — simplicity and coarse measurement demand it; nobody weighs postage to the milligram.

When you build a piecewise model, the seams are a decision, not an accident: ask of every breakpoint, “should a tiny step across this line cost a tiny amount — or a leap?” Then verify your intent the only way that counts: compute both pieces at the seam and compare.

Example 4

A shipping service charges \$6 for parcels up to 1 lb, \$11 for parcels over 1 lb up to 3 lb, and \$19 for parcels over 3 lb up to 8 lb. Write the model, and contrast its seams with the tax code's.

Solution:

\(S(w) = \begin{cases} 6 & \text{if } 0 < w \leq 1 \\ 11 & \text{if } 1 < w \leq 3 \\ 19 & \text{if } 3 < w \leq 8 \end{cases}\)

Test the seam at \(w = 1\): the first piece gives \(S(1) = 6\), while just across the line \(S(1.01) = 11\). The pieces do not agree — the model jumps by \$5 at one pound, and by \$8 at three pounds.

Answer: As boxed: a genuine step function, discontinuous at both breakpoints — one extra ounce, whole new price. The tax code must not jump (a jump would mean one earned dollar costing five in tax — the cliff the “portion above” language exists to prevent); the shipping tier wants its jump (three prices are printable on a sign; a continuous ramp is not). Same mathematics, opposite seams, both correct — because the seam is a policy choice.

📊 In Excel: The Tax Code as a Nested IF 12 min

Build the model republic in a workbook and interrogate it:

  1. Column A: incomes from 0 to 100,000 in steps of 1,000. In B2, the whole tax code in one formula: =IF(A2<=20000, 0.1*A2, IF(A2<=50000, 2000+0.2*(A2-20000), 8000+0.3*(A2-50000))). Fill down. The nested IFs are the piecewise braces, machine-readable.
  2. Interrogate the seams: add rows for incomes 19,999 / 20,001 and 49,999 / 50,001. The tax amounts differ by fractions of a dollar across each seam — continuity, witnessed numerically.
  3. Chart column B against A: three straight segments, steepening at each breakpoint — a graph that turns up its slope at every seam but never leaps. You are looking at the shape of “portion above.”
  4. Add an effective-rate column, =B2/A2, and chart it: watch it climb from 10% toward — but never reaching — 30%. The effective rate chases the top marginal rate forever without catching it, because the cheap early brackets are always in the average. An asymptote, hiding in a tax code.

Keep the workbook — the honors stretch on tonight's set asks you to design a bracket schedule of your own, and this sheet is the fastest way to audit your design.

▶  Try it yourself: In the model republic, compute \(T(35{,}000)\) and the effective rate at that income. Work it on paper before you peek.
\(35{,}000\) lies in the second bracket: \(T(35{,}000) = 2000 + 0.20(35{,}000 - 20{,}000) = 2000 + 3000 = 5000\). Effective rate: \(5000/35{,}000 \approx 14.3\%\) — below the 20% marginal rate, because the first \$20,000 rode cheaply at 10%.
▶  Try it yourself: At what income does the model republic's effective rate first reach 15%?
The effective rate is 10% throughout the first bracket, so look in the second: solve \(\dfrac{2000 + 0.20(x - 20{,}000)}{x} = 0.15\). Then \(2000 + 0.2x - 4000 = 0.15x\), so \(0.05x = 2000\) and \(x = 40{,}000\). Check: \(T(40{,}000) = 2000 + 4000 = 6000\), and \(6000/40{,}000 = 15\%\) ✓.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Applying the top rate to the entire income — the cliff myth. “\$80,000 is in the 30% bracket, so the tax is \(0.30 \times 80{,}000 = 24{,}000\).” The true bill, from Example 2, is \$17,000 — the myth overcharges by \$7,000, because brackets tax portions, not wholes. Every dollar is taxed at the rate of the bracket it lives in, and the dollars below the breakpoint never hear about the rates above them. When you see a bracket table, read each rate as a slope over its own interval, never as a label stamped on the whole income.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. What does the phrase “on the portion above” do to a piecewise model's seams, and why does it make bracket cliffs mathematically impossible?

2. How do you verify continuity at a breakpoint, rather than assume it — and what single computation settles the “raise pushed me into a higher bracket” myth?

3. One taxpayer, two honest rates: what different questions do the marginal rate and the effective rate answer, and why can they never be equal above the first bracket?

4. When should a piecewise model's seam jump and when must it not — and why is that a policy decision rather than a mathematical one?
