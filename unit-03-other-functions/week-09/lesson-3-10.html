Radical Functions: Graphs and Equations
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to graph square-root and cube-root functions with transformations, stating the domain and range of each.

  • I will be able to solve radical equations with the full guard-and-judge discipline: post the domain guards first, solve second, judge every candidate.

  • I will be able to explain precisely where extraneous solutions come from — not as bad luck, but as the predictable side effect of an irreversible operation.

📚 VOCABULARY

Radical function: A function whose rule contains a variable under a radical sign, such as \(f(x) = \sqrt{x-2}+1\) or \(g(x) = \sqrt[3]{x}\).

Index: The small number naming which root is taken — 2 (usually unwritten) for square roots, 3 for cube roots. Even and odd indices behave as differently as even and odd powers did in Unit 2.

Principal square root: The nonnegative root. \(\sqrt{9}\) is 3 and only 3; the symbol \(\sqrt{\phantom{x}}\) never outputs a negative number. This one convention drives everything in today's lesson.

Extraneous solution: A candidate produced by correct algebra that fails the original equation — from Latin extraneus, “from outside”: an outsider that slipped in when the equation was transformed.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

The symbol at the center of today's lesson has a birthday. In 1525, the German mathematician Christoff Rudolff published Coss, the first German algebra book, and in it drew a strange new mark: √. Many historians read it as a deformed letter r, for the Latin radix — “root,” the same word that gives us the vegetable radish and the adjective radical. A number's square root really was pictured as its root: the thing buried underneath from which it grew, since a square of area 9 grows from a side of 3. Today we study the functions that live under that five-century-old sign — and the strange ghosts that appear when we try to dig the roots up carelessly.

Start with the parent \(f(x) = \sqrt{x}\). Because the principal square root is only defined for nonnegative inputs and only produces nonnegative outputs, the graph is a half-parabola lying on its side, launching from \((0,0)\) through \((1,1)\), \((4,2)\), and \((9,3)\) — domain \([0, \infty)\), range \([0, \infty)\). Every transformation rule you have used since Algebra 2 applies unchanged, and the domain and range travel with the graph.

Example 1

Graph \(f(x) = \sqrt{x-2} + 1\), and state its domain and range.

Solution:

This is the parent shifted right 2 and up 1. The launch point \((0,0)\) moves to \((2,1)\), and the anchor points move with it. Choose inputs that make the radicand a perfect square:

\(f(2) = \sqrt{0} + 1 = 1, \qquad f(3) = \sqrt{1} + 1 = 2, \qquad f(6) = \sqrt{4} + 1 = 3\)

Plot \((2,1)\), \((3,2)\), \((6,3)\) and draw the rising half-curve through them, steep at first and ever-flattening.

Answer: Domain \([2, \infty)\) — the radicand \(x - 2\) must be \(\geq 0\); range \([1, \infty)\) — the root's nonnegative outputs, lifted by 1. The domain restriction is not decoration: it is a guard posted by the even index, and it will matter enormously when we solve equations.

Example 2

Graph the cube-root parent \(g(x) = \sqrt[3]{x}\), state its domain and range, and explain why it needs no guard at all.

Solution:

Anchor points: \(g(-8) = -2\), \(g(-1) = -1\), \(g(0) = 0\), \(g(1) = 1\), \(g(8) = 2\). The curve sweeps through the origin from lower left to upper right — both directions, forever.

Why no restriction? Because cubing preserves sign: a negative number has a perfectly good real cube root (\((-2)^3 = -8\), so \(\sqrt[3]{-8} = -2\)), where no real number squares to a negative. The even index forbids; the odd index permits.

Answer: Domain: all real numbers; range: all real numbers. No guard is needed — and, as we will see, no judging step either when cube-root equations are solved. Even and odd indices split the radical family exactly as even and odd multiplicities split polynomial behavior in Unit 2: the parity of the exponent is destiny.

Example 3

Solve \(\sqrt{x+5} = x - 1\) with the full guard-and-judge discipline.

Solution:

Guard first. Two conditions must hold before any algebra: the radicand needs \(x + 5 \geq 0\), so \(x \geq -5\); and since a principal square root is never negative, the right side must satisfy \(x - 1 \geq 0\), so \(x \geq 1\). The guards together: any solution must have \(x \geq 1\). Posted.

Solve second. Square both sides:

\(x + 5 = (x-1)^2 = x^2 - 2x + 1\)

\(x^2 - 3x - 4 = 0\)

\((x - 4)(x + 1) = 0\)

\(x = 4 \;\; \text{or} \;\; x = -1\)

Judge every candidate. Substitute each into the original equation:

\(x = 4\):   \(\sqrt{4+5} = \sqrt{9} = 3\) and \(4 - 1 = 3\) ✓

\(x = -1\):   \(\sqrt{-1+5} = \sqrt{4} = 2\) but \(-1 - 1 = -2\) ✗

Answer: \(x = 4\); the candidate \(x = -1\) is extraneous. Notice the guard saw it coming: \(-1 < 1\) violated the posted condition before we ever substituted. The candidate satisfies not our equation but its dark twin, \(\sqrt{x+5} = -(x-1)\) — check: \(\sqrt{4} = 2\) and \(-(-1-1) = 2\) ✓. Where that twin came from is the conceptual heart of the lesson, just below.

Where Ghost Solutions Come From

Squaring is not reversible. The squaring map sends both \(y\) and \(-y\) to the same number \(y^2\) — two different inputs, one output — so information is destroyed the moment you square. Consequence: the squared equation \(A^2 = B^2\) is true whenever \(A = B\) or \(A = -B\). It is a bigger equation, satisfied by more inputs than the original.

  • When you solve the squared equation, your algebra faithfully recovers everyone who satisfies it — including candidates who only ever satisfied the reflected equation \(A = -B\). Those outsiders are the extraneous solutions: extraneus, from outside.
  • This is not a new moral. In Unit 2, clearing denominators could enlarge a rational equation's solution set, and the honest solver kept the original as judge. Different operation — multiplying by a vanishing factor there, squaring here — same moral: a transformation of an equation can enlarge its solution set, so the original equation remains the only court that counts.

Hence the discipline, unchanged from Unit 2: post the guards first, solve second, judge every candidate against the original. The ghosts are not accidents; they are the predictable exhaust of an irreversible step — which is why the judging step is method, not paranoia.

Example 4

Solve \(\sqrt{2x+3} - \sqrt{x-2} = 2\).

Solution:

Guard: \(2x + 3 \geq 0\) gives \(x \geq -\frac{3}{2}\), and \(x - 2 \geq 0\) gives \(x \geq 2\). Combined guard: \(x \geq 2\).

Solve: Isolate one radical before squaring — \(\sqrt{2x+3} = 2 + \sqrt{x-2}\) — then square:

\(2x + 3 = 4 + 4\sqrt{x-2} + (x - 2)\)

\(2x + 3 = x + 2 + 4\sqrt{x-2}\)

\(x + 1 = 4\sqrt{x-2}\)

One radical remains; isolate held, square again:

\(x^2 + 2x + 1 = 16(x - 2) = 16x - 32\)

\(x^2 - 14x + 33 = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; (x - 11)(x - 3) = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x = 11 \;\text{or}\; x = 3\)

Judge:

\(x = 11\):   \(\sqrt{25} - \sqrt{9} = 5 - 3 = 2\) ✓

\(x = 3\):   \(\sqrt{9} - \sqrt{1} = 3 - 1 = 2\) ✓

Answer: \(x = 11\) and \(x = 3\) — both genuine. Two squarings and not one ghost: the judging step acquits as well as convicts. You run the trial not because every candidate is guilty, but because you cannot know which are until the evidence is heard.

💻 In Desmos: Watch the Ghost Being Born 8 min

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), put Example 3 under the lights:

  1. Graph y = sqrt(x+5) and y = x - 1. The half-curve and the line cross exactly once — click the intersection: \(x = 4\). The original equation has one solution, and here it is.
  2. Now graph the squared version: y = x + 5 and y = (x-1)^2. A line and a full parabola — and they cross twice, at \(x = 4\) and \(x = -1\).
  3. Look at where the second intersection lives: at \(x = -1\), the parabola's left branch — the branch that squaring created by folding negative values of \(x - 1\) upward. The phantom intersection appears at exactly the moment you square. You have just watched a ghost being born.
  4. The two pictures are the whole theory in pixels: squaring turned a one-solution equation into a two-solution equation, and only the original graph knows which solution is real. The calculator confirms; your algebra convinces.

⚠️ AI CHECKPOINT: THE GHOST IT KEPT

A student asked an AI to solve \(\sqrt{3x+7} = x + 1\). It answered:

“Square both sides: 3x + 7 = x² + 2x + 1. Rearrange: x² − x − 6 = 0, which factors as (x − 3)(x + 2) = 0. The solutions are x = 3 and x = −2.”

Audit it yourself before reading on. The algebra is flawless — every line follows from the last. Now judge the candidates: \(x = 3\) gives \(\sqrt{16} = 4\) and \(3 + 1 = 4\) ✓. But \(x = -2\) gives \(\sqrt{1} = 1\) while \(-2 + 1 = -1\) ✗. The machine kept a ghost: \(x = -2\) satisfies only the reflected equation \(\sqrt{3x+7} = -(x+1)\), and a guard posted at the start (\(x + 1 \geq 0\), so \(x \geq -1\)) would have flagged it instantly.

Name the failure precisely, because you have seen it before: this is the same pattern as Unit 2's rational-equation checkpoint — fluent execution of the middle of the method, with the guard and the judgment silently omitted. The middle is where AI shines; the edges — the parts that exist to catch the middle's side effects — are where a mathematician must supervise. On this week's Focus set you will audit a fresh transcript of exactly this kind and write the examiner's report yourself.

▶  Try it yourself: Solve \(\sqrt{x+7} = x + 1\) with the full discipline — guards, solution, judgment. Work it on paper before you peek.
Guard: \(x + 7 \geq 0\) and \(x + 1 \geq 0\), so \(x \geq -1\). Square: \(x + 7 = x^2 + 2x + 1\), giving \(x^2 + x - 6 = 0\), so \((x+3)(x-2) = 0\): candidates \(x = 2\) and \(x = -3\). Judge: \(x = 2\): \(\sqrt{9} = 3 = 2 + 1\) ✓. \(x = -3\): \(\sqrt{4} = 2\) but \(-3 + 1 = -2\) ✗ — extraneous (and the guard \(x \geq -1\) had already barred it). Solution: \(x = 2\).
▶  Try it yourself: Graph \(h(x) = \sqrt[3]{x+1} - 2\) with three anchor points, and state its domain and range.
The cube-root parent shifted left 1 and down 2. Anchors: \(h(-1) = \sqrt[3]{0} - 2 = -2\); \(h(0) = \sqrt[3]{1} - 2 = -1\); \(h(-9) = \sqrt[3]{-8} - 2 = -4\); \(h(7) = \sqrt[3]{8} - 2 = 0\). Domain: all reals; range: all reals — the odd index posts no guards, so the shifts change the center point \((-1, -2)\) but restrict nothing.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Squaring term by term across a sum: writing \((\sqrt{a} + b)^2 = a + b^2\). It is not — the binomial square has three terms, \((\sqrt{a} + b)^2 = a + 2b\sqrt{a} + b^2\), and the cross term is the whole story: it is precisely the piece that carries the remaining radical in a two-radical problem like Example 4. Drop it and the second squaring you needed never happens — the "answer" that follows is fiction. Squaring applies to sides of an equation, whole and entire, never to terms one at a time.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Rudolff's 1525 radical sign was bare — just the hook, √, with no bar across the top. The horizontal bar (the vinculum, Latin for “bond” or “chain”) was joined to it by René Descartes in his Géométrie of 1637, so that the sign could embrace exactly the expression being rooted. The symbol you write tonight is a collaboration across 112 years — a German cossist's hook wearing a French philosopher's chain.

Conclusion

Radical functions brought the course a new kind of caution: the even index posts a guard at the door of its domain, the odd index needs none, and the difference is nothing but the parity of a power — the same even/odd logic that has organized graphs since Unit 2. Solving radical equations demanded the full discipline — guard, solve, judge — because squaring is irreversible: it folds the number line, merges an equation with its reflected twin, and lets outsiders through. You watched the ghost born in Desmos, caught an AI keeping one, and saw the judging step acquit two honest candidates. This is the material of this week's Focus assignment — the one set your teacher grades for method — so bring the whole discipline, in order, with nothing skipped because it felt obvious. Tomorrow, a different kind of assembly: functions built from pieces, each rule governing its own territory, with all the interesting arguments happening at the borders.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does an even index restrict a radical function's domain and range while an odd index restricts nothing — and what single algebraic fact explains both?

2. Squaring both sides is legal algebra, yet it manufactures solutions from nowhere. What exactly does squaring destroy, and which equation do the ghost candidates actually satisfy?

3. How is the guard-and-judge discipline for radical equations the same moral as Unit 2's rule for cleared denominators — and why must the original equation always be the judge?

4. In Example 4 both candidates survived judgment. If ghosts do not always appear, why is the judging step still non-negotiable method rather than optional caution?
