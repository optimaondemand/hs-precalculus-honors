📋 THE SCENARIO

For three weeks you have met growth and decay on the page — chessboards, compounding ladders, half-lives with tidy numbers. This task hands you the real record. You will choose one genuine dataset, put it in evidence in Excel, fit exponential (and, where apt, logarithmic) models to it, interrogate the model with Week 8’s tools, and then defend your model choice in writing — with an AI critic looking over your shoulder, and you looking over its shoulder.

Choose one dataset (or propose your own to your teacher first): ① world or national population across several decades; ② a radioactive-decay dataset (activity readings over time); ③ Moore’s law — transistor counts of landmark chips by year; ④ caffeine leaving the body (half-life roughly 5 hours — an approximation; people vary); or ⑤ the cooling of a hot drink, which you may collect yourself with a mug, a kitchen thermometer, and a timer — the only option where the data comes from your own kitchen. Whichever you pick: real numbers, from a real source you name. Napier spent twenty years building tables from real astronomy; you can spend one week being honest with eight data points.

🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
The world handed you a table of numbers, and you are about to claim a function is hiding inside it. What evidence earns that claim — and where, exactly, does your model stop telling the truth?
📝 THE TASK

Part 1 — Get the data on record. On a sheet named Data, enter at least 8 data points (time or year in one column, quantity in the next), and record your source in a labeled cell at the top — the publication, website, or “my own kitchen, measured with a digital thermometer, 2-minute intervals.” Real data only: no invented numbers, no smoothing, no trimming the points that spoil the pattern. If you collected the cooling data yourself, the wobbles are the honest record — leave them in.

Part 2 — Fit the models, twice. On a sheet named Model, make a scatter chart of your data and add an exponential trendline, displaying the equation and \(R^2\). Then fit a second model by hand: choose two well-separated data points and solve for \(a\) and \(b\) in \(y = ab^x\) on paper, exactly as in Lesson 3-1 — then type the derivation into a labeled cell block and plot your hand model beside the trendline. Compare the two in labeled cells: how close are the coefficients, and which data points pull them apart? If your dataset flattens rather than explodes (cooling is the prime suspect — a drink approaches room temperature and stops), also add a logarithmic trendline, compare the two \(R^2\) values, and say which family tells the truer story and how you can tell from the shape, not just the score.

Part 3 — Interrogate the model. On a sheet named Answers, in full sentences citing your own cells: state the growth or decay factor and translate it into a percent-per-time-step statement a non-mathematician could read; compute the doubling time or half-life exactly with logarithms (Week 8’s tools — show the \(\ln\) computation, not a slider estimate); make one prediction beyond your data with the model, and then state its domain of honesty: the interval where you would defend the prediction, with a reason for each endpoint. Population cannot compound forever; transistor counts meet the size of atoms; the drink will never cool below the room. Name your model’s wall.

Part 4 — The AI round. Write a short paragraph (4–6 sentences) on the Answers sheet defending your model choice from the evidence. Paste that paragraph to your AI tutor and ask it to critique your argument. Then write the final entry: a critique of the critique — what the AI got right, what it missed or confidently invented, and your final verdict, signed with your name. You have spent three weeks catching machines skipping the judgment step; this is the judgment step, performed on your own work and on your critic in the same breath.

Honors stretch (optional): Linearize. Add a column computing \(\log(y)\) for your data and chart it against \(x\): genuinely exponential data straightens into a line. Explain in two or three sentences why — taking the log of \(y = ab^x\) gives \(\log y = \log a + x\log b\), a line with slope \(\log b\) — and read your growth factor off the slope as a cross-check. This semi-log trick is how scientists have spotted exponentials in the wild for a century, and it is the product and power rules doing the work.

📊 HOW THIS IS SCORED

Completion-scored (10 pts): full credit for a working, formula-driven workbook built on real, sourced data, meeting the success criteria. A worked model workbook is released after the due date — your dataset will rightly differ from everyone’s, but your Model sheet’s structure, your logarithmic computations, and your reasoning should hold up beside it. The four-criterion standard (understanding, reasoning, presentation, honesty) applies to your written answers exactly as it does on paper — and on this task, honesty includes the source cell, the unedited data, and conceding the AI’s good points.
