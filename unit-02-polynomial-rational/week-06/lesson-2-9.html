Solving Rational Equations and Inequalities
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to solve rational equations by clearing denominators, and reject extraneous solutions with stated reasons.

  • I will be able to explain where extraneous solutions come from — what the clearing step does that creates them.

  • I will be able to solve rational inequalities with a sign chart, treating zeros and forbidden inputs as different kinds of boundary points.

📚 VOCABULARY

Rational equation: An equation containing at least one rational expression.

LCD (least common denominator): The smallest expression every denominator in the equation divides into; multiplying through by it clears all fractions.

Extraneous solution: A value produced by legitimate algebra that fails the original equation — typically by landing on a forbidden input.

Boundary point: For a rational inequality, any zero of the numerator or the denominator — the only places the expression can change sign.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

The word algebra comes from the title of al-Khwarizmi's ninth-century treatise: al-jabr, “the restoration” — the art of moving a term across the equals sign and keeping the equation true. Every equation-solving move you have ever made is licensed by that one principle: do the same thing to both sides, and truth is preserved. Today you meet the fine print. One of the standard moves — multiplying both sides by an expression containing \(x\) — preserves truth almost everywhere, and the “almost” manufactures counterfeit solutions that look exactly like real ones. Learning to mint no counterfeits, and to catch every one that slips through, is today's craft. It is also, not incidentally, a place where AI assistants fail constantly — and you will catch one doing it.

The method first, on an honest example.

Example 1

Solve \(\dfrac{x}{x-3} = 2 - \dfrac{3}{x-3}\).

Solution:

Before anything else, post the guard: the denominators forbid \(x = 3\). Write that down first — it is the standard against which every candidate will be judged.

Multiply both sides by the LCD, \((x - 3)\):

\(x = 2(x - 3) - 3\)

\(x = 2x - 6 - 3\)

\(x = 2x - 9\)

\(x = 9\)

Judge the candidate: \(9 \neq 3\), so it is not forbidden. Verify in the original:

Left: \(\dfrac{9}{6} = \dfrac{3}{2}\).   Right: \(2 - \dfrac{3}{6} = 2 - \dfrac{1}{2} = \dfrac{3}{2}\) ✓

Answer: \(x = 9\).

Now the counterfeit. Watch every step below — each one is legal algebra — and watch what comes out.

Example 2

Solve \(\dfrac{1}{x-2} + \dfrac{1}{x+2} = \dfrac{4}{x^2 - 4}\).

Solution:

Post the guard: \(x^2 - 4 = (x-2)(x+2)\), so \(x = \pm 2\) are forbidden.

Multiply through by the LCD, \((x-2)(x+2)\):

\((x + 2) + (x - 2) = 4\)

\(2x = 4\)

\(x = 2\)

Judge the candidate: \(x = 2\) is on the forbidden list. Substituted into the original equation, it demands division by zero on both sides — it is not a solution of anything. It is extraneous, and there is no other candidate.

Answer: No solution. And that verdict is the honest one: this equation asks for a number that makes two sides equal, and no number on earth does. “No solution,” with the extraneous candidate shown and rejected in writing, is a complete and correct answer — a blank or an unexamined “\(x = 2\)” are the failures.

Where Extraneous Solutions Come From

Multiplying both sides by \((x-2)(x+2)\) is multiplication by a number only when that expression is not zero. At \(x = 2\), you multiplied both sides by zero — and multiplying by zero is the one move that manufactures truth: \(\text{false} \times 0 = 0 = \text{true} \times 0\). The cleared equation is therefore more generous than the original: it keeps every real solution and may add solutions exactly at the LCD's zeros — nowhere else.

Hence the discipline, every time: ① post the forbidden list before clearing; ② clear and solve; ③ judge every candidate against the list and verify survivors in the original. The check is not paranoia; it is the mathematics of what the clearing step actually did.

Example 3

Solve \(x + \dfrac{6}{x} = 5\).

Solution:

Guard: \(x \neq 0\). Multiply through by \(x\):

\(x^2 + 6 = 5x\)

\(x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0\)

\((x - 2)(x - 3) = 0\)

\(x = 2 \;\; \text{or} \;\; x = 3\)

Neither is forbidden. Verify both: \(2 + \frac{6}{2} = 5\) ✓ and \(3 + \frac{6}{3} = 5\) ✓.

Answer: \(x = 2\) or \(x = 3\). Clearing a rational equation often raises the degree — here to a quadratic — so expect multiple candidates, and judge each one separately.

⚠️ AI CHECKPOINT: THE CONFIDENT COUNTERFEIT

A student asked an AI to solve \(\dfrac{3}{x-4} + 2 = \dfrac{x-1}{x-4}\). It answered:

“Multiply both sides by (x − 4) to clear the fractions: 3 + 2(x − 4) = x − 1. Simplify: 3 + 2x − 8 = x − 1, so 2x − 5 = x − 1, giving x = 4. The solution is x = 4.”

The algebra is flawless — every line follows from the last. And the answer is worthless: \(x = 4\) zeroes every denominator in the problem. The AI performed step ② of the discipline perfectly and skipped steps ① and ③ entirely — it never posted the forbidden list and never judged its candidate. Substituting \(x = 4\) back (as the AI did not) asks both sides to divide by zero; the equation in fact has no solution.

Note precisely what failed: not computation, but procedure — the machine executed the middle of the method and omitted the parts that exist to catch the middle's side effects. This is the AI failure pattern of the whole unit: fluent execution, missing discipline. On tonight's problem set you will audit a full AI solution of this kind and write the examiner's report yourself.

Now inequalities — where the same forbidden inputs demand even more respect. The temptation is to clear denominators exactly as we just did. Resist it: multiplying an inequality by \((x - c)\) flips the inequality sign when the factor is negative and keeps it when positive — and \((x - c)\) is sometimes each. There is a cleaner road, and it is one you already know: get zero on one side, and read signs from a chart — exactly as in Lesson 2-8.

Key Fact: Solving a Rational Inequality

  1. Rearrange to compare with zero: move everything to one side and combine into a single fraction. (Never multiply both sides by anything containing \(x\).)
  2. Find the boundary points: the zeros of the numerator and of the denominator — the only places the expression can change sign.
  3. Build the sign chart: test one value in each interval.
  4. Read off the answer, minding the boundary types: a numerator zero may be included (it makes the expression 0 — include it exactly when the inequality allows equality); a denominator zero is never included — the expression does not exist there, whatever the inequality sign says.

Two kinds of fencepost, two different rules. Bracket versus parenthesis at each boundary is not notation fussiness — it is the difference between a value that solves the inequality and a value where the question cannot even be asked.

Example 4

Solve \(\dfrac{x+3}{x-1} \le 0\).

Solution:

Already compared with zero. Boundary points: numerator zero at \(x = -3\); denominator zero at \(x = 1\). Three intervals; test each:

\(x = -4: \; \dfrac{-1}{-5} > 0 \qquad x = 0: \; \dfrac{3}{-1} < 0 \qquad x = 2: \; \dfrac{5}{1} > 0\)

The expression is negative only on \((-3, 1)\). Boundaries: at \(x = -3\) the expression equals 0, and “\(\le\)” permits equality — include it. At \(x = 1\) the expression does not exist — exclude it, sign or no sign.

Answer: \([-3, 1)\). One square bracket, one parenthesis, each earned by a different reason.

Example 5

Solve \(\dfrac{x}{x+2} \ge 3\).

Solution:

Do not multiply by \((x+2)\) — its sign is unknown. Subtract 3 and combine:

\(\dfrac{x}{x+2} - 3 \ge 0\)

\(\dfrac{x - 3(x+2)}{x+2} \ge 0\)

\(\dfrac{-2x - 6}{x+2} \ge 0\)

Boundary points: numerator zero at \(x = -3\); denominator zero at \(x = -2\). Test the three intervals:

\(x = -4: \; \dfrac{8 - 6}{-2} = -1 < 0 \qquad x = -2.5: \; \dfrac{5 - 6}{-0.5} = 2 > 0 \qquad x = 0: \; \dfrac{-6}{2} = -3 < 0\)

Positive only on \((-3, -2)\). Include \(x = -3\) (numerator zero, “\(\ge\)” allows equality); exclude \(x = -2\) (forbidden).

Answer: \([-3, -2)\). Sanity check with a member: \(x = -2.5\) gives \(\frac{-2.5}{-0.5} = 5 \ge 3\) ✓; and a non-member: \(x = 0\) gives \(0 \ge 3\), false ✓.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Multiplying a rational inequality through by its denominator. Try it on Example 5: \(x \ge 3(x+2)\) gives \(x \le -3\)… after a sign flip you could only justify by already knowing \(x + 2 < 0\) — which you don't, yet. The result (\(x \le -3\)) disagrees with the true answer \([-3, -2)\) almost everywhere. Equations tolerate the clearing move (with the check); inequalities do not. Zero on one side, one fraction, sign chart — every time.
▶  Try it yourself: Solve \(\dfrac{2}{x+1} = \dfrac{x-1}{x+1} + 1\). Work the full discipline — guard, clear, judge — before you peek.
Guard: \(x \neq -1\). Clear by \((x+1)\): \(2 = (x-1) + (x+1) = 2x\), so \(x = 1\). Judge: not forbidden. Verify: left \(\frac{2}{2} = 1\); right \(\frac{0}{2} + 1 = 1\) ✓. Answer: \(x = 1\).
▶  Try it yourself: Solve \(\dfrac{(x-2)(x+4)}{x-1} > 0\) with a sign chart.
Boundaries: \(-4, 2\) (numerator zeros), \(1\) (forbidden). Tests: \(x=-5: \frac{(-7)(-1)}{-6} < 0\); \(x=0: \frac{(-2)(4)}{-1} > 0\); \(x=1.5: \frac{(-0.5)(5.5)}{0.5} < 0\); \(x=3: \frac{(1)(7)}{2} > 0\). Strict inequality, so all boundaries excluded. Answer: \((-4, 1) \cup (2, \infty)\).
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Extraneous solutions are not a quirk of rational equations — they appear whenever a solving move runs in one direction only. Squaring both sides creates them too (you will meet that version with radical equations in Unit 3), and for the same reason: squaring, like multiplying by zero, can turn a false statement true. Mathematicians call such moves irreversible, and the profession's standing rule is exactly the one you learned today: any irreversible step incurs a debt, and the debt is paid by checking candidates in the original. Al-jabr restores; the check keeps the restoration honest.

Conclusion

Solving with rational expressions is a discipline of guarded moves: post the forbidden list before clearing, expect the cleared equation to be more generous than the original, and judge every candidate — counterfeits arise exactly at the LCD's zeros and nowhere else. Inequalities refuse the clearing move altogether and submit instead to the sign chart, with two kinds of fencepost that earn different brackets. And you watched an AI execute flawless algebra into a worthless answer, because the discipline — not the computation — is where the mathematics lives. Tomorrow these equations go to work: average cost, concentration, and shared labor, where the asymptotes you have studied become honest statements about the long run of real processes.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. What exactly does multiplying both sides by an \(x\)-containing expression do to an equation's solution set, and at which inputs can it add counterfeit solutions?

2. Why is “no solution,” with the extraneous candidate shown and rejected, a complete mathematical answer?

3. Why must rational inequalities be solved by sign chart rather than by clearing denominators?

4. At the boundary points of a rational inequality, what two different reasons decide bracket versus parenthesis?
