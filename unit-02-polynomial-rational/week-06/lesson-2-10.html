Modeling with Rational Functions
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to build rational-function models for average cost, concentration, and shared-work situations.

  • I will be able to interpret a model's asymptotes as honest statements about the long run of the real process.

  • I will be able to solve questions inside these models — equations and long-run limits — and report answers in the situation's own language.

📚 VOCABULARY

Average cost: Total cost divided by quantity produced, \(\overline{C}(x) = \frac{C(x)}{x}\) — what each unit costs on average, fixed costs included.

Fixed cost / variable cost: Costs that do not depend on quantity (rent, equipment) versus costs incurred per unit (materials, labor).

Concentration: Amount of a substance per amount of mixture — a ratio of two quantities that may both change in time.

Rate of work: The fraction of a job completed per unit time; a worker finishing alone in \(t\) hours works at rate \(\frac{1}{t}\) job per hour.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Polynomials modeled quantities built by multiplying — lengths into volumes. But an enormous share of the world's honest questions are ratios: cost per unit, sugar per gallon, work per hour. Ratio questions produce rational functions, and here the unit's virtue collects its full wages. A rational model's asymptotes are not decorations on a graph — they are the model telling the truth about the long run: the unit cost you can approach but never beat, the concentration a tank drifts toward and cannot pass. An asymptote, read rightly, is a promise about forever. Today you learn to read three classic models that keep such promises.

Example 1 — Average cost

A workshop that makes standing desks pays $12,500 per month in fixed costs (rent, machines, insurance) plus $8 in materials per desk. Build the average-cost model, evaluate it at 100 and at 5,000 desks, and interpret its horizontal asymptote.

Solution:

Define variables in words: let \(x\) be desks produced per month (\(x > 0\)); total cost is \(C(x) = 12{,}500 + 8x\) dollars. The average cost per desk is

\(\overline{C}(x) = \dfrac{12{,}500 + 8x}{x} = \dfrac{12{,}500}{x} + 8\)

Evaluate:

\(\overline{C}(100) = 125 + 8 = \$133 \text{ per desk} \qquad \overline{C}(5000) = 2.50 + 8 = \$10.50 \text{ per desk}\)

The second form of the model is the story itself: each desk carries its own $8 of materials plus its share of the rent, \(\frac{12{,}500}{x}\) — and the more desks share the rent, the thinner each share. Degrees are equal upstairs and down, so the HA is the ratio of leading coefficients: \(y = 8\).

Answer: \(\overline{C}(x) = 8 + \frac{12{,}500}{x}\); average cost falls from $133 toward the asymptote \(y = 8\). Interpretation: $8 is the floor — the materials cost that no production volume can dilute. The workshop can approach $8 per desk as closely as scale allows and can never reach it, because the rent's share is never quite zero. An economist would call $8 the marginal cost; the graph calls it an asymptote; both are naming the same honest limit.

Example 2 — Concentration

A 200-gallon tank starts with 10 pounds of sugar dissolved in it. Syrup containing 0.5 pound of sugar per gallon flows in at 10 gallons per minute (nothing drains). Model the sugar concentration over time, find when it reaches 0.4 lb/gal, and interpret the long run.

Solution:

Define variables in words: at \(t\) minutes, the tank holds \(200 + 10t\) gallons, containing \(10 + (0.5)(10)t = 10 + 5t\) pounds of sugar. Concentration is the ratio:

\(C(t) = \dfrac{10 + 5t}{200 + 10t}, \qquad t \ge 0\)

Starting value: \(C(0) = \frac{10}{200} = 0.05\) lb/gal. Set \(C(t) = 0.4\) and solve (guard: the denominator is never zero for \(t \ge 0\), so no extraneous ambush here):

\(10 + 5t = 0.4(200 + 10t)\)

\(10 + 5t = 80 + 4t\)

\(t = 70\)

Long run: degrees equal, HA \(= \frac{5}{10} = 0.5\) lb/gal.

Answer: The concentration hits 0.4 lb/gal at \(t = 70\) minutes (check: \(C(70) = \frac{360}{900} = 0.4\) ✓), rising ever after toward — but never reaching — 0.5 lb/gal. And 0.5 is no coincidence: it is exactly the incoming syrup's concentration. The asymptote states the physically obvious truth with algebraic precision: pour 0.5-strength syrup in forever, and the tank becomes ever more like what you pour into it, while the original 200 gallons of weak mixture never entirely stops mattering. The model knew the physics.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Modeling the concentration by tracking only the sugar — writing \(C(t) = \frac{10 + 5t}{200}\) as if the tank's volume stood still. A concentration is a ratio of two changing quantities, and both must be modeled: numerator the substance, denominator the mixture. The give-away that something is wrong: the sugar-only version grows without bound, predicting the tank eventually exceeds 1 lb/gal — stronger than the incoming syrup itself, which no amount of pouring can achieve. When a model out-predicts its own inputs, a denominator has gone missing.

Example 3 — Shared work

Pump A can fill a pool alone in 5 hours. With pump B helping, the pool fills in 3 hours. How long would B need alone?

Solution:

Define variables in words: let \(b\) be B's solo time in hours. Convert times to rates, because rates — not times — are what add when workers cooperate: A fills \(\frac{1}{5}\) of the pool per hour, B fills \(\frac{1}{b}\), and together they fill \(\frac{1}{3}\) per hour:

\(\dfrac{1}{5} + \dfrac{1}{b} = \dfrac{1}{3}\)

Guard: \(b \neq 0\). Multiply through by the LCD, \(15b\):

\(3b + 15 = 5b\)

\(15 = 2b\)

\(b = \dfrac{15}{2} = 7.5\)

Answer: Pump B alone needs 7.5 hours. Sense-check the answer against the structure: B alone (7.5 h) is slower than A alone (5 h), and both are slower than the team (3 h) — as cooperation demands. If your shared-work answer ever comes out faster than the team or faster than the better worker, a rate got added upside down. (Why do rates add and times not? Two 5-hour pumps together do not take 10 hours — they take 2.5. The reciprocal is where the addition honestly lives.)

📊 Try It in Excel: Watching the Floor Approach 15 min

Make Example 1's asymptote a thing you have seen. In a new workbook (school Microsoft account):

  1. Column A, header Desks: enter 50, 100, 200, 500, 1000, 2000, 5000, 10000, 50000.
  2. Column B, header Average cost: in B2 type =(12500 + 8*A2)/A2 and fill down.
  3. Column C, header Share of rent: in C2 type =12500/A2 and fill down. Watch B and C together: the average cost is always exactly \(8 + \) the rent share, and the rent share is what melts.
  4. Insert a scatter chart of A vs. B. The curve falls steeply, then flattens onto an invisible floor. Add a row with 1,000,000 desks: $8.0125. Ten million: $8.00125. The floor is real, and it is never touched.
  5. Now interrogate the model like a modeler: use Goal Seek (Data → What-If Analysis) or algebra to find the production level where average cost first drops below $10. (Algebra says \(\frac{12500}{x} = 2\), so \(x = 6{,}250\) — confirm it on the sheet.)

Economies of scale, the price floor, the long tail of diminishing returns — a semester of economics vocabulary, all sitting inside one rational function and one spreadsheet column.

💻 On Your Calculator: Concentration in Motion 5 min

At desmos.com/calculator, graph Example 2: C(t) = (10 + 5t)/(200 + 10t) with {t > 0} appended, plus the dashed lines y = 0.5 and y = 0.4. Click the intersection with \(y = 0.4\) to confirm \(t = 70\), then follow the curve rightward and watch it press against 0.5 without crossing — the incoming syrup's concentration, enforced forever. One graph, the whole story of the tank.

▶  Try it yourself: A pottery studio has $6,000 monthly fixed costs and $14 of materials per piece. Build the average-cost model, state its asymptote with its meaning, and find how many pieces bring the average cost below $20. Work it on paper before you peek.
\(\overline{C}(x) = \frac{6000 + 14x}{x} = 14 + \frac{6000}{x}\); asymptote \(y = 14\), the per-piece materials floor no volume can beat. Below \$20: \(\frac{6000}{x} < 6\), so \(x > 1000\) pieces. Check: \(\overline{C}(1000) = 14 + 6 = 20\) exactly, and more pieces only thin the rent's share further.
▶  Try it yourself: One printer finishes a mailing alone in 12 hours. With a second printer, the job takes 4 hours. How long would the second printer need alone — and how do you know your answer is sensible before checking it?
\(\frac{1}{12} + \frac{1}{s} = \frac{1}{4}\) gives \(\frac{1}{s} = \frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{12} = \frac{2}{12} = \frac{1}{6}\), so \(s = 6\) hours. Sensible: slower than the team (4 h) but here faster than the first printer — legal, since nothing requires the helper to be the weaker machine. The hard floor is only that no solo time may beat the team's time. Check: \(\frac{1}{12} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{3}{12} = \frac{1}{4}\) ✓.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Shared-work problems are among the oldest homework in existence. The Rhind Papyrus poses grain-and-labor versions; Diophantus, in third-century Alexandria, solved cistern problems with two spouts filling and one draining — the same reciprocal arithmetic you used for the pumps, seventeen centuries before your textbook. Even the famous rhyme about Diophantus's own life span (“his boyhood lasted one-sixth of his life…”) is a rational equation in disguise. Humanity has always wanted to know how long the job takes when help arrives.

Conclusion

Three models, one grammar: a ratio of two honestly-tracked quantities, a domain that respects the situation, and asymptotes that state the long run without flattery — the cost floor scale cannot break, the concentration ceiling the inflow enforces, the team time no worker can beat alone. Rational models tell the truth at infinity, which is exactly what polynomial models — whose ends always run to \(\pm\infty\) — cannot do. That contrast is the hinge of tomorrow's synthesis lesson, the unit's last: given a real situation and two candidate model families, how do you choose the one that will still be telling the truth when the data runs out? Bring everything; tomorrow uses all of it.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. In the average-cost model, what real thing does each algebraic piece — the \(8\), the \(\frac{12{,}500}{x}\), the asymptote — correspond to?

2. Why did the tank's asymptote come out equal to the incoming syrup's concentration — and what would it mean about the physics if a model's long run exceeded its inputs?

3. Why do rates add when workers cooperate, while times do not — and what sense-checks does that structure hand you for free?

4. What can a rational model say honestly about the long run that no polynomial model can?
