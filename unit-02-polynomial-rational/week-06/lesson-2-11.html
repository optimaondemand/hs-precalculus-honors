🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to select between polynomial and rational models for a real situation, using long-run behavior as the deciding evidence.

  • I will be able to write a model defense: claim, evidence from end behavior and asymptotes, domain of honesty, verdict.

  • I will be able to articulate how this unit's tools — end behavior, zeros, division, asymptotes — form one connected account of two function families.

📚 VOCABULARY

Model family: A class of functions sharing one structural form — this unit's two: polynomials and rational functions.

Long-run behavior: What a model predicts as its input grows without bound — a polynomial's ends run to \(\pm\infty\); a rational function's may level onto an asymptote.

Saturation: A real process leveling off toward a ceiling or floor it cannot pass — the behavior rational models capture and polynomials cannot.

Model defense: A short written argument for a model choice, resting on structure and long-run truth rather than fit alone.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Six weeks ago this unit opened with a duel in Bologna; it closes with a quieter contest that you will referee for the rest of your mathematical life: two candidate models, one real situation — which function deserves your trust? Everything the unit built was preparation for this question. End behavior (2-1) and asymptotes (2-7, 2-8) are the two families' sworn testimony about the long run; zeros and domains say where each model lives and dies; and the fitting lesson (2-6) taught the cardinal rule that a good score is not a good reason. Today assembles it all into a working judgment — the judgment your Focus assignment and Thursday's unit test will ask you to exercise in writing.

Start with the two families' characters, honestly compared:

Two Families, Two Kinds of Long-Run Truth

  • Polynomials have ends that run away: every polynomial's outputs go to \(+\infty\) or \(-\infty\) in both directions, no exceptions (the leading term guarantees it). They are the honest choice for quantities built by multiplying dimensions (volumes, areas) on a bounded domain — and a dishonest choice for anything that levels off, because a polynomial cannot level off. Ever.
  • Rational functions can hold a long-run level: horizontal asymptotes let them encode floors, ceilings, and saturation — per-unit costs, concentrations, anything “per” anything. They are the honest choice when the situation itself has a limit it approaches but cannot pass.

The deciding question: what does the real situation do in the long run? If it runs away, a polynomial can say so. If it levels off, only a rational function (of this unit's families) can tell that truth. Match the family's destiny to the situation's destiny first; fit coefficients second.

Example 1

A patient takes a single dose of medication, and the blood concentration is measured hourly for five hours: it rises quickly, peaks near 2.5 mg/L around hour 1, and declines. Two models fit the five data points about equally well: a quadratic \(q(t) = -0.9t^2 + 3.4t + 0.1\) and the rational function \(r(t) = \dfrac{5t}{t^2 + 1}\). Which deserves trust, and why?

Solution:

Interrogate both models about the long run — the hours beyond the data, which is where models earn their keep.

The quadratic: negative leading coefficient, so both ends fall to \(-\infty\). It predicts the concentration crosses zero (around \(t \approx 3.8\)) and then goes negative, without bound — less than no drug in the blood, and ever more so. Structurally false, not just inaccurate.

The rational candidate: numerator degree 1, denominator degree 2 — horizontal asymptote \(y = 0\). It predicts the concentration decays toward zero and never dips below it: check the formula — for \(t \ge 0\), \(\frac{5t}{t^2+1}\) is never negative. Peak: \(r(1) = \frac{5}{2} = 2.5\) mg/L, matching the data. Long run: the drug washes out, approaching zero forever — which is what bodies actually do.

Answer: The rational model — not because it fits better (it doesn't, noticeably), but because its structure tells the truth about the ends: medication washes out toward zero; it does not go negative. The quadratic was never a model of pharmacology; it was a curve visiting the data.

Key Fact: The Model Defense — a Four-Line Argument

Every model choice in this course is defended in writing, in this shape:

  1. Claim: the family (and degree) chosen.
  2. Structural evidence: what in the situation dictates the form — dimensions multiplying, a ratio of changing quantities, a saturation level.
  3. Long-run evidence: what the situation does at its far end, and how the model's end behavior or asymptote matches it — including what the rejected candidate would falsely predict.
  4. Domain of honesty: where the model's answers deserve trust, stated as an interval with reasons.

Notice what is absent: \(R^2\). Fit enters only after form is settled — it tunes coefficients inside an honest family; it never chooses the family. A defense that leads with the fit score has conceded the argument.

Example 2

A startup's accountant fits the company's average cost per unit over its first six quarters with a downward-sloping line, \(L(x) = 31 - 0.004x\), and reports: “at this rate, average cost hits zero near 7,750 units and goes negative — production will eventually pay for itself!” Write the model defense that corrects this, given fixed costs of $11,500 per quarter and $8 materials per unit.

Solution:

Claim: average cost is a rational function, \(\overline{C}(x) = \frac{11{,}500 + 8x}{x} = 8 + \frac{11{,}500}{x}\), not a line.

Structural evidence: average cost is by definition a ratio — total cost over quantity — and its numerator and denominator both change with \(x\). The form is dictated before any data arrives.

Long-run evidence: the rational model has horizontal asymptote \(y = 8\): average cost falls forever toward the $8 materials floor and never reaches it, because each unit always carries its own materials plus a nonzero share of rent. The line, extended, predicts \(\$0\) and then negative cost per unit — production so efficient the units pay the company to make them. No cost structure on earth does this; the line's long run is fiction.

Domain of honesty: the line approximates the rational curve decently over the sampled quarters (where the curve is nearly straight) — that is why it fit — but its trustworthy domain ends where the data does. The rational model is honest on all \(x > 0\).

Answer: The accountant fit a tangent-line snapshot of a curving truth and extrapolated the snapshot. The rational model, built from the cost structure, is the one whose forever can be trusted — and its asymptote delivers the honest verdict: costs approach $8 per unit and no cleverness of scale beats the floor.

Example 3

When is the polynomial the honest choice? Revisit the corner-cut box, \(V(x) = x(20-2x)(16-2x)\), and defend it against a rational challenger.

Solution:

Claim: a cubic polynomial, in factored form, on the domain \((0, 8)\).

Structural evidence: volume is three lengths multiplied; each length is linear in the cut \(x\); the product is degree 3 by construction. No ratio exists anywhere in the situation — nothing is “per” anything.

Long-run evidence: there is no long run! The situation itself is bounded — cuts live strictly between 0 and 8 cm — so the cubic's runaway ends are never consulted. A polynomial's one structural weakness is irrelevant on a bounded domain. (A rational challenger would need an asymptote to justify its denominator, and this situation has nothing that saturates — the extra machinery would model nothing.)

Domain of honesty: \(0 < x < 8\), from the scissors, declared at birth.

Answer: The cubic, exactly as built in Lesson 2-6. The moral completes the unit: neither family is “better.” Polynomials are honest where quantities multiply on bounded domains; rational functions are honest where quantities ratio and situations saturate. Honesty is a property of the match, not of the family.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Defending a model with “it fits the data better.” Over any modest data range, a high-degree polynomial will out-fit an honest rational model every time — more coefficients always hug points more tightly. The Example 1 quadratic fit five points beautifully while predicting negative medication. Fit measures the past; structure predicts the future. On the unit test and the applied task, a fit-first defense scores as what it is: the reasoning criterion's “what without why.”

💻 On Your Calculator: The Long Run on Trial 10 min

Stage Example 1's contest at desmos.com/calculator (or below):

  1. Enter the two candidates: q(t) = -0.9t^2 + 3.4t + 0.1 and r(t) = 5t/(t^2 + 1), with {t > 0} appended to each.
  2. Restrict your window to the data's range first — \(0 \le t \le 5\), \(0 \le y \le 3\). The two curves nearly coincide: inside the data, the contest looks like a tie. This is why fit alone cannot referee.
  3. Now widen to \(t\) from 0 to 15. Watch the quadratic dive through zero and plunge; watch the rational curve settle gently onto the axis. The models agreed about the past and disagree completely about the future — and only one future is pharmacology.
  4. Add the line y = 0: the boundary between medicine and nonsense, crossed by exactly one candidate.

This two-window habit — data window, then destiny window — is the fastest model-judgment tool you own. Use it on the applied task this week.

▶  Try it yourself: A wildlife preserve's deer population grows quickly at first, then levels off near the land's carrying capacity. A consultant offers a cubic model with a strong fit. Write the two-sentence core of the rejection. Work it before you peek.
A leveling-off population saturates toward a ceiling, and no polynomial can hold a level — every cubic's ends run to \(\pm\infty\), so this one must eventually predict either unbounded deer or negative deer. The honest family needs a horizontal asymptote at the carrying capacity, which is rational-function territory (and, later in Unit 3, logistic territory) — the fit score cannot repair a family that is structurally unable to tell the story's ending.
▶  Try it yourself: Name the honest family, with one structural reason and one long-run reason each: ① the area of a square photo enlarged by scale factor \(s\); ② the average download cost per gigabyte on a plan with a $40 base fee; ③ the concentration of pollutant in a lake as clean water flows through.
① Polynomial (degree 2): two dimensions multiply; the domain is bounded by physical print sizes, so runaway ends never testify. ② Rational: cost per gigabyte is a ratio; asymptote at the per-GB rate is the floor the base fee's share approaches. ③ Rational: amount over volume, both changing; the long run saturates toward the inflow's (clean) concentration — an asymptote at zero, which is the honest fate of a flushed lake.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The statistician George Box gave applied mathematics its most-quoted sentence: “All models are wrong, but some are useful.” Less quoted is his next thought — that the practical question is “how wrong do they have to be to not be useful.” This unit's answer: a model stops being useful exactly where it starts lying about structure — negative volumes, negative concentrations, costs below the materials floor. Honest modeling is not the search for a right model; it is the discipline of knowing precisely where and how yours is wrong.

Conclusion

This unit began with a promise: a polynomial cannot lie about its long run. It ends with the responsibility that promise creates — since the functions testify truthfully, the modeler who picks the wrong witness owns the falsehood. You can now read a polynomial's destiny from its leading term, dismantle it to its zeros and rebuild it to order, read a rational function's floors and ceilings from its degrees, and — the capstone skill — match a family's destiny to a situation's and defend the match in four honest lines. Thursday's unit test gathers all of it: fluency, reasoning, and one synthesis problem that reaches back to Unit 1. And this week's applied task, The Honest Box, hands you a real sheet of card, real scissors, and real noisy data — the referee's chair is yours.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. What can a rational function say about the long run that no polynomial can — and what real situations need exactly that testimony?

2. Why does fit measure the past while structure predicts the future, and where in a model defense does each kind of evidence belong?

3. When is a polynomial the honest choice despite its runaway ends — what makes its structural weakness irrelevant?

4. Looking back across the unit: how do end behavior, zeros, division, and asymptotes combine into one account of how these two families tell the truth?
