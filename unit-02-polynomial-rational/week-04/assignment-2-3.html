📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
This is Week 4's Focus assignment — the one set this week your teacher grades for method, not just completion. Two problems below carry a gold ⭐ METHOD PROBLEM badge: one asks you to prove the Factor Theorem and put it to work; the other puts you in the examiner's chair over an AI's confident, flawed division. Those two are read closely and scored against the rubric, looking at how you reason. Give every problem your honest work, and give the method problems your best mathematical writing: every division ending in its identity, every claim carrying its check.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
Every division hides a multiplication statement, and every remainder hides a function value. How do those two facts, welded together, turn zeros and factors into the same thing?
📝 PROBLEM SET

Show your work for every problem in neat vertical form — one step per line, equals signs aligned, final answers labeled. End every division by writing the identity dividend = divisor × quotient + remainder. Fractions stay exact.

1
Divide by long division and state the identity: \((3x^3 + 2x^2 - x + 4) \div (x^2 - 1)\). Verify by expanding your identity.
2
Divide by synthetic division and state the identity: \((x^4 - 5x^2 + 4x - 12) \div (x + 3)\). (Mind both traps from the lesson: the placeholder and the sign of \(k\).) Audit your remainder with the Remainder Theorem.
3
Use synthetic division to evaluate \(f(2)\) for \(f(x) = 2x^4 - x^3 + 3x - 5\), then confirm by direct substitution. One sentence: which method would you choose for \(f(2)\) if \(f\) had degree 40, and why?
⭐ METHOD PROBLEM — your teacher grades this one for method
4
① Prove the Factor Theorem, both directions, taking the Remainder Theorem as given: (⇒) if \(x - k\) is a factor of \(f(x)\), then \(f(k) = 0\); (⇐) if \(f(k) = 0\), then \(x - k\) is a factor. Write it for a classmate who has seen the Remainder Theorem but not this proof — every line earned, full sentences connecting the algebra. ② Apply it: show that \(x - 2\) is a factor of \(f(x) = x^3 - 4x^2 + x + 6\), divide it out synthetically, factor completely, and verify every zero by substitution.
5
Without performing any division: ① decide whether \(x + 2\) is a factor of \(x^5 + 32\), with justification; ② find the remainder when \(x^5 + 32\) is divided by \(x - 1\). Name the theorem doing the work each time.
6
A division ran in reverse: when \(f(x)\) is divided by \(x - 4\), the quotient is \(x^2 + 3x - 2\) and the remainder is 7. Reconstruct \(f(x)\) in expanded form, and state \(f(4)\) without substituting into your expanded answer — then substitute anyway to check.
⭐ METHOD PROBLEM — your teacher grades this one for method
7
A student asked an AI to divide \(f(x) = x^4 - 2x^3 + x - 5\) by \(x + 1\) synthetically. The AI answered: “Using the coefficients 1, −2, 0, 1, −5 with k = 1: bring down 1; multiply and add to get −1; then −1; then 0; then −5. The quotient is \(x^3 - x^2 - x\) and the remainder is \(-5\).” Work as an examiner, in three numbered parts: ① Audit the claim without redoing its division — use the Remainder Theorem to compute what the remainder must be, and state the contradiction in a full sentence. ② Locate the exact error in the AI's setup (its arithmetic is internally correct — the flaw is upstream) and name it. ③ Perform the correct division, state the identity, and audit your own remainder the same way you audited the machine's. Close with one sentence: why did checking the AI's steps one by one have no chance of catching this error?
8
Honors stretch: ① Find the value of \(c\) for which \(x^3 + cx + 10\) leaves remainder 4 when divided by \(x + 2\). ② Prove, in two lines, that \(x - 1\) is a factor of \(x^n - 1\) for every positive integer \(n\) — one theorem, one substitution, infinitely many factorizations settled at once.
💡 CHECK YOURSELF WITH DESMOS
After your hand work is done, graph problem 4's polynomial \(f(x) = x^3 - 4x^2 + x + 6\) in Desmos (desmos.com/calculator) and click the \(x\)-intercepts: they should be exactly the zeros your factorization produced. Then graph problem 6's reconstructed \(f(x)\) together with \(y = 7\) and confirm the curve passes through \((4, 7)\). The calculator confirms; your algebra convinces.

📊 GRADING RUBRIC

Your teacher applies this rubric to the two ⭐ method problems (the rest of the set is checked for honest completion). This same four-criterion standard governs every hand-graded item in this course, all year.

Criteria Excellent (4) Proficient (3) Developing (2) Beginning (1) Pts
Understanding of the material
4 pts		 Method fits the problem exactly; definitions, notation, and results are used correctly throughout. Method is sound with a minor conceptual slip that does not derail the solution. Partial grasp; the approach starts correctly but loses the thread. The method does not match the mathematics being asked. 4 pts
Quality of reasoning
4 pts		 Every step follows from the last; the why is stated, and verification is actually performed. Reasoning is mostly explicit; one link in the chain is asserted rather than shown. Steps are present but read as recipe-following; explanations restate what without why. Answer appears with little or no supporting reasoning. 4 pts
Presentation & precision
4 pts		 Clean vertical layout, aligned equals signs, labeled answers, exact values; the page reads as a visual argument. Organized and legible with small lapses in labeling or alignment. Readable but disorderly; the grader must hunt for the thread. Work is scattered or illegible; steps cannot be followed. 4 pts
Intellectual honesty
4 pts		 All work shown faithfully as done; uncertainty and gaps are named plainly; nothing is dressed up. Work is genuine; one rough patch is smoothed over rather than acknowledged. Some steps appear reverse-engineered from the answer rather than worked. The page misrepresents how the work was actually done. 4 pts
