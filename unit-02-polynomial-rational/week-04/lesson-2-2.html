Graphs of Polynomial Functions
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to find the zeros of a polynomial in factored form and state the multiplicity of each.

  • I will be able to predict how a graph behaves at each \(x\)-intercept — crossing, touching, or flattening — from the multiplicity of the zero there.

  • I will be able to sketch a complete polynomial graph from factored form, and bound the number of zeros and turning points from the degree.

  • I will be able to use a sign change to guarantee that a zero exists between two inputs, and explain why the guarantee works.

📚 VOCABULARY

Zero (root): An input \(c\) with \(f(c) = 0\); on the graph, an \(x\)-intercept at \((c, 0)\).

Multiplicity: The number of times a zero's factor appears — if \((x-c)^m\) divides the polynomial, the zero \(c\) has multiplicity \(m\).

Turning point: A point where the graph changes direction, from rising to falling or falling to rising.

Smooth and continuous: Polynomial graphs have no sharp corners (smooth) and no breaks, jumps, or holes (continuous) — drawable without lifting the pencil.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Yesterday you learned that a polynomial's ends are non-negotiable. Today we enter the middle of the graph — the part the leading term shrugs off — and discover it is just as law-governed. The middle's story is told at the \(x\)-intercepts, and the storyteller is the factored form.

When Cardano published the cubic solution in Ars Magna, the deepest question it raised was not how to find the roots of an equation but how many there are and how they sit. Over the next three centuries mathematicians learned that a polynomial's roots, counted properly, are its skeleton: know where the graph meets the axis and how it meets it, and the whole shape hangs on that frame. “Counted properly” is the key phrase, and it is the idea this lesson adds to your toolkit: multiplicity.

If a polynomial is factored, its zeros are on display. Since a product is zero exactly when one of its factors is zero, the zeros of \(f(x) = (x-3)(x+1)^2\) are \(x = 3\) and \(x = -1\). But the two are not equal citizens: the factor \((x+1)\) appears squared. We say \(x = -1\) is a zero of multiplicity 2, and \(x = 3\) a zero of multiplicity 1. The graph treats them very differently.

Multiplicity and Behavior at the Intercept

Suppose \(c\) is a zero of multiplicity \(m\) — the factor \((x-c)^m\) appears in the polynomial. Near \(x = c\), the graph behaves like a scaled copy of \(y = k(x-c)^m\):

  • \(m = 1\): the graph crosses the axis cleanly, like a line passing through.
  • \(m\) even: the graph touches the axis and turns back — a bounce, like the vertex of a parabola sitting on the axis. Near \(c\), \((x-c)^m \ge 0\) on both sides, so the output cannot change sign there.
  • \(m\) odd, \(m \ge 3\): the graph crosses, but flattens as it does — an S-shaped pause at the axis, like \(y = x^3\) at the origin. The sign still changes; the crossing just lingers.

The why is a sign argument: crossing the axis means changing sign, and \((x-c)^m\) changes sign at \(c\) exactly when \(m\) is odd. Even powers destroy the sign change; odd powers preserve it.

Example 1

Find the zeros of \(f(x) = -2x^3(x-1)^2(x+3)\), state each multiplicity, and describe the graph's behavior at each intercept.

Solution:

Read the factors: \(x^3\) gives the zero \(x = 0\) with multiplicity 3; \((x-1)^2\) gives \(x = 1\) with multiplicity 2; \((x+3)\) gives \(x = -3\) with multiplicity 1.

\(x = -3\): multiplicity 1 — clean crossing

\(x = 0\): multiplicity 3 — flattened crossing (the \(x^3\) pause)

\(x = 1\): multiplicity 2 — touch and turn back

Note the bookkeeping: the multiplicities total \(3 + 2 + 1 = 6\), which is the degree. Six roots, counted properly, for a degree-6 polynomial — the skeleton is complete.

Answer: Zeros \(-3\) (mult. 1, crosses), \(0\) (mult. 3, crosses with flattening), \(1\) (mult. 2, touches).

Now let us build a full sketch by hand. The procedure assembles everything you know so far: end behavior claims the two ends, the zeros nail down the axis meetings, multiplicity dictates the manner of each meeting, and the \(y\)-intercept anchors the height. Between consecutive zeros a polynomial cannot touch the axis, so it stays one sign throughout each gap — the pieces can only connect one way.

Example 2

Sketch \(g(x) = (x+2)^2(x-3)\) by hand.

Solution:

End behavior: degree \(2 + 1 = 3\), leading coefficient \(1 \cdot 1 = 1\): odd degree, positive — falls left, rises right.

Zeros: \(x = -2\) (multiplicity 2: touch) and \(x = 3\) (multiplicity 1: cross).

\(y\)-intercept:

\(g(0) = (0+2)^2(0-3) = 4 \cdot (-3) = -12\)

Assemble, left to right: the graph comes up from \(-\infty\), rises to touch the axis at \(x = -2\), turns back down (it may not cross — multiplicity 2 forbids it), dips through \((0, -12)\), then turns and crosses cleanly at \(x = 3\), rising to \(+\infty\). Two turning points — the maximum allowed for degree 3 is \(3 - 1 = 2\), so the sketch is as wiggly as this polynomial can be.

Answer: A cubic that bounces off the axis at \(-2\), bottoms out below the axis (passing through \((0,-12)\)), and crosses at \(3\). Check the logic: between the zeros \(-2\) and \(3\), the graph never touches the axis, so it stays negative the whole way — consistent with \(g(0) = -12\).

Key Fact: Two Bounds Every Polynomial Obeys

For a polynomial of degree \(n\):

  • The graph has at most \(n\) \(x\)-intercepts (the multiplicities of all zeros total exactly \(n\), so distinct zeros number at most \(n\)).
  • The graph has at most \(n - 1\) turning points. Each turn happens strictly between axis-crossings of the direction of travel, and there is one fewer gap than the degree allows.

These are ceilings, not promises — \(y = x^5\) is degree 5 with one intercept and zero turning points. But no degree-5 polynomial will ever show you six intercepts or five turns. If your sketch does, the sketch is wrong.

Example 3

Show that \(f(x) = x^3 - 5x + 1\) has a zero between \(x = 0\) and \(x = 1\), without finding it.

Solution:

Evaluate at both ends:

\(f(0) = 0 - 0 + 1 = 1 > 0\)

\(f(1) = 1 - 5 + 1 = -3 < 0\)

The graph is above the axis at \(x = 0\) and below it at \(x = 1\). A polynomial graph is continuous — drawable without lifting the pencil — so on its way from height \(1\) down to height \(-3\) it must pass through height \(0\) somewhere in between. That crossing is a zero.

Answer: Since \(f(0)\) and \(f(1)\) have opposite signs and \(f\) is continuous, a zero lies in \((0, 1)\). This sign-change guarantee is your first taste of the Intermediate Value Theorem, a pillar of the calculus waiting one course ahead. Notice its honest limits, though: a sign change proves a zero is there, but no sign change proves nothing — the graph could dip down to touch the axis and come back (a multiplicity-2 zero) without ever going negative.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Drawing the graph crossing the axis at every intercept. An even-multiplicity zero is a one-way mirror: the graph arrives, touches, and retreats to the side it came from. If you cross at a squared factor, every sign your graph shows after that point is wrong. Before sketching, annotate each zero with its multiplicity and the verdict — cross, touch, or flatten — and only then draw.

Example 4

Write a formula for the polynomial graphed below-described: degree 3; zeros at \(x = -1\) (crossing cleanly) and \(x = 2\) (touching); \(y\)-intercept \((0, -8)\).

Solution:

The zero at \(-1\) crosses cleanly: multiplicity 1, factor \((x+1)\). The zero at \(2\) touches: even multiplicity, and since the degree is 3, it must be exactly 2 (any more would overshoot): factor \((x-2)^2\). Multiplicities \(1 + 2 = 3\) ✓. So

\(f(x) = a(x+1)(x-2)^2\)

for some stretch factor \(a\), which the \(y\)-intercept determines:

\(f(0) = a(1)(4) = 4a = -8\)

\(a = -2\)

Answer: \(f(x) = -2(x+1)(x-2)^2\). Verify the story it tells: degree 3, leading coefficient \(-2\) — rises left, falls right — and a graph arriving from above, crossing at \(-1\), passing \((0,-8)\)… wait: \(f(0) = -8\) is below the axis, and the touch at \(x=2\) must happen from below. Rises left, crosses down through \(-1\), dips through \((0,-8)\), rises to touch the axis at \(2\) from beneath, and falls away to \(-\infty\). Every clue consistent — the formula answers to all its evidence.

💻 On Your Calculator: Windows, Zeros, and the Whole Truth 15 min

This is one of the year's designated calculator-craft lessons: today you learn windowing — making the calculator show you the whole graph, not a misleading slice. Open desmos.com/calculator (or the window below):

  1. Graph f(x) = (x+2)^2 (x-3), Example 2's cubic. In the default window you can already see both intercepts — click each one. Desmos labels the points: gray dots are zeros and turning points. Confirm: touch at \(-2\), cross at \(3\), minimum near \((1.33, -18.5)\).
  2. Now graph g(x) = (x-1)(x-30)(x+25). The default window shows what looks like one steep line — a lie of cropping, not of algebra. The zeros at \(-25\) and \(30\) are off-screen, and the interesting valley between them is thousands of units deep.
  3. Fix the window: click the wrench (top right), set \(x\) from \(-40\) to \(40\) and \(y\) from \(-15000\) to \(15000\). Now the graph tells the truth: three crossings, two turning points, the full cubic shape.
  4. Windowing rule of thumb: set the \(x\)-range to cover all zeros with margin, then widen the \(y\)-range until every turning point is visible. Your degree bounds tell you how many of each to hunt for — if the degree is 3 and you see one turning point, keep widening.

The calculator draws fast and thinks not at all. You know a cubic with three real zeros must turn twice; the machine will happily crop both turns out of view. The window is your responsibility — that is what “the mathematician supervises the tool” means in practice.

▶  Try it yourself: Find the zeros and multiplicities of \(h(x) = 4x(x-5)^2(x+1)^3\), give the behavior at each intercept, and state the degree and end behavior. Work it on paper before you peek.
Zeros: \(0\) (mult. 1, crosses), \(5\) (mult. 2, touches), \(-1\) (mult. 3, flattened crossing). Degree \(1+2+3 = 6\), leading coefficient \(4\): even degree, positive — both ends rise. As \(x \to \pm\infty, h(x) \to \infty\).
▶  Try it yourself: Write a formula for a degree-4 polynomial with zeros at \(-3\) (touching) and \(1\) (touching) whose \(y\)-intercept is \((0, 18)\).
Both zeros touch, so both have even multiplicity; degree 4 forces multiplicity 2 each: \(f(x) = a(x+3)^2(x-1)^2\). Then \(f(0) = a(9)(1) = 9a = 18\), so \(a = 2\) and \(f(x) = 2(x+3)^2(x-1)^2\). Check the shape: positive leading coefficient, both ends rise, and the graph touches the axis at both zeros — it never goes below the axis at all.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Multiplicity is not bookkeeping pedantry — it is physics. In engineering, a system whose “characteristic polynomial” has a repeated root behaves qualitatively differently from one with distinct roots: a car's suspension is deliberately tuned near a double root (“critical damping”) so it settles without bouncing. The difference between a crossing and a touch, which you learned to read today, is the difference between a car that oscillates after a pothole and one that doesn't.

Conclusion

The middle of a polynomial's graph is governed at its \(x\)-intercepts: each zero's multiplicity dictates a crossing, a touch, or a flattened pause, the degree caps how many intercepts and turns can exist at all, and a simple sign change is enough to guarantee a hidden zero between two inputs. With end behavior from Lesson 2-1, you can now sketch any factored polynomial by hand and audit any calculator graph for cropped truths. The obvious next question is the hard one: what if the polynomial is not factored? Handing you the tools to factor it yourself — division, and two theorems of startling elegance — is the business of Lesson 2-3.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does an even-multiplicity zero force the graph to touch and turn back, while an odd multiplicity lets it cross? What is the sign argument?

2. Where do the bounds “at most \(n\) intercepts” and “at most \(n-1\) turning points” come from, and how do they let you audit a graph — hand-drawn or machine-drawn — for errors?

3. What does a sign change between \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\) guarantee, what property of polynomials makes the guarantee work, and what does the absence of a sign change fail to tell you?

4. Given a graph, what clues recover its formula — and which single piece of information pins down the stretch factor \(a\)?
