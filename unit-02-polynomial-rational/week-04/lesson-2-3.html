Dividing Polynomials: The Remainder and Factor Theorems
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to divide polynomials by long division, and state the result in the form dividend = divisor × quotient + remainder.

  • I will be able to perform synthetic division for divisors of the form \(x - k\), including inserting placeholders for missing terms.

  • I will be able to state, prove, and apply the Remainder Theorem and the Factor Theorem.

  • I will be able to audit a division — mine or a machine's — using the Remainder Theorem as an independent check.

📚 VOCABULARY

Division algorithm (for polynomials): For polynomials \(f(x)\) and \(d(x) \neq 0\), there are unique polynomials \(q(x)\) (quotient) and \(r(x)\) (remainder) with \(f(x) = d(x)\,q(x) + r(x)\), where the degree of \(r\) is less than the degree of \(d\).

Synthetic division: A compressed bookkeeping of long division that works when the divisor is \(x - k\); only coefficients travel.

Remainder Theorem: Dividing \(f(x)\) by \(x - k\) leaves the remainder \(f(k)\).

Factor Theorem: \(x - k\) is a factor of \(f(x)\) exactly when \(f(k) = 0\).

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Yesterday's lesson ended with a challenge: everything you can do with a factored polynomial is worthless if nobody hands you the factors. Today you forge the tool that finds them. It is, of all things, division — the same long division you learned for whole numbers in grammar school, wearing algebraic dress — and it comes with two theorems so clean they feel like magic tricks until you prove them. Then they feel better than magic: they feel true.

First, remember what division of numbers really asserts. When you divide 47 by 5 and get “9 remainder 2,” you are claiming \(47 = 5 \cdot 9 + 2\) — a multiplication statement, checkable in one line. Polynomial division makes exactly the same kind of claim:

The Division Algorithm

Dividing a polynomial \(f(x)\) by a nonzero polynomial \(d(x)\) produces a unique quotient \(q(x)\) and remainder \(r(x)\) with

\(\Large f(x) = d(x)\,q(x) + r(x), \qquad \deg r < \deg d\)

The degree condition is what makes the answer finished: you keep dividing until what is left over is too small to divide again — just as 47 ÷ 5 stops when the leftover 2 is smaller than 5. Every division you do today should end by writing this identity out; it is both the answer and its own receipt.

Example 1

Divide \(2x^3 - 3x^2 + 4x + 5\) by \(x^2 + 1\) using long division.

Solution:

Ask the leading terms: how many times does \(x^2\) go into \(2x^3\)? Answer: \(2x\). Multiply back, subtract, and repeat:

\(2x \cdot (x^2 + 1) = 2x^3 + 2x\)

\((2x^3 - 3x^2 + 4x + 5) - (2x^3 + 2x) = -3x^2 + 2x + 5\)

Now: how many times does \(x^2\) go into \(-3x^2\)? Answer: \(-3\).

\(-3 \cdot (x^2 + 1) = -3x^2 - 3\)

\((-3x^2 + 2x + 5) - (-3x^2 - 3) = 2x + 8\)

The leftover \(2x + 8\) has degree 1, less than the divisor's degree 2 — the division is finished.

Answer: Quotient \(q(x) = 2x - 3\), remainder \(r(x) = 2x + 8\); that is,

\(2x^3 - 3x^2 + 4x + 5 = (x^2+1)(2x-3) + (2x + 8)\)

Verify the receipt by expanding: \((x^2+1)(2x-3) = 2x^3 - 3x^2 + 2x - 3\), and adding \(2x + 8\) restores \(2x^3 - 3x^2 + 4x + 5\) ✓. Thirty seconds of multiplication, and the answer is beyond doubt.

When the divisor has the special form \(x - k\), the long-division scaffolding carries almost no information — every column does the same two moves: multiply by \(k\), add. Synthetic division strips the process to its coefficients.

Example 2

Divide \(x^3 - 7x + 6\) by \(x - 2\) using synthetic division, and factor the polynomial completely.

Solution:

Write the coefficients of the dividend in degree order, inserting 0 for the missing \(x^2\) term: \(1, \; 0, \; -7, \; 6\). The divisor \(x - 2\) means \(k = 2\). Bring down the 1; then repeat multiply by 2, add across the row:

\(\begin{array}{c|rrrr} 2 & 1 & 0 & -7 & 6 \\ & & 2 & 4 & -6 \\ \hline & 1 & 2 & -3 & \fbox{0} \end{array}\)

The bottom row reads off a quotient one degree lower than the dividend, and the boxed final entry is the remainder: \(q(x) = x^2 + 2x - 3\), \(r = 0\). Remainder zero means \(x - 2\) divides evenly — it is a factor. The quotient factors by inspection: \(x^2 + 2x - 3 = (x+3)(x-1)\).

Answer: \(x^3 - 7x + 6 = (x-2)(x+3)(x-1)\). Zeros: \(2, -3, 1\) — yesterday's whole graphing toolkit now applies to a polynomial that arrived unfactored.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Two slips cause almost every synthetic-division error. First: forgetting the zero placeholder for a missing term — divide \(x^3 - 7x + 6\) with the row \(1, -7, 6\) and every number after the first is wrong. Second: using the wrong sign for \(k\) — dividing by \(x + 3\) means \(k = -3\), because \(x + 3 = x - (-3)\). Both slips are caught by the same audit: the Remainder Theorem below gives you an independent check that costs one substitution.

Now for the theorems. Look back at the boxed remainder in Example 2 and ask the question a mathematician always asks: is that number telling me something? It is — and the proof is two lines long, using nothing but the division algorithm you already trust.

Key Fact: The Remainder Theorem — with Proof

\(\Large\) When \(f(x)\) is divided by \(x - k\), the remainder is \(f(k)\).

Proof. The division algorithm gives \(f(x) = (x-k)\,q(x) + r\), where the remainder \(r\) must have degree less than 1 — so \(r\) is a constant. This identity holds for every value of \(x\), so choose the one value that makes the first term vanish: \(x = k\).

\(f(k) = (k - k)\,q(k) + r = 0 + r = r \qquad \blacksquare\)

Read the consequence both ways: evaluating \(f(k)\) tells you a remainder without dividing, and a synthetic division tells you \(f(k)\) without substituting. Two computations, one truth — and each can audit the other.

Example 3

Use the Remainder Theorem (via synthetic division) to find \(f(-2)\) for \(f(x) = x^4 - 3x^2 + 5x - 2\), and confirm by direct substitution.

Solution:

Coefficients with placeholders: \(1, \; 0, \; -3, \; 5, \; -2\), and \(k = -2\):

\(\begin{array}{c|rrrrr} -2 & 1 & 0 & -3 & 5 & -2 \\ & & -2 & 4 & -2 & -6 \\ \hline & 1 & -2 & 1 & 3 & \fbox{-8} \end{array}\)

The remainder is \(-8\), so \(f(-2) = -8\). Direct check:

\(f(-2) = 16 - 3(4) + 5(-2) - 2 = 16 - 12 - 10 - 2 = -8\) ✓

Answer: \(f(-2) = -8\), by two independent roads. (For large polynomials, the synthetic road is genuinely faster — this is how computers evaluate polynomials, under the name Horner's method.)

The Factor Theorem is the Remainder Theorem's finest consequence — the special case where the remainder is zero, stated in both directions.

Key Fact: The Factor Theorem — with Proof

\(\Large\) \(x - k\) is a factor of \(f(x)\)  \(\iff\)  \(f(k) = 0\).

Proof. (⇒) If \(x - k\) is a factor, then \(f(x) = (x-k)\,q(x)\) exactly, and substituting \(x = k\) gives \(f(k) = 0 \cdot q(k) = 0\).

(⇐) If \(f(k) = 0\), the Remainder Theorem says the remainder on dividing by \(x-k\) is \(f(k) = 0\), so \(f(x) = (x-k)\,q(x) + 0\) — a clean factorization. \(\blacksquare\)

This theorem is the bridge of the whole unit: it says zeros and factors are the same information. Find a zero, and you own a factor; divide it out, and the polynomial drops a degree. Repeat, and any polynomial surrenders one root at a time — exactly the strategy Lesson 2-4 turns into a systematic hunt.

Example 4

Show that \(x + 1\) is a factor of \(f(x) = x^3 + 2x^2 - 5x - 6\), and factor \(f\) completely.

Solution:

By the Factor Theorem, \(x + 1 = x - (-1)\) is a factor exactly when \(f(-1) = 0\):

\(f(-1) = -1 + 2 + 5 - 6 = 0\) ✓

Factor confirmed. Divide it out synthetically with \(k = -1\), coefficients \(1, 2, -5, -6\):

\(\begin{array}{c|rrrr} -1 & 1 & 2 & -5 & -6 \\ & & -1 & -1 & 6 \\ \hline & 1 & 1 & -6 & \fbox{0} \end{array}\)

Quotient: \(x^2 + x - 6 = (x+3)(x-2)\).

Answer: \(f(x) = (x+1)(x+3)(x-2)\), with zeros \(-1, -3, 2\). Audit the whole factorization at once: the constant terms multiply to \((1)(3)(-2) = -6\), matching \(f\)'s constant term ✓, and one more spot check \(f(2) = 8 + 8 - 10 - 6 = 0\) ✓.

⚠️ AI CHECKPOINT: AUDIT THE MACHINE'S DIVISION

A student asked an AI to divide \(x^3 - 7x + 6\) by \(x - 2\) using synthetic division. It replied, confidently and step by step:

“Set up the synthetic division with the coefficients 1, −7, and 6, using k = 2. Bring down the 1. Multiply: 1 × 2 = 2; add: −7 + 2 = −5. Multiply: −5 × 2 = −10; add: 6 + (−10) = −4. So the quotient is \(x - 5\) with remainder \(-4\).”

Every arithmetic step in that paragraph is correct — and the answer is still wrong. Audit it with the Remainder Theorem, the thirty-second check that needs no division at all: the remainder on dividing by \(x - 2\) must equal \(f(2) = 8 - 14 + 6 = 0\). The AI claims the remainder is \(-4\). Contradiction — something upstream of the arithmetic is broken. Now find it: the AI listed three coefficients for a degree-3 polynomial. It skipped the zero placeholder for the missing \(x^2\) term, so its entire row was misaligned — the exact first slip from this lesson's Common Mistake box, executed flawlessly.

This is the pattern to internalize: an AI's arithmetic can be perfect while its setup is wrong, and no amount of rereading its steps will reveal a setup error — only an independent check can. The Remainder Theorem is precisely such a check. On this week's Focus assignment, you will run this audit on another AI division and write up your findings like an examiner.

▶  Try it yourself: Use synthetic division to divide \(2x^3 + x^2 - 13x + 6\) by \(x - 2\), state the quotient and remainder, and factor completely if the remainder allows. Work it on paper before you peek.
Coefficients \(2, 1, -13, 6\) with \(k = 2\): bring down 2; \(2\cdot2=4\), \(1+4=5\); \(5\cdot2=10\), \(-13+10=-3\); \(-3\cdot2=-6\), \(6-6=0\). Quotient \(2x^2 + 5x - 3\), remainder 0 — so \(x-2\) is a factor, and \(2x^2+5x-3 = (2x-1)(x+3)\). Complete factorization: \((x-2)(2x-1)(x+3)\), zeros \(2, \tfrac{1}{2}, -3\). Audit: \(f(2) = 16 + 4 - 26 + 6 = 0\) ✓.
▶  Try it yourself: Without dividing, find the remainder when \(f(x) = x^{50} - 3x + 1\) is divided by \(x - 1\). Then explain why no division scheme on earth would be a better method.
By the Remainder Theorem the remainder is \(f(1) = 1 - 3 + 1 = -1\). A synthetic division here would need a row of 51 coefficients (49 of them zeros); the theorem replaces all of it with one substitution. Knowing which tool is cheapest is part of the mathematics.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Synthetic division is often called Ruffini's rule, after the Italian mathematician Paolo Ruffini, who published it in 1809 — the same Ruffini who gave the first (nearly complete) proof that degree-5 equations have no general solution in radicals, closing the road that Tartaglia and Cardano's cubic triumph had opened. The compressed evaluation scheme is also called Horner's method, and it is still how software evaluates polynomials today: your calculator has been doing synthetic division all along, silently, every time you plot a curve.

Conclusion

Division is the unit's engine room. The division algorithm turns every quotient into a checkable identity; synthetic division makes dividing by \(x - k\) nearly free; the Remainder Theorem reveals that the leftover was a function value in disguise; and the Factor Theorem welds zeros to factors permanently — find one, own the other. You also learned this unit's deepest habit of intellectual honesty: audit by an independent road, because a flawless-looking computation can rest on a broken setup, whether the computer is silicon or you. Tomorrow the hunt begins in earnest: Lesson 2-4 asks how to find that first zero when nobody hands it to you — and answers with a theorem that narrows infinitely many suspects to a finite list.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. What multiplication statement does every polynomial division secretly make, and how does that statement let you verify any division in under a minute?

2. Why must the remainder on dividing by \(x - k\) be a constant — and why must that constant be \(f(k)\)?

3. In what precise sense are zeros and factors the same information? Which theorem says so, and what does each direction of its proof rest on?

4. Why can an error of setup survive perfect arithmetic, and what makes an independent check (rather than a re-reading) the only reliable audit?
