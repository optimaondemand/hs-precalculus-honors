🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to use the Rational Zero Theorem to list every possible rational zero of a polynomial, and explain where the list comes from.

  • I will be able to find all zeros of a polynomial by combining the candidate list, synthetic division, and the Factor Theorem.

  • I will be able to state the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra and its consequence for factoring, and count a polynomial's zeros with multiplicity.

📚 VOCABULARY

Rational Zero Theorem: If a polynomial with integer coefficients has a rational zero \(\frac{p}{q}\) (in lowest terms), then \(p\) divides the constant term and \(q\) divides the leading coefficient.

Fundamental Theorem of Algebra (FTA): Every polynomial of degree \(n \ge 1\) has at least one complex zero.

Linear Factorization Theorem: Every degree-\(n\) polynomial factors into exactly \(n\) linear factors over the complex numbers: \(f(x) = a_n(x - c_1)(x - c_2)\cdots(x - c_n)\).

Descartes' Rule of Signs: The number of positive real zeros of \(f\) equals the number of sign changes in \(f(x)\), or less than that by an even number; the same rule applied to \(f(-x)\) counts the negative zeros.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Yesterday's Factor Theorem left one question hanging over the whole unit: division can peel a factor off a polynomial once you know a zero — but who tells you the first zero? Guessing among infinitely many numbers is not a method. Today's first theorem converts the infinite guessing game into a finite checklist, and today's second theorem — proven by a 21-year-old in his doctoral thesis — guarantees the hunt can never come up empty.

Start with an observation about a factored polynomial with integer coefficients. If

\(f(x) = (3x - 1)(x + 2)(x - 1) = 3x^3 + 2x^2 - 7x + 2\)

then its zeros are \(\frac{1}{3}, -2, 1\). Look at where their parts ended up: every numerator (1, −2, 1) divides the constant term 2, and every denominator (3, 1, 1) divides the leading coefficient 3. That is no accident — when the factors multiply out, the constant term is built from the numerators and the leading coefficient from the denominators. Run the logic backwards and you have a suspect list:

The Rational Zero Theorem

If \(f(x) = a_n x^n + \cdots + a_1 x + a_0\) has integer coefficients, then every rational zero, written in lowest terms as \(\frac{p}{q}\), satisfies:

\(\Large p \mid a_0 \quad \text{and} \quad q \mid a_n\)

To build the candidate list: divisors of the constant term over divisors of the leading coefficient, with both signs.

Read the theorem honestly: it does not promise that any candidate is a zero. It promises that no rational number outside the list can be one. It is a suspect list, not a conviction — each candidate still faces trial by substitution (or synthetic division, which is the same trial with the quotient as a bonus).

Example 1

List all possible rational zeros of \(f(x) = 2x^3 + x^2 - 8x + 3\).

Solution:

Numerators \(p\) divide the constant term 3: \(\pm 1, \pm 3\). Denominators \(q\) divide the leading coefficient 2: \(\pm 1, \pm 2\). All quotients \(\frac{p}{q}\):

\(\pm 1, \quad \pm 3, \quad \pm\dfrac{1}{2}, \quad \pm\dfrac{3}{2}\)

Answer: Eight candidates — a finite trial docket, where a moment ago every rational number on the line was a suspect.

Example 2

Find all zeros of \(f(x) = 2x^3 + x^2 - 8x + 3\), exactly.

Solution:

Try candidates from Example 1. \(f(1) = 2 + 1 - 8 + 3 = -2\) — not a zero. Try \(x = \frac{3}{2}\) by synthetic division (coefficients \(2, 1, -8, 3\), \(k = \frac{3}{2}\)):

\(\begin{array}{c|rrrr} \frac{3}{2} & 2 & 1 & -8 & 3 \\ & & 3 & 6 & -3 \\ \hline & 2 & 4 & -2 & \fbox{0} \end{array}\)

Remainder zero: \(\frac{3}{2}\) is a zero, and the quotient is \(2x^2 + 4x - 2 = 2(x^2 + 2x - 1)\). The quadratic surrenders its zeros to the quadratic formula:

\(x = \dfrac{-2 \pm \sqrt{4 + 4}}{2} = \dfrac{-2 \pm 2\sqrt{2}}{2} = -1 \pm \sqrt{2}\)

Answer: The zeros are \(\dfrac{3}{2}, \; -1 + \sqrt{2}, \; -1 - \sqrt{2}\). Notice the division of labor: the theorem found the one rational zero, and dividing it out dropped the problem to a quadratic, where the quadratic formula handles the irrational pair. The candidates list could never have named \(-1 + \sqrt{2}\) — it is not rational — and that is not a failure of the theorem but the boundary of its jurisdiction, honestly drawn.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Building the ratio upside down: candidates are \(\dfrac{\text{divisors of the constant}}{\text{divisors of the leading coefficient}}\) — constant on top. Flip it and you will hunt for zeros of \(2x^3 + \cdots + 3\) among \(\pm 2, \pm\frac{2}{3}\), none of which can work. Anchor the direction with the simplest case: \(2x - 1\) has zero \(\frac{1}{2}\) — the constant's divisor 1 over the leading coefficient's divisor 2.

The hunt works — but could a polynomial simply have no zeros to find? For real numbers, yes: \(x^2 + 1\) never touches the axis. The great question of Cardano's century and the two after it was whether the complex numbers — those “impossible” quantities that kept surfacing inside the cubic formula — were enough to guarantee every polynomial a full set of roots. In 1799, Carl Friedrich Gauss made the guarantee his doctoral thesis. He was twenty-two, and he found the existing “proofs” so leaky that he spent the first half of the thesis demolishing them before building his own. (You met Gauss as a nine-year-old summing 1 to 100 in Unit 1; he will keep turning up. Mathematics has a short list of names like that.)

Key Fact: The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, and What It Buys

FTA: every polynomial of degree \(n \ge 1\) (real or complex coefficients) has at least one zero among the complex numbers.

The cascade: that single guarantee, run through the Factor Theorem over and over, factors everything. \(f\) has a zero \(c_1\), so \(f(x) = (x - c_1)q_1(x)\); the quotient \(q_1\) has degree \(n-1\) and (if \(n - 1 \ge 1\)) FTA hands it a zero \(c_2\); divide again; repeat until the quotient is a constant. The result is the Linear Factorization Theorem:

\(\Large f(x) = a_n (x - c_1)(x - c_2) \cdots (x - c_n)\)

Every degree-\(n\) polynomial has exactly \(n\) complex zeros, counted with multiplicity — no more, no fewer. The bookkeeping you noticed in Lesson 2-2 (multiplicities summing to the degree) was this theorem showing through. Some zeros may be complex rather than real — which is why a graph may show fewer than \(n\) intercepts — but the algebra never comes up short.

Example 3

Find all zeros of \(f(x) = x^3 + 2x^2 - 11x - 12\), and write its linear factorization.

Solution:

Candidates: divisors of \(-12\) over divisors of 1: \(\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 3, \pm 4, \pm 6, \pm 12\). Try \(x = -1\):

\(f(-1) = -1 + 2 + 11 - 12 = 0\) ✓

Divide out \(x + 1\) synthetically (\(k = -1\), coefficients \(1, 2, -11, -12\)):

\(\begin{array}{c|rrrr} -1 & 1 & 2 & -11 & -12 \\ & & -1 & -1 & 12 \\ \hline & 1 & 1 & -12 & \fbox{0} \end{array}\)

Quotient: \(x^2 + x - 12 = (x + 4)(x - 3)\).

Answer: \(f(x) = (x+1)(x+4)(x-3)\); zeros \(-1, -4, 3\). Three zeros for degree 3 — the FTA's ledger balances. Audit: the constant terms multiply to \((1)(4)(-3) = -12\) ✓.

Example 4

Before hunting, profile the suspect: use Descartes' Rule of Signs on \(f(x) = x^4 - 3x^3 + 6x^2 - 4x - 12\).

Solution:

Count sign changes across the coefficients of \(f(x)\): \(+ \to - \to + \to - \to -\) — changes at the first, second, and third steps: 3 sign changes, so 3 or 1 positive real zeros (the count can only drop by even numbers, because complex zeros will arrive in pairs — tomorrow's story). Now \(f(-x) = x^4 + 3x^3 + 6x^2 + 4x - 12\): signs \(+ \to + \to + \to + \to -\) — 1 sign change, so exactly 1 negative real zero.

Answer: Either 3 or 1 positive zeros, exactly 1 negative zero. A profile like this tells you where to spend your synthetic divisions — and if you find one negative zero, you may stop looking for a second.

💻 On Your Calculator: Shrink the Suspect List First 10 min

The Rational Zero Theorem for \(f(x) = 2x^3 + x^2 - 8x + 3\) produced eight candidates. Desmos can tell you which ones are even worth a synthetic division. At desmos.com/calculator (or below):

  1. Graph f(x) = 2x^3 + x^2 - 8x + 3 and click the \(x\)-intercepts. Desmos shows them near \(x = -2.414\), \(x = 0.414\), and \(x = 1.5\).
  2. Now cross-examine the candidate list \(\pm 1, \pm 3, \pm\frac{1}{2}, \pm\frac{3}{2}\) against the picture. Only \(\frac{3}{2} = 1.5\) sits on an intercept — test that one first, and skip the other seven entirely.
  3. The intercepts at \(\pm\)0.414-ish match no candidate — the graph is telling you those zeros are irrational, so expect the quadratic formula to finish the job (as it did, with \(-1 \pm \sqrt{2} \approx 0.414, -2.414\)).

This is the honest division of labor between tool and theorem: Desmos locates zeros approximately and instantly; the algebra certifies them exactly. A decimal on a screen is a lead, not a proof — \(0.414\) and \(\sqrt{2} - 1\) are different claims, and only one of them is mathematics.

▶  Try it yourself: Find all zeros of \(f(x) = x^3 - 6x^2 + 3x + 10\). Work it on paper before you peek.
Candidates: \(\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 5, \pm 10\). Test \(x = -1\): \(f(-1) = -1 - 6 - 3 + 10 = 0\) ✓. Synthetic division by \(x + 1\) gives quotient \(x^2 - 7x + 10 = (x-2)(x-5)\). Zeros: \(-1, 2, 5\); factorization \(f(x) = (x+1)(x-2)(x-5)\). Audit: constants \((1)(-2)(-5) = 10\) ✓.
▶  Try it yourself: A degree-5 polynomial has real coefficients and exactly two \(x\)-intercepts, one of which is a touch (multiplicity 2). Account for all five zeros the FTA promises.
The touch contributes a zero of multiplicity 2; the other intercept (a crossing) contributes at least 1. That accounts for 3 of the 5; the remaining 2 zeros are complex (not real), which is why the graph cannot show them. Total: \(2 + 1 + 2 = 5\) — the ledger balances. (Why must the missing zeros come as a pair? Tomorrow's lesson proves it.)
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra guarantees that every polynomial equation has solutions — it does not promise a formula for them. Cardano's century found formulas for degrees 3 and 4, and then the search stalled for 250 years. In the 1820s, Niels Henrik Abel proved the stall was permanent: for degree 5 and beyond, no general formula in radicals exists at all. The zeros are there (Gauss); a formula for them is not (Abel). That is why your method today — hunt a rational zero, divide, repeat — is not a classroom exercise but the genuine article: for most polynomials, intelligent hunting is all there has ever been.

Conclusion

Today the unit's machinery became a complete method. The Rational Zero Theorem turns an infinite search into a finite docket; synthetic division tries each suspect and hands you the quotient when one confesses; the quadratic formula finishes whatever quadratic remains; and behind it all, Gauss's Fundamental Theorem guarantees the count always comes out exactly \(n\), with the graph showing you the real zeros and the algebra owing you the rest. That phrase — the rest — is tomorrow's subject: the zeros that are not real numbers, why they always arrive in matched pairs, and how to build polynomials to order from any list of zeros you are handed.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Where does the Rational Zero Theorem's candidate list come from, and what exactly does the theorem promise — and refuse to promise?

2. How do the Rational Zero Theorem, synthetic division, and the Factor Theorem combine into a single method for factoring an unfactored polynomial?

3. What does the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra guarantee, and how does one zero's guarantee cascade into exactly \(n\) of them?

4. When a degree-\(n\) graph shows fewer than \(n\) intercepts, where are the missing zeros — and why is the graph unable to show them?
