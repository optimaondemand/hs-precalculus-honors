Graphing Rational Functions
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to determine a rational function's horizontal or slant asymptote by comparing degrees — and explain why the comparison works.

  • I will be able to produce a complete graph of a rational function by hand: intercepts, asymptotes, holes, sign chart, and branches, all in order.

  • I will be able to verify a hand-drawn graph with technology and reconcile any disagreement.

📚 VOCABULARY

Horizontal asymptote (HA): A horizontal line \(y = L\) that the graph approaches as \(x \to \infty\) or \(x \to -\infty\) — the function's long-run level.

Slant (oblique) asymptote: A slanted line the graph hugs at the far ends, occurring when the numerator's degree exceeds the denominator's by exactly one; found by dividing.

Sign chart: A number line marked at every zero and vertical asymptote, with the function's sign determined on each interval between them.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Yesterday answered the vertical question: what a rational function does near its forbidden inputs. Today answers the horizontal one — what it does in the long run — and the two answers together let you draw these graphs completely, by hand, with every feature certified. This is the skill your teacher grades closely this week, and it is graded on order: a rational graph is not sketched, it is assembled, feature by feature, each one justified before the curve is drawn.

Start with the long-run question. What does \(f(x) = \dfrac{2x^2 - 2x - 4}{x^2 - 9}\) do when \(x\) is enormous? Divide every term, top and bottom, by \(x^2\) — the highest power in sight:

\(f(x) = \dfrac{2 - \frac{2}{x} - \frac{4}{x^2}}{1 - \frac{9}{x^2}}\)

As \(x\) grows, every fraction with \(x\) downstairs melts toward zero — that is precisely yesterday's reciprocal behavior, reused — and the whole expression settles toward \(\frac{2}{1} = 2\). The graph flattens onto the line \(y = 2\): a horizontal asymptote. Notice whose 2 and whose 1 survived: the leading coefficients. The lower terms melted precisely because they were lower — the same leading-term dominance that ran Lesson 2-1, now running the quotient.

The Long Run of a Rational Function: Compare the Degrees

For \(f(x) = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\) with \(\deg p = n\), \(\deg q = m\):

  • \(n < m\): the bottom outgrows the top — horizontal asymptote \(y = 0\).
  • \(n = m\): a fair fight, settled by leading coefficients — horizontal asymptote \(y = \dfrac{a_n}{b_m}\).
  • \(n = m + 1\): the top wins by one degree — a slant asymptote, found by dividing: \(f(x) = (\text{line}) + \frac{r(x)}{q(x)}\), and the leftover fraction melts to zero at the ends, leaving the line.
  • \(n > m + 1\): no line contains it — the ends behave like the polynomial quotient (rare in this course, but the division still tells all).

The honest reading of an HA: it is a statement about the ends only. The middle of the graph may cross a horizontal asymptote — and often does. (A vertical asymptote can never be crossed: the function does not exist there. The two kinds of asymptote carry different licenses; confusing them is this lesson's classic error.)

Example 1

State the long-run behavior of each:   ① \(f(x) = \dfrac{3x^2 + 1}{x^2 - 4}\)   ② \(g(x) = \dfrac{2x}{x^2 + 1}\)   ③ \(h(x) = \dfrac{x^2}{x - 1}\)

Solution:

① Degrees equal (2 = 2): HA at the ratio of leading coefficients, \(y = 3\).

② Top degree 1 < bottom degree 2: HA \(y = 0\). (And note: \(g(0) = 0\) — the graph passes straight through its own horizontal asymptote at the origin. Legal, because the HA only governs the ends.)

③ Top degree 2 = bottom degree 1 + 1: slant asymptote. Divide \(x^2\) by \(x - 1\) synthetically (coefficients \(1, 0, 0\), \(k = 1\)): bottom row \(1, 1, \fbox{1}\) — quotient \(x + 1\), remainder 1, so

\(h(x) = x + 1 + \dfrac{1}{x - 1}\)

At the far ends the leftover \(\frac{1}{x-1}\) melts away, and the graph hugs the line \(y = x + 1\).

Answer: ① HA \(y = 3\)   ② HA \(y = 0\)   ③ slant asymptote \(y = x + 1\). One diagnostic — compare the degrees — three different fates. And see what ③'s division did: last week's synthetic division is this week's graphing tool. Nothing in this unit retires.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Treating a horizontal asymptote as a fence the graph may never touch. That is true of vertical asymptotes — the function does not exist there — but an HA is a claim about the far ends only, and the middle of the graph crosses it freely. In Example 2 below, the curve crosses its own HA at \(x = 7\), computed exactly. If your sketch refuses to cross an HA that the algebra says is crossed, the sketch is wrong, not the algebra. When in doubt, solve \(f(x) = L\): solutions are crossings, and they are ordinary points like any other.

Now the main event: the complete graph, assembled in disciplined order. This is the procedure to internalize — it appears on this week's Focus assignment and again on the unit test.

Key Fact: The Complete-Graph Procedure

  1. Factor numerator and denominator completely; record the domain; mark any holes.
  2. Intercepts: \(x\)-intercepts at the numerator's surviving zeros; \(y\)-intercept at \(f(0)\).
  3. Vertical asymptotes: the denominator's surviving zeros (yesterday's work).
  4. Long-run behavior: compare degrees — HA or slant line, drawn dashed.
  5. Sign chart: mark every \(x\)-intercept and VA on a number line; test one point per interval. The function cannot change sign anywhere else — the same fencepost logic as polynomial graphs.
  6. Assemble the branches: in each region, the signs, intercepts, and asymptote approaches admit exactly one reasonable curve. Draw it; check for HA crossings by solving \(f(x) = L\).

Every line of the procedure cites a theorem you own. That is what “complete” means: not a pretty curve, but a curve where every feature could survive cross-examination.

Example 2

Produce a complete graph of \(f(x) = \dfrac{2x^2 - 2x - 4}{x^2 - 9}\).

Solution:

1. Factor: \(f(x) = \dfrac{2(x-2)(x+1)}{(x-3)(x+3)}\). Domain: \(x \neq \pm 3\); nothing cancels, so no holes.

2. Intercepts: \(x\)-intercepts at \(2\) and \(-1\); \(y\)-intercept \(f(0) = \dfrac{-4}{-9} = \dfrac{4}{9}\).

3. Vertical asymptotes: \(x = 3\) and \(x = -3\).

4. Long run: degrees equal, HA \(y = 2\) (the opening computation). Crossing check: solve \(f(x) = 2\):

\(2x^2 - 2x - 4 = 2(x^2 - 9) = 2x^2 - 18\)

\(-2x - 4 = -18 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x = 7\)

The graph crosses its HA once, at \((7, 2)\), then re-approaches the line from below as \(x \to \infty\).

5. Sign chart: markers at \(-3, -1, 2, 3\) give five intervals; one test point each:

\(f(-4) = \frac{2(-6)(-3)}{(-7)(-1)} = \frac{36}{7} > 0 \qquad f(-2) = \frac{2(-4)(-1)}{(-5)(1)} = -\frac{8}{5} < 0 \qquad f(0) = \frac{4}{9} > 0\)

\(f(2.5) = \frac{2(0.5)(3.5)}{(-0.5)(5.5)} < 0 \qquad f(4) = \frac{2(2)(5)}{(1)(7)} = \frac{20}{7} > 0\)

Signs, left to right: \(+, \; -, \; +, \; -, \; +\).

6. Assemble: Left branch: positive, hugging \(y = 2\) from above at the far left, rising to \(+\infty\) at \(x = -3^-\). Middle: falls from \(+\infty\)… no — read the signs: on \((-3, -1)\) the function is negative, so the branch climbs from \(-\infty\) at \(x = -3^+\), crosses at \(-1\), arches over the positive stretch through \((0, \frac{4}{9})\), crosses down at \(2\), and plunges to \(-\infty\) at \(x = 3^-\). Right branch: falls from \(+\infty\) at \(x = 3^+\), dips to cross \(y = 2\) at \((7, 2)\), and approaches the HA from below thereafter.

Answer: Three branches, every feature computed above. (Notice the honest self-correction in step 6: the first instinct for the middle branch contradicted the sign chart, and the sign chart wins — it is computed evidence, not impression. Checking each branch against the chart before inking it is exactly what the rubric's reasoning criterion looks for.)

Example 3

Graph \(g(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 4x + 5}{x - 2}\), slant asymptote and all.

Solution:

Factor / domain: the numerator's discriminant is \(16 - 20 < 0\) — it does not factor over the reals, so there are no \(x\)-intercepts. Domain: \(x \neq 2\); VA \(x = 2\).

\(y\)-intercept: \(g(0) = \frac{5}{-2} = -\frac{5}{2}\).

Long run: top degree exceeds bottom by one — divide synthetically (coefficients \(1, -4, 5\), \(k = 2\)): bottom row \(1, -2, \fbox{1}\), so

\(g(x) = x - 2 + \dfrac{1}{x - 2}\)

Slant asymptote \(y = x - 2\), approached at both far ends; the leftover \(\frac{1}{x-2}\) also hands us the VA behavior for free: positive side up, negative side down.

Assemble: right of the VA, the graph sits above the slant line (the leftover is positive) — a branch falling from \(+\infty\), bottoming out, and climbing along the line. Left of the VA it sits below the line, arching down through \((0, -\frac{5}{2})\) from the line toward \(-\infty\). No axis crossings anywhere — consistent with the missing \(x\)-intercepts.

Answer: Two branches straddling the point where VA meets slant line, never touching the axis. The rewritten form \(g(x) = (x-2) + \frac{1}{x-2}\) told the entire story: line plus melting correction — a rational function is its long-run line plus a reciprocal-shaped disagreement, and the disagreement is exactly what yesterday taught you to read.

💻 On Your Calculator: Verifying a Complete Graph 15 min

This is the year's second designated calculator-craft lesson: verifying a multi-feature hand graph. At desmos.com/calculator (or below), rebuild Example 2:

  1. Line 1: f(x) = (2x^2 - 2x - 4)/(x^2 - 9).
  2. Lines 2–4: the asymptote frame — y = 2, x = 3, x = -3. Click each line's icon and make them dashed: claims, not curve.
  3. Audit your features one by one: click the intercepts (should be \(-1\), \(2\), and \(y\)-intercept \(0.444\ldots = \frac{4}{9}\)); trace the far ends onto \(y = 2\); confirm the crossing at \((7, 2)\) by clicking the intersection of Line 1 and \(y = 2\).
  4. Window discipline from Lesson 2-2 applies doubly here: the default window hides the HA crossing at \(x = 7\) and the left end's approach. Widen to \(x\) from \(-20\) to \(20\) before declaring victory.
  5. Then rebuild Example 3 with its slant: graph g(x) = (x^2 - 4x + 5)/(x - 2) and y = x - 2 (dashed). Zoom out and watch the branches close onto the line — the leftover \(\frac{1}{x-2}\) visibly melting.

The order matters: hand graph first, machine second. Used that way, every disagreement is a discovery — either your algebra slipped or your window lies, and finding out which is where the learning is.

Equal degrees: HA \(y = 3\). Solve \(f(x) = 3\): \(3x^2 + x = 3x^2 + 3\) gives \(x = 3\). Yes — one crossing, at \((3, 3)\), after which the graph approaches \(y = 3\) from… test \(f(10) = \frac{310}{101} \approx 3.07\): from above. The ends obey the asymptote; the crossing was the middle's business.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The rewriting trick behind slant asymptotes — quotient plus remainder-over-divisor — is one of the most reused ideas in higher mathematics. In calculus it splits rational functions for integration; in number theory the same maneuver on integers powers the Euclidean algorithm, the 2,300-year-old procedure that still secures modern cryptography. When you wrote \(g(x) = (x-2) + \frac{1}{x-2}\) today, you used the same lever Euclid did — division with remainder as a way of seeing structure, not just computing.

Conclusion

A rational function's graph is fully governed: the numerator's zeros place the intercepts, the denominator's zeros raise the vertical asymptotes, the degree comparison sets the long-run line — horizontal or slant — and the sign chart locks every branch into its region. Between the sign chart's fenceposts and the asymptotes' approaches, there is essentially one graph the evidence allows, and you can now draw it and defend it feature by feature. The graphs are done; what remains is to solve with these functions. Next week opens with rational equations and inequalities — where the forbidden inputs you have learned to respect become the source of a famous trap, and an AI will walk straight into it for you.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does dividing every term by the highest power of \(x\) reveal the horizontal asymptote, and which coefficients survive the melt?

2. What different licenses do vertical and horizontal asymptotes carry — which can be crossed, which cannot, and why?

3. How does polynomial division convert a top-heavy rational function into “line plus melting correction,” and what does each piece of that form control?

4. In the complete-graph procedure, what theorem or computation certifies each feature — and why does the sign chart outrank your drawing instincts?
