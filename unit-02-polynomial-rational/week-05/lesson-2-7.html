Rational Functions: Domain and Vertical Asymptotes
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to find the domain of any rational function and explain what happens at each excluded input.

  • I will be able to distinguish a vertical asymptote from a hole, connect each to the factored form, and find the coordinates of any hole.

  • I will be able to describe a graph's behavior on each side of a vertical asymptote using arrow notation, with sign analysis as evidence.

📚 VOCABULARY

Rational function: A quotient of two polynomials, \(f(x) = \dfrac{p(x)}{q(x)}\) with \(q(x) \neq 0\) — ratio-nal, a function built as a ratio.

Domain (of a rational function): All real numbers except the zeros of the denominator.

Vertical asymptote: A vertical line \(x = c\) that the graph approaches without bound — outputs rush to \(\infty\) or \(-\infty\) as inputs close in on \(c\).

Hole (removable discontinuity): A single missing point in an otherwise smooth stretch of graph, occurring where numerator and denominator share a factor.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

For four lessons this unit has multiplied polynomials' factors together. Today we divide one polynomial by another, and a genuinely new phenomenon enters the course: places where a function does not exist — and, stranger, places where it grows without any bound at all. The word for the lines a graph chases forever without touching is ancient: Apollonius of Perga, the same geometer whose Conics will anchor Unit 5, called them asymptωtos — “not falling together.” A curve and a line, approaching each other for eternity, never meeting: the Greeks found this worth naming, and after today you will too.

Begin with the simplest possible ratio, the reciprocal function \(f(x) = \frac{1}{x}\), and interrogate it near its forbidden input \(x = 0\):

\(f(0.1) = 10, \qquad f(0.01) = 100, \qquad f(0.001) = 1000, \; \ldots\)

\(f(-0.1) = -10, \qquad f(-0.01) = -100, \qquad f(-0.001) = -1000, \; \ldots\)

Divide 1 by an ever-tinier positive number and the quotient grows past every bound; approach from the negative side and it plunges. Nothing is “reaching infinity” — infinity is not a place — but the outputs exceed any target you name, and arrow notation says exactly that:

as \(x \to 0^{+}, \; f(x) \to \infty\)   and   as \(x \to 0^{-}, \; f(x) \to -\infty\)

The little superscript signs mean “from the right” and “from the left,” and the line \(x = 0\) is a vertical asymptote. The reciprocal-squared parent \(g(x) = \frac{1}{x^2}\) behaves differently in one telling way: squaring destroys the sign, so both sides fly upward. One parent splits at its asymptote; the other rises in unison — and the difference is even-versus-odd powers again, the same sign logic as Lesson 2-2's multiplicities.

Domain, Asymptotes, and Holes — All from the Factors

For a rational function, factor the numerator and denominator completely. Then:

  • Domain: exclude every zero of the denominator — before any simplifying. Cancellation changes the formula, never the domain.
  • Vertical asymptote at \(x = c\): the factor \((x - c)\) survives in the denominator after all common factors cancel. Near \(c\), the function is (nonzero) ÷ (approaching zero) — outputs explode.
  • Hole at \(x = c\): the factor \((x - c)\) cancels completely out of the denominator. The function is undefined at \(c\) but tame beside it — the graph has a single puncture, whose height is found by substituting \(c\) into the simplified formula.

One question decides everything: after cancelling, is the troublesome factor still downstairs? Still there — asymptote. Gone — hole.

Example 1

Find the domain of \(f(x) = \dfrac{x + 3}{x^2 - 9}\), and classify each excluded input as a vertical asymptote or a hole.

Solution:

Factor: \(f(x) = \dfrac{x+3}{(x-3)(x+3)}\). The denominator vanishes at \(x = 3\) and \(x = -3\); the domain is all reals except \(\pm 3\) — recorded now, before anything cancels.

Now cancel the shared factor \((x+3)\), leaving the simplified form \(\dfrac{1}{x - 3}\), valid everywhere except the punctures we noted.

At \(x = 3\): the factor \((x-3)\) survives downstairs — vertical asymptote.

At \(x = -3\): the factor \((x+3)\) cancelled away — hole, at height \(\dfrac{1}{-3-3} = -\dfrac{1}{6}\).

Answer: Domain: all reals except \(\pm 3\); vertical asymptote \(x = 3\); hole at \(\left(-3, -\frac{1}{6}\right)\). Two excluded inputs, two utterly different fates — and only the factored form can tell them apart.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Simplifying first and reading the domain from what is left. Cancel \(\frac{x+3}{(x-3)(x+3)}\) down to \(\frac{1}{x-3}\) and you will report the domain as “all reals except 3” — quietly resurrecting a point at \(x = -3\) where the original function never existed. Domain first, from the original denominator; simplify second. The simplified formula is a different function everywhere the cancelled factor vanished, and the hole is the honest record of the difference.

Example 2

Locate all vertical asymptotes and holes of \(g(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - x - 6}{x^2 - 4}\), with exact hole coordinates.

Solution:

Factor top and bottom:

\(g(x) = \dfrac{(x-3)(x+2)}{(x-2)(x+2)}\)

Excluded inputs: \(x = 2, -2\). The factor \((x+2)\) cancels — hole at \(x = -2\); the factor \((x-2)\) survives — asymptote at \(x = 2\). The simplified form is \(\dfrac{x-3}{x-2}\), so the hole's height is

\(\dfrac{-2 - 3}{-2 - 2} = \dfrac{-5}{-4} = \dfrac{5}{4}\)

Answer: Vertical asymptote \(x = 2\); hole at \(\left(-2, \frac{5}{4}\right)\); domain all reals except \(2\) and \(-2\).

Example 3

Describe the behavior of \(g\) from Example 2 on each side of its vertical asymptote, using sign analysis rather than a table.

Solution:

Work with the simplified form \(\dfrac{x-3}{x-2}\) near \(x = 2\). The numerator there is close to \(2 - 3 = -1\): negative and steady. The denominator changes sign across the asymptote:

From the left (\(x \to 2^{-}\)): \(\dfrac{\text{negative}}{\text{tiny negative}} \to +\infty\)

From the right (\(x \to 2^{+}\)): \(\dfrac{\text{negative}}{\text{tiny positive}} \to -\infty\)

Answer: as \(x \to 2^{-}, \; g(x) \to \infty\); as \(x \to 2^{+}, \; g(x) \to -\infty\). Spot-check the evidence with one value per side: \(g(1.9) = \frac{-1.1}{-0.1} = 11\) and \(g(2.1) = \frac{-0.9}{0.1} = -9\) ✓. Notice the craft: a sign argument settled the direction of an infinite quantity — you never needed the actual sizes, only who was positive, who was negative, and who was shrinking.

Example 4

Graph \(f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x + 2} - 3\) by transformation, and state its domain, vertical asymptote, and the horizontal line it hugs at the far ends.

Solution:

This is the parent \(\frac{1}{x}\) shifted left 2 and down 3 — the same transformation language you have used since Algebra 2, applying to this new family without modification. Everything the parent owns moves with it: the vertical asymptote \(x = 0\) slides to \(x = -2\); the horizontal guide \(y = 0\) that the parent's ends hug slides to \(y = -3\).

Answer: Domain: all reals except \(-2\); vertical asymptote \(x = -2\); ends hugging \(y = -3\) (a horizontal asymptote — named today, given its full theory tomorrow). Anchor points: \(f(-1) = 1 - 3 = -2\) and \(f(-3) = -1 - 3 = -4\), one on each branch.

💻 On Your Calculator: Asymptotes, Holes, and What the Machine Won't Show 10 min

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), put Example 2 on trial:

  1. Graph g(x) = (x^2 - x - 6)/(x^2 - 4). The branches flying up and down at \(x = 2\) announce the asymptote plainly.
  2. Now hunt the hole: the curve through \(x = -2\) looks perfectly unbroken. Desmos does not draw holes. Click on the curve at \(x = -2\), though, and it confesses: the point reads \((-2, \text{undefined})\). The puncture is real; the rendering just cannot spare the pixel.
  3. Make a table for the asymptote approach: click the + and add a table with \(x\) values \(1.9, 1.99, 1.999, 2.001, 2.01, 2.1\) and \(y_1 = g(x)\). Watch the outputs blow up with opposite signs on the two sides — your Example 3 sign analysis, in numbers.
  4. Older graphing calculators used to connect the two branches with a spurious near-vertical line — drawing across the asymptote as if the function existed there. Desmos is better behaved, but the lesson stands for every tool: the machine draws pixels; you certify existence. A graph window can neither display a hole nor prove an asymptote — only the factored form does that.

▶  Try it yourself: For \(h(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 1}{x^2 - 3x + 2}\), find the domain, all vertical asymptotes, and all holes with coordinates. Work it on paper before you peek.
Factor: \(h(x) = \dfrac{(x-1)(x+1)}{(x-1)(x-2)}\). Domain: all reals except \(1\) and \(2\). The factor \((x-1)\) cancels — hole at \(x = 1\), height \(\frac{1+1}{1-2} = -2\), so the hole is \((1, -2)\). The factor \((x-2)\) survives — vertical asymptote \(x = 2\).
▶  Try it yourself: Using sign analysis, determine the behavior of \(f(x) = \dfrac{x+1}{(x-4)^2}\) on each side of its vertical asymptote — and say what the squared factor changes.
Near \(x = 4\) the numerator is near \(5\) (positive); the denominator \((x-4)^2\) is tiny and positive on both sides — squaring erases the sign change. So \(f(x) \to +\infty\) from both directions: the two branches rise together, like the parent \(1/x^2\). Even powers downstairs act exactly like even multiplicities in Lesson 2-2: no sign change across the special input.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Why not simply let \(\frac{1}{0}\) be infinity? Mathematicians tried. The trouble is that the two sides of the reciprocal's asymptote disagree — \(+\infty\) from the right, \(-\infty\) from the left — so no single value keeps the function honest. Division by zero stays undefined not from timidity but from integrity: an answer that contradicts itself is worse than no answer. (In projective geometry there is a consistent way to glue the two infinities into one point — but that is a different geometry, with different rules, and a story for far down the road.)

Conclusion

Rational functions introduced the course's first forbidden inputs, and you learned that not all forbidden inputs are alike: a factor that survives in the denominator digs a vertical asymptote, where the graph flies unboundedly with directions you can settle by pure sign analysis; a factor that cancels leaves only a hole, a single honest puncture with computable coordinates. The rule of order is absolute — domain first, from the original; simplify second — and no graphing window can certify what the factored form proves. Yesterday's asymptote question was vertical: what happens near a forbidden input. Tomorrow completes the picture with the horizontal question — what these functions do at the far ends — and assembles everything into complete, hand-drawn graphs. That is this week's Focus material: bring today's factoring discipline with you.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why must a rational function's domain be read from the original denominator, and what does the simplified formula lose track of?

2. One cancelled factor makes a hole; one surviving factor makes an asymptote. What is the mechanism behind each fate?

3. How can sign analysis determine the direction of an infinite plunge without computing a single large value?

4. What can a graphing calculator show you about asymptotes and holes, and what must the algebra certify because pixels cannot?
