Modeling and Fitting Polynomial Functions
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to build a polynomial model from a geometric situation, state its domain of honesty, and solve questions with it.

  • I will be able to fit a polynomial trendline to real data in Excel and interpret what the fit does — and does not — certify.

  • I will be able to judge when a polynomial model is honest and when it merely fits: form from structure, predictions tested against sense, domain named.

📚 VOCABULARY

Mathematical model: A function chosen to stand in for a real situation, so that questions about the situation become questions about the function.

Domain of honesty: The set of inputs where the model's answers deserve trust — usually narrower than the function's algebraic domain.

Trendline (regression fit): The polynomial of a chosen degree that comes closest to a set of data points, computed by software.

Overfitting: Making a model complicated enough to match every wobble of the data — including the wobbles that are noise, not structure.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In 1613, Johannes Kepler — between working out the orbits of the planets — got married, bought wine for the wedding, and grew suspicious of how the merchant measured the barrel. The merchant poked a rod diagonally into the barrel and read off a number. Could one length honestly measure a volume? Kepler went home and wrote a small book, Nova stereometria doliorum vinariorum (“New solid geometry of wine barrels”), working out barrel volumes and discovering along the way which barrel shape holds the most wine for a given rod measurement. It is one of history's finest examples of the modeling instinct: a real question, a function built to answer it, and an honest reckoning of where the function can be trusted.

Today you do exactly that, with a problem you can hold in your hands. Take a rectangular sheet of card — say 20 cm by 16 cm. Cut equal squares of side \(x\) from the four corners, fold up the flaps, and you have an open-top box. Small cut: a wide, shallow tray. Big cut: a tall, skinny tube. Somewhere in between hides the box of greatest volume. Before any algebra — write down your guess for the best \(x\). Keep it; you will grade it shortly, and on this unit's applied task you will grade an AI's guess the same way.

Example 1

Build the volume model for the box, and state its domain of honesty.

Solution:

Define the variables in words first: let \(x\) be the side of each cut square in cm; then the folded box has height \(x\), length \(20 - 2x\) (two flaps left the length), and width \(16 - 2x\). Volume is the product:

\(V(x) = x(20 - 2x)(16 - 2x)\)

Expanding (do it once, to know the family): \((20-2x)(16-2x) = 4x^2 - 72x + 320\), so

\(V(x) = 4x^3 - 72x^2 + 320x\)

A cubic — of course a cubic: three lengths multiplied. The model's form came from the structure of the situation, not from choosing a curve that looked nice. That is the signature of an honest model.

Domain of honesty: the algebra accepts any \(x\), but the scissors do not. We need \(x > 0\) (there must be a cut) and \(16 - 2x > 0\) (the short side must survive), so:

\(0 < x < 8\)

Answer: \(V(x) = x(20-2x)(16-2x) = 4x^3 - 72x^2 + 320x\), honest on \(0 < x < 8\). Note what the factored form displays: the model's zeros are \(x = 0, 8, 10\) — the cuts that destroy the box entirely. Two of them are the fenceposts of the honest domain; the third (\(x = 10\)) is already fiction, since a 10 cm cut cannot even be made.

Example 2

Find the volume of the box when \(x = 2\), and use the model to grade the “always cut a quarter of the short side” rule of thumb (\(x = 4\)).

Solution:

\(V(2) = 2(20-4)(16-4) = 2 \cdot 16 \cdot 12 = 384 \text{ cm}^3\)

\(V(4) = 4(20-8)(16-8) = 4 \cdot 12 \cdot 8 = 384 \text{ cm}^3\)

The same volume — a genuine surprise, and a lesson: two very different box shapes (a low tray and a tall block) can enclose identical space, which means neither is obviously the maximum. The peak lies somewhere between them.

Answer: \(V(2) = V(4) = 384\) cm³. Where between 2 and 4 is the true best cut? The calculator moment below settles it — and grades your opening guess.

💻 On Your Calculator: Hunting the Maximum 10 min

At desmos.com/calculator (or below):

  1. Graph V(x) = x(20 - 2x)(16 - 2x), and add the domain fence by typing it as V(x) = x(20-2x)(16-2x) {0 < x < 8} — Desmos will draw only the honest part.
  2. Click the hump's peak. Desmos reports the maximum near \((2.94, 420.1)\): the best cut is about 2.94 cm, for about 420 cm³ — nearly 10% more than the 384 of \(x = 2\) or \(x = 4\). How close was your opening guess?
  3. Now solve \(V(x) = 350\) graphically: add the line y = 350 and click the intersections. Two honest solutions appear, near \(x \approx 1.66\) and \(x \approx 4.63\) — two different boxes holding 350 cm³. (The algebra of a cubic equation promises a third solution; it lies beyond \(x = 8\), outside the domain of honesty — a “solution” to the equation that is not a solution to the problem. Keeping those apart is the modeler's first duty.)

In the calculus you will learn to find that peak exactly, by hand — it is one of that subject's founding problems, and Kepler's barrels stood at its cradle. For now, the graph finds it and you certify that it makes sense.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Trusting a model outside its domain of honesty. Ask our volume model about a 9 cm cut — a cut that is physically impossible, since the 16 cm side only offers 8 — and it answers smoothly: \(V(9) = 4(729) - 72(81) + 320(9) = -36\) cm³. Negative volume. The function did not break; it was never a box outside \((0, 8)\). Every model you build this year gets its domain written next to it, at birth — an honest model knows where it stops being about anything.

Now the second half of the lesson: what if nobody gives you the dimensions — only data? Suppose you actually built five of these boxes, measured their volumes with rice and a measuring cup, and got slightly imperfect numbers (all real measurement is imperfect):

\((1, 250), \quad (2, 390), \quad (3, 415), \quad (4, 380), \quad (5, 305)\)

Software can find the polynomial of any chosen degree that comes closest to these points — Excel calls it a trendline, and it reports a statistic \(R^2\) between 0 and 1 measuring how much of the data's variation the curve captures. The mathematics of how (least squares) waits for a later course; the judgment of what to fit is today's skill, and no software supplies it.

📊 Try It in Excel: Fitting a Cubic Trendline 15 min

Open a blank Excel workbook (school Microsoft account, as always):

  1. Enter the five data points: cut sizes in A2:A6, measured volumes in B2:B6, with headers Cut (cm) and Volume (cm³) in row 1.
  2. Select the data and Insert → Scatter chart (points only, no lines — the data are the facts; the curve is a claim).
  3. Click the chart, then the + → Trendline → More Options → Polynomial, Order 3. Check Display Equation on chart and Display R-squared value on chart.
  4. Read the fitted cubic. Its coefficients should land reasonably near the true model's \(4, -72, 320, 0\) — the fit has recovered the physics from five noisy measurements, which is the genuinely wonderful thing about regression.
  5. Now, for the cautionary tale: change the trendline order to 4. \(R^2\) climbs. The curve hugs the points more tightly. Is the model better? Hold that question for the box below.

Why order 3? Not because it scored well — because boxes have three dimensions. The degree was decided by the structure of the situation before the data ever arrived. When the form comes from the physics and the coefficients come from the data, each is doing its honest job.

⚠️ AI CHECKPOINT: THE PERFECT FIT THAT LIES

A student showed an AI the five box measurements and asked what polynomial to fit. It answered:

“With five data points, I recommend a degree-4 polynomial trendline. A degree-4 polynomial has five coefficients, so it will pass through all five points exactly, giving you R² = 1 — a perfect fit that captures 100% of the variation in your data.”

Every sentence is factually true. The advice is still bad, and you now know enough to say why. A degree-4 curve through all five points must treat the measurement noise as shape — it will wiggle to honor every wobble of the rice-and-measuring-cup process. Its \(R^2 = 1\) certifies only that the curve memorized the data, not that it learned the box. Ask it to predict — the volume at \(x = 2.5\), or the best cut — and its answers chase noise; the honest cubic, scoring a humbler \(R^2\), predicts better, because its form was earned from the geometry. And ask the deeper question the AI never raised: what should the degree be? It reasoned from the number of data points; a modeler reasons from the number of dimensions.

The standing lesson: a fit statistic measures agreement with the data you have; honesty is about the situation the data came from. When an AI recommends a model, ask it — and yourself — where does the form come from? “It fits better” is not an answer. You will run this exact judgment on the unit's applied task, The Honest Box, with your own measured data.

Example 3

A different modeling shape: a shipping crate must have a square base and a height 3 feet greater than its base side. Build the volume model, state its domain, and find the base side for which the volume is exactly 200 cubic feet.

Solution:

Define variables in words: let \(s\) be the base side in feet; the height is \(s + 3\); the volume is

\(V(s) = s^2(s + 3) = s^3 + 3s^2, \qquad s > 0\)

Set the volume to 200 and gather everything on one side:

\(s^3 + 3s^2 - 200 = 0\)

A cubic equation — and last week built exactly the tool for it. Rational-zero candidates include \(5\) (a divisor of 200): test \(5^3 + 3(25) - 200 = 125 + 75 - 200 = 0\) ✓. Divide out \((s - 5)\) synthetically (coefficients \(1, 3, 0, -200\), \(k = 5\)):

\(\begin{array}{c|rrrr} 5 & 1 & 3 & 0 & -200 \\ & & 5 & 40 & 200 \\ \hline & 1 & 8 & 40 & \fbox{0} \end{array}\)

\(s^3 + 3s^2 - 200 = (s - 5)(s^2 + 8s + 40)\)

The quadratic's discriminant is \(64 - 160 < 0\): its two zeros are a complex conjugate pair — algebraically impeccable, and useless to a carpenter. The FTA's three promised solutions sort themselves into one honest answer and two that fail the domain.

Answer: The base is 5 ft — a \(5 \times 5 \times 8\) crate. Verify in the original situation, never just the equation: \(5^2 \cdot 8 = 200\) cubic feet ✓. Note where each rejection happened in writing: the complex pair was dismissed with its discriminant shown, not silently dropped. A modeler's solution accounts for every root the algebra produces.

▶  Try it yourself: A box is built from a 12 cm × 12 cm sheet by the same corner-cut fold. Build \(V(x)\) in factored and expanded form, state the domain of honesty, and compute \(V(2)\). Work it on paper before you peek.
\(V(x) = x(12-2x)^2 = 4x^3 - 48x^2 + 144x\), honest on \(0 < x < 6\). \(V(2) = 2(8)^2 = 128\) cm³. (Square sheet, so the factor \((12-2x)\) appears squared — a multiplicity-2 zero at \(x = 6\): the model touches zero volume there rather than crossing into fiction. The graph knows the geometry.)
▶  Try it yourself: Your Excel fit of noisy data returns \(R^2 = 0.97\) for a cubic and \(R^2 = 0.999\) for a degree-7 polynomial. Which do you report, and what two questions decide it?
Report the cubic. The deciding questions: Where does each form come from? (The cubic from the three-dimensional structure; the degree-7 from nothing but the scoreboard.) How does each behave between and beyond the data? (The cubic stays box-shaped; the degree-7 wiggles through noise and exits the data range steeply toward nonsense.) \(R^2\) rewards memorization; honesty requires structure.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Kepler's barrel book contained a result he found merely charming but posterity found prophetic: near a volume's maximum, changing the shape a little changes the volume almost not at all — you saw it yourself, with \(V(2) = V(4)\) flanking the gentle summit at 2.94. Fifty years later, that observation — at a peak, the rate of change is zero — became the cornerstone of the differential calculus. A wedding purchase, honestly questioned, opened one of mathematics' great doors.

Conclusion

Modeling is the unit's virtue made practical. An honest polynomial model earns its form from the situation's structure (three lengths, so a cubic), earns its coefficients from the given facts or the data, and carries its domain of honesty on its sleeve — refusing to answer questions about boxes that cannot exist. Fitting software and AI advisers can supply curves and scores in seconds; only the modeler can supply the judgment about where form should come from, and you practiced exactly that judgment against a confident, statistically flawless, wrong recommendation. Tomorrow the unit crosses its watershed: functions built not by multiplying factors but by dividing them — rational functions — where the places a function fails to exist become the most informative places on its graph.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. What distinguishes a model whose form comes from structure from one whose form comes from fit — and why does the difference show up most in predictions?

2. Why is a model's domain of honesty usually narrower than its function's algebraic domain, and whose job is it to say so?

3. What does \(R^2 = 1\) actually certify, and how can a perfect score mark a worse model?

4. A cubic equation from a real situation has three algebraic solutions and one honest one. What disqualifies the other two, and where in your written solution should each be dismissed?
