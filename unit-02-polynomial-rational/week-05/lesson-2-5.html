🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to use the Conjugate Pairs Theorem to find the remaining zeros of a polynomial when a complex zero is given, and explain why complex zeros of real polynomials must come in pairs.

  • I will be able to construct a polynomial to order from a list of zeros (real, repeated, or complex) and one additional point.

  • I will be able to sketch a polynomial from factored form, combining end behavior and multiplicity into one coherent graph.

📚 VOCABULARY

Complex conjugate: The conjugate of \(a + bi\) is \(a - bi\) — same real part, opposite imaginary part.

Conjugate Pairs Theorem: If a polynomial has real coefficients and \(a + bi\) is a zero (with \(b \neq 0\)), then \(a - bi\) is also a zero.

Repeated zero: A zero of multiplicity 2 or more — its factor appears more than once (Lesson 2-2's touch-and-flatten behavior).

Irreducible quadratic factor: A quadratic factor with no real zeros (negative discriminant); over the real numbers it will not break into linear pieces.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

When Cardano's cubic formula was applied to certain equations, something scandalous happened: square roots of negative numbers appeared in the middle of the computation — and then, if one had the nerve to keep calculating with them, they cancelled, leaving perfectly ordinary real answers. Rafael Bombelli, a Bolognese engineer, had that nerve. In 1572 he worked out the arithmetic of these “impossible” quantities and called his own idea “a wild thought.” The full story of complex numbers — their plane, their geometry, their courage — is Unit 9's to tell. Today we need just one of their habits, and it is a habit of scrupulous honesty: in any polynomial with real coefficients, complex zeros never appear alone. Each one arrives with its mirror image, and the pair does its work jointly and invisibly.

Why must they pair up? Here is the shape of the argument. Complex conjugation — flipping \(i\) to \(-i\) — respects addition and multiplication: the conjugate of a sum is the sum of the conjugates, and likewise for products. Now take a polynomial \(f\) whose coefficients are all real, and suppose \(f(z) = 0\). Conjugate the entire equation. Every coefficient, being real, is its own conjugate — the polynomial passes through the mirror unchanged — while \(z\) becomes \(\bar{z}\). The equation that comes out the other side reads \(f(\bar{z}) = 0\). The mirror image of a zero is a zero. \(\blacksquare\)

The Conjugate Pairs Theorem — and What a Pair Builds

If \(f\) has real coefficients and \(a + bi\) is a zero (\(b \neq 0\)), then \(a - bi\) is a zero too. Multiplying the pair's factors always produces a quadratic with real coefficients:

\(\large \big(x - (a+bi)\big)\big(x - (a-bi)\big) = (x - a)^2 + b^2 = x^2 - 2ax + (a^2 + b^2)\)

This is how a real polynomial keeps complex zeros without showing complex coefficients: it carries them inside irreducible quadratic factors. Over the real numbers, every polynomial factors into linear pieces (one per real zero) and irreducible quadratics (one per conjugate pair) — nothing else. The graph shows the linear factors as intercepts; the quadratic factors leave no mark on the axis at all.

Example 1

Given that \(2 + 2i\) is a zero of \(f(x) = x^4 - 10x^3 + 37x^2 - 68x + 40\), find all zeros.

Solution:

By the Conjugate Pairs Theorem, \(2 - 2i\) is also a zero. Build the real quadratic the pair generates, using the boxed identity with \(a = 2, b = 2\):

\((x-2)^2 + 4 = x^2 - 4x + 8\)

Divide it out of \(f\) by long division:

\(x^4 - 10x^3 + 37x^2 - 68x + 40 = (x^2 - 4x + 8)\,q(x)\)

First term: \(x^2\). Multiply and subtract: \(x^2(x^2-4x+8) = x^4 - 4x^3 + 8x^2\), leaving \(-6x^3 + 29x^2 - 68x + 40\).

Next term: \(-6x\). Multiply and subtract: \(-6x(x^2-4x+8) = -6x^3 + 24x^2 - 48x\), leaving \(5x^2 - 20x + 40\).

Next term: \(+5\). Multiply and subtract: \(5(x^2 - 4x + 8) = 5x^2 - 20x + 40\), leaving \(0\) — remainder zero, as the Factor Theorem demands.

So \(q(x) = x^2 - 6x + 5 = (x-1)(x-5)\).

Answer: The zeros are \(2 + 2i, \; 2 - 2i, \; 1, \; 5\) — four zeros for degree 4, the FTA ledger balanced. Notice what the graph of \(f\) shows: intercepts at 1 and 5 only. The complex pair works underground, shaping the curve's height between the intercepts without ever touching the axis.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Expanding the conjugate-pair product by brute FOIL and mangling the \(i^2\) terms. Use the identity instead: the pair \(a \pm bi\) always produces \((x-a)^2 + b^2\) — sum of squares, plus sign. Writing \((x-2)^2 - 4\) instead of \((x-2)^2 + 4\) for the pair \(2 \pm 2i\) silently converts your complex pair into the real zeros \(0\) and \(4\), and everything downstream is fiction. Check the discriminant of your quadratic: if it isn't negative, it doesn't hold a complex pair.

Now reverse the whole unit's direction. So far, polynomials have been handed to us and interrogated for zeros. But engineers, physicists, and test-writers constantly do the opposite: they know where a system must vanish and need the polynomial that does it. The Linear Factorization Theorem makes this construction almost mechanical — with one degree of freedom left over, the stretch factor \(a\), which a single extra point pins down.

Key Fact: Building a Polynomial to Order

  1. One factor per zero: a zero \(c\) of multiplicity \(m\) contributes \((x - c)^m\); a complex zero contributes its conjugate too (jointly: \((x-a)^2 + b^2\)).
  2. Check the degree: multiplicities must total the required degree — if they don't, the zero list and the degree are in contradiction, and honesty requires saying so.
  3. Attach the stretch factor: \(f(x) = a \cdot (\text{product of factors})\), then use the given point to solve for \(a\).
  4. Verify against every given fact — each zero and the point.

Without the extra point, infinitely many polynomials share the same zeros — every vertical stretch of the graph. The zeros are the skeleton; the point puts flesh at one location, and that is enough to determine all of it.

Example 2

Find the degree-3 polynomial with real coefficients that has zeros \(-1\) and \(2i\), and satisfies \(f(0) = 8\).

Solution:

The zero \(2i = 0 + 2i\) drags in its conjugate \(-2i\) — that is not optional if the coefficients are to be real. Zeros: \(-1, 2i, -2i\); multiplicities \(1+1+1 = 3\) ✓. The complex pair builds \((x - 0)^2 + 2^2 = x^2 + 4\):

\(f(x) = a(x + 1)(x^2 + 4)\)

Pin down \(a\) with the point:

\(f(0) = a(1)(4) = 4a = 8\)

\(a = 2\)

Expand: \(f(x) = 2(x+1)(x^2+4) = 2x^3 + 2x^2 + 8x + 8\).

Answer: \(f(x) = 2x^3 + 2x^2 + 8x + 8\). Verify: \(f(-1) = -2 + 2 - 8 + 8 = 0\) ✓; \(f(0) = 8\) ✓; and \(f(2i) = 2(8i^3)+2(4i^2)+16i+8 = -16i - 8 + 16i + 8 = 0\) ✓. Real coefficients throughout — the conjugate pair kept its bargain.

Example 3

Find the degree-3 polynomial with a zero of multiplicity 2 at \(x = 2\), a zero at \(x = -1\), passing through \((1, 4)\).

Solution:

\(f(x) = a(x-2)^2(x+1)\)

\(f(1) = a(1-2)^2(1+1) = 2a = 4\)

\(a = 2\)

Answer: \(f(x) = 2(x-2)^2(x+1)\). Sketch it mentally as a Lesson 2-2 audit: degree 3, leading coefficient 2 — falls left, rises right; crosses at \(-1\), touches at \(2\) from above (the graph is positive on both sides of 2 — test \(f(1) = 4 > 0\) and \(f(3) = 2(1)(4) = 8 > 0\) ✓).

Example 4

Sketch \(f(x) = -(x+2)(x-1)^2\) completely, then reconcile the sketch with the FTA's count of zeros.

Solution:

End behavior: degree 3, leading coefficient \(-1\): rises left, falls right. Zeros: \(-2\) (multiplicity 1, crosses), \(1\) (multiplicity 2, touches). \(y\)-intercept: \(f(0) = -(2)(1) = -2\).

Assemble: down from \(+\infty\) on the left, crossing at \(-2\), dipping below the axis through \((0, -2)\), rising to touch the axis at \(1\) from below, then falling away to \(-\infty\).

Answer: The FTA promises three zeros for degree 3, and the graph delivers all three as intercept events: one at \(-2\), and two at \(1\) — the touch is a double zero, two roots standing in the same spot. A graph shows fewer than \(n\) intercept locations either because zeros are complex (Example 1) or because they are stacked (here). Reading which is which — from multiplicities and degree — is exactly the accounting skill this week's problems drill.

💻 On Your Calculator: The Zeros the Axis Never Shows 10 min

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), watch a conjugate pair work underground:

  1. Graph Example 1's quartic: f(x) = x^4 - 10x^3 + 37x^2 - 68x + 40. Confirm: intercepts at 1 and 5 only — the pair \(2 \pm 2i\) is invisible.
  2. Now graph the pair's quadratic by itself: g(x) = x^2 - 4x + 8. It floats entirely above the axis — its minimum is at \((2, 4)\), never reaching zero. That hovering gap is what a complex pair looks like in real coordinates.
  3. Add a slider: type h(x) = x^2 - 4x + c and slide \(c\) from 8 down through 4. At \(c = 4\) the parabola kisses the axis — the complex pair merges into a real double zero at \(x = 2\). Below 4, it splits into two real crossings. One slider, three regimes: complex pair, repeated zero, distinct real pair — the discriminant \(16 - 4c\) changing sign before your eyes.

This is the honest geometry behind today's algebra: complex zeros are not exotic — they are what a parabola's roots become when it lifts off the axis, and the double zero is the exact moment of takeoff.

▶  Try it yourself: One zero of \(f(x) = x^3 - 5x^2 + 9x - 5\) is \(2 + i\). Find the other two. Work it on paper before you peek.
The conjugate \(2 - i\) is also a zero; the pair builds \((x-2)^2 + 1 = x^2 - 4x + 5\). Dividing: \(x^3 - 5x^2 + 9x - 5 = (x^2 - 4x + 5)(x - 1)\) (check: the quotient must be linear with leading term \(x\) and constant \(-5/5 = -1\); expanding confirms it). The zeros are \(2+i, \; 2-i, \; 1\). Audit: \(f(1) = 1 - 5 + 9 - 5 = 0\) ✓.
▶  Try it yourself: Build the degree-4 polynomial with zeros \(3i\) and \(-2\) (multiplicity 2) satisfying \(f(1) = 45\), in factored form.
\(3i\) brings \(-3i\); the pair builds \(x^2 + 9\). With \((x+2)^2\), multiplicities total \(2 + 2 = 4\) ✓. So \(f(x) = a(x^2+9)(x+2)^2\), and \(f(1) = a(10)(9) = 90a = 45\) gives \(a = \frac{1}{2}\). Answer: \(f(x) = \frac{1}{2}(x^2+9)(x+2)^2\).
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Cardano himself met complex numbers first — in Ars Magna he briefly considered the problem of splitting 10 into two parts whose product is 40, found the answer \(5 \pm \sqrt{-15}\), called the work “mental torture,” and declared the result “as subtle as it is useless.” Bombelli's “wild thought” a generation later was to take such numbers seriously enough to compute with them. It took another 250 years — until Argand and Gauss gave them a plane to live on — for the mathematical world to stop apologizing for them. When you reach Unit 9, you will see what Cardano couldn't: they were never useless; they were geometry waiting to be noticed.

Conclusion

This lesson completed the polynomial half of the unit's ledger. Complex zeros of real polynomials travel in conjugate pairs, working invisibly inside irreducible quadratic factors; repeated zeros stack multiple roots on one intercept; and between those two effects, every “missing” intercept on a graph is accounted for — the algebra never loses a zero, it only hides some from the axis. You can now run the machine in both directions: tear a polynomial down to its zeros, or build one to order from a zero list and a point. Tomorrow the machine leaves the workshop: Lesson 2-6 puts polynomials to work as models of real quantities — and asks the unit's virtue question in its sharpest form yet: when is a polynomial model telling the truth, and when is it merely fitting the data?

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why do complex zeros of real-coefficient polynomials always come in conjugate pairs — what does the mirror argument actually use?

2. How does a real polynomial “store” a complex pair without showing complex coefficients, and what does that storage look like on the graph?

3. When you build a polynomial from a zero list, why is one extra point exactly the right amount of additional information — no more, no less?

4. A degree-\(n\) graph shows fewer than \(n\) intercept locations. What are the two possible explanations, and how do you tell them apart?
