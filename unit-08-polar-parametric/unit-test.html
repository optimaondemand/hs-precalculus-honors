📋 BEFORE YOU BEGIN

This test gathers the whole unit into one sitting: polar-rectangular point and equation conversion, graphing circles, cardioids, limaçons, lemniscates, and rose curves, sketching and eliminating the parameter from parametric curves, and translating fluently among all three representations. It has three parts: Part A (fluency), Part B (reasoning — write these as arguments), and Part C (one synthesis problem connecting this unit to Unit 7).

Ground rules. Set aside a quiet block of about 90 minutes and complete the test in one honest sitting. Work alone: no classmates, no AI tools, no solution websites — this is the one place where the mathematics must be entirely yours. A calculator may be used for arithmetic only (evaluating a decimal, checking a computation); every setup, classification, general-solution reasoning, and step of written argument must appear on your page — the calculator may check a number, never supply the reasoning. Lesson pages and notes stay closed.

Write the honor line at the top of your first page and sign it: “I completed this test on my own, without help from people, notes, or AI tools.” Intellectual honesty is a quarter of the rubric — and in the unit whose virtue is wonder, honest work is what keeps wonder from curdling into shortcuts.

📝 PART A — FLUENCY (Lessons 8-1 through 8-8, 8-11)

Neat vertical work for every problem — one step per line, exact values kept exact, labeled answers.

1
Point conversion: ① Convert the polar point \(\left(3,\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\right)\) to rectangular coordinates, exact values. ② Convert the rectangular point \((-2,2\sqrt3)\) to polar coordinates, with \(r>0\) and \(0\le\theta<2\pi\).
2
Negative \(r\): Plot the point \(\left(-2,\dfrac{\pi}{4}\right)\) by finding its equivalent positive-\(r\) form, then convert to rectangular coordinates. Show both the negative-\(r\) reasoning and the final point.
3
Equation conversion, both directions: ① Convert the rectangular equation \(x^2+y^2=6y\) to polar form. ② Convert the polar equation \(r=4\cos\theta\) to rectangular form, and identify the circle's center and radius.
4
Cardioid: For \(r=2-2\sin\theta\), state the maximum value of \(r\) and the \(\theta\) at which it occurs, state the value of \(\theta\) at which the curve touches the pole, and name the curve's axis of symmetry.
5
Limaçon classification: Classify \(r=4+2\cos\theta\) (inner loop, cardioid, dimpled, or convex), showing the ratio test you used, and state its maximum and minimum \(r\)-values.
6
Lemniscate: For \(r^2=9\sin(2\theta)\), state the number of petals, and state the condition on \(\theta\) that must hold for \(r\) to be a real number at all.
7
Rose curve: For \(r=2\cos(3\theta)\), state the number of petals and the reasoning (parity of \(n\)) that determines it, and state the maximum length of each petal.
8
Parametric sketch and elimination: For \(x=2t+1,\ y=t^2-3\): ① complete a table for \(t=-1,0,1,2\) and note the direction of motion as \(t\) increases. ② Eliminate the parameter to find the rectangular equation.
📝 PART B — REASONING (write these as arguments)

These problems are graded primarily on the quality of your reasoning. Full sentences where explanation is asked; every claim earned, not asserted.

9
Classify and justify a limaçon. Consider \(r=3+2\sin\theta\). ① Compute \(r\) at \(\theta=0,\ \frac{\pi}{2},\ \pi,\ \frac{3\pi}{2}\), and state the maximum and minimum values of \(r\). ② Using the ratio of the constant term to the coefficient, classify this limaçon (inner loop, cardioid, dimpled, or convex), and explain in your own words what the ratio test is actually checking — what would have to be true of \(r\) for an inner loop to exist, and why your computed minimum in ① rules that out here. ③ Sketch the general shape (described in words is acceptable: is it "pinched" anywhere, or smoothly convex?) and justify your description from your four computed points.
10
Convert a projectile model and interpret it. A ball's motion is modeled parametrically by \(x(t)=20t,\ y(t)=15t-4.9t^2\) (SI units, \(t\) in seconds). ① Eliminate the parameter to find \(y\) as a function of \(x\), showing the substitution \(t=\frac{x}{20}\) explicitly. ② Identify the shape of the resulting rectangular equation and state its concavity. ③ Find the ball's range (the positive \(x\)-intercept of your rectangular equation), and separately find the flight time directly from \(y(t)=0\); show that \(x(\text{flight time})\) equals the range you found in ②. ④ In two or three sentences, explain why a rectangular equation, on its own, cannot tell you when the ball reaches any particular point on its path, even though it can tell you the path's exact shape.
📝 PART C — SYNTHESIS (this unit meets Unit 7)
11
Consider the limaçon \(r=1+2\sin\theta\).
① Find every \(\theta\) in \([0,2\pi)\) at which the curve passes through the pole (\(r=0\)), using exactly the general-solution-then-restrict method Lesson 7-8 taught for solving trigonometric equations: set \(1+2\sin\theta=0\), solve for \(\sin\theta\), identify the reference angle, place both solutions by quadrant (citing which quadrants give a negative sine), write the general solution with \(+2\pi k\), and only then restrict to \([0,2\pi)\).
② Interpret geometrically. In two or three sentences, explain what these two \(\theta\)-values represent on the curve itself — what does it mean, physically, for a polar curve to reach \(r=0\) at two different angles rather than just one?
③ Classify and cross-check. Using the ratio test from Part B, classify this limaçon (inner loop, cardioid, dimpled, or convex). Explain, in your own words, why a limaçon has an inner loop if and only if \(r=0\) has solutions at all — that is, why your answer to ① finding two genuine solutions is itself evidence for your classification in ③, not a separate, unrelated fact.
④ Name the connection. One or two sentences: which specific technique from Lesson 7-8 did you use in ①, and why would simply reading "where the graph looks like it touches zero" off a sketch not have been sufficient without that algebraic technique?
📊 GRADING RUBRIC

The whole test is graded against the standing four-criterion rubric, each criterion scored out of 10. Part A supplies most of the evidence for understanding and precision; Parts B and C supply most of the evidence for reasoning.

Criteria What excellent looks like Pts
Understanding of the material Polar–rectangular conversion, curve identification (cardioid, limaçon, lemniscate, rose), and parametric sketching/elimination all executed correctly and fluently, with notation exact throughout. 10 pts
Quality of reasoning The limaçon classification argument and the projectile interpretation both read as genuine arguments; the Part C connection to Lesson 7-8's general-solution method is made explicitly and correctly, not merely gestured at. 10 pts
Presentation & precision Clean vertical work throughout, aligned equals signs, exact values kept in radical or \(\pi\)-form where called for, pages in order — forty points of work a stranger could follow. 10 pts
Intellectual honesty Honor line signed and honored; work shown as actually done; any problem you could not finish carries an honest note of where and why you stopped — which earns respect here, never punishment. 10 pts
