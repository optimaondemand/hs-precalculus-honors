🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to write the parametric equations of a launched object's motion, given its initial speed, launch angle, and initial height.

  • I will be able to find a projectile's time of flight, maximum height, and horizontal range, and verify these values against one another rather than trusting a single formula alone.

  • I will be able to recognize when the shortcut "time to max height is half the flight time" applies, and when a launch height forces the general quadratic-formula approach instead.

📚 VOCABULARY

Parametric equations of motion: a pair of equations \(x(t)\) and \(y(t)\) that give an object's horizontal and vertical position as explicit functions of a single shared variable, time \(t\) — the same two-functions-one-parameter idea the last two lessons built, now carrying real physical motion.

Initial velocity components: the horizontal and vertical parts of a launch speed \(v_0\) fired at angle \(\theta\) above the horizontal — \(v_{0x}=v_0\cos\theta\) and \(v_{0y}=v_0\sin\theta\) — found by treating the launch velocity as the hypotenuse of a right triangle.

Time of flight: the duration between launch and landing, found by solving \(y(t)=0\) (or \(y(t)=\) the landing height, when it differs from the launch height).

Trajectory: the path traced in the \(xy\)-plane, recovered by eliminating \(t\) from the parametric equations — for projectile motion, always a downward-opening parabola.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In 1638, Galileo Galilei published Two New Sciences, the book that first argued something that sounds almost too simple to be a discovery: a thrown object's horizontal motion and its vertical motion do not interfere with each other at all. The ball you toss sideways falls at exactly the same rate as a ball you simply drop, even while it is also sailing forward. Galileo had no notation for a parameter \(t\) shared between two coordinates — that language came later — but his insight is precisely what a parametric model makes explicit: one clock, two independent readings, \(x(t)\) tracking a steady horizontal drift and \(y(t)\) tracking a falling body, both ticking off the same \(t\). The last two lessons built the machinery of parametric equations in the abstract, tracing curves and eliminating parameters. Today that machinery meets its oldest and most natural home: a thrown, kicked, or launched object, obeying nothing more exotic than constant velocity in one direction and constant acceleration in the other.

Why Position Splits Into Two Independent Motions

Ignoring air resistance, a launched object feels exactly one force after it leaves the launcher's hand: gravity, pulling straight down at a constant rate \(g\) (\(32\ \text{ft/s}^2\) or \(9.8\ \text{m/s}^2\), depending on which unit system a problem uses — never mix the two within one problem). Gravity has no horizontal component, so nothing ever slows or speeds the object's sideways drift: horizontal position grows at the constant rate \(v_{0x}\), with no acceleration term at all. Vertically, the object starts at height \(h_0\) with upward speed \(v_{0y}\) and is decelerated, stopped, and then accelerated downward by gravity — the familiar constant-acceleration formula from physics, written here as a function of \(t\). Splitting the launch velocity \(v_0\) into its two components with \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\) is exactly the right-triangle decomposition Unit 6 built; today it feeds directly into two independent equations of motion.

Key Fact: The Projectile Parametric Model

For an object launched with speed \(v_0\), angle \(\theta\) above the horizontal, from initial height \(h_0\), under constant gravitational acceleration \(g\):

\(x(t) = v_0\cos\theta \cdot t \qquad\qquad y(t) = h_0 + v_0\sin\theta\cdot t - \dfrac{1}{2}g t^2\)

Time of flight solves \(y(t)=0\) (or \(y(t)=\)landing height). Maximum height occurs when the vertical velocity is momentarily zero, at \(t=\dfrac{v_0\sin\theta}{g}\) — this instant is always valid, no matter what \(h_0\) is. Range, the horizontal distance traveled, is \(R = x(\text{flight time})\); the shortcut formula \(R=\dfrac{v_0^2\sin(2\theta)}{g}\) is only valid when launch and landing heights are equal (\(h_0=0\), landing at \(0\)).

Example 1 — Ground Launch, the Symmetric Case

A ball is launched from ground level at \(v_0=96\) ft/s, \(\theta=30^\circ\), with \(g=32\) ft/s\(^2\). Find the time of flight, the maximum height, and the range — then verify the range two ways.

Solution:

Components: \(v_{0x}=96\cos30^\circ=96\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2}=48\sqrt3\) ft/s; \(v_{0y}=96\sin30^\circ=96\cdot\frac12=48\) ft/s. So \(x(t)=48\sqrt3\,t\) and \(y(t)=48t-16t^2\).

Time of flight — solve \(y(t)=0\): \(48t-16t^2=0 \Rightarrow 16t(3-t)=0 \Rightarrow t=0\) or \(t=3\). Flight time \(=3\) s.

Maximum height — at \(t=\dfrac{v_{0y}}{g}=\dfrac{48}{32}=1.5\) s: \(y(1.5)=48(1.5)-16(1.5)^2=72-36=36\) ft.

Range, two ways. Directly: \(x(3)=48\sqrt3(3)=144\sqrt3\approx249.42\) ft. By formula: \(R=\dfrac{96^2\sin60^\circ}{32}=\dfrac{9216\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2}}{32}=144\sqrt3\approx249.42\) ft.

Answer: Flight time \(3\) s; maximum height \(36\) ft at \(t=1.5\) s; range \(144\sqrt3\approx249.4\) ft — the direct computation and the shortcut formula agree exactly, because launch and landing heights are both \(0\).

Example 2 — A Launch Height Breaks the Symmetry

A shot-put is released from \(h_0=1.5\) m, at \(v_0=20\) m/s, \(\theta=30^\circ\), with \(g=9.8\) m/s\(^2\). Find the time of flight and the maximum height.

Solution:

\(v_{0x}=20\cos30^\circ=10\sqrt3\approx17.32\) m/s; \(v_{0y}=20\sin30^\circ=10\) m/s. So \(y(t)=1.5+10t-4.9t^2\).

Time of flight — this no longer factors cleanly, because \(y(0)=1.5\ne0\). Set \(y(t)=0\) and use the quadratic formula on \(4.9t^2-10t-1.5=0\):

\(t=\dfrac{10\pm\sqrt{100+4(4.9)(1.5)}}{2(4.9)}=\dfrac{10\pm\sqrt{129.4}}{9.8}\)

The negative root (\(t\approx-0.140\) s) is not physical — time cannot run backward from launch. Keeping the positive root: \(t\approx\dfrac{10+11.375}{9.8}\approx2.18\) s.

Maximum height — still at \(t=\dfrac{v_{0y}}{g}=\dfrac{10}{9.8}\approx1.02\) s (this formula never depended on \(h_0\)): \(y(1.02)\approx1.5+10(1.02)-4.9(1.02)^2\approx6.60\) m.

Answer: Flight time \(\approx2.18\) s; maximum height \(\approx6.60\) m at \(t\approx1.02\) s. Notice: twice the time to max height would be \(\approx2.04\) s — close to, but not equal to, the actual flight time of \(2.18\) s. The next box explains why.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Assuming flight time is always double the time to maximum height. That shortcut is true only when the object launches and lands at the same height (a parabola symmetric about its own apex) — exactly Example 1's case, where launch and landing were both at \(0\) ft. Example 2 launched from \(h_0=1.5\) m above the landing surface, so the upward half of the flight (from \(1.5\) m to the \(6.60\) m peak) covers less vertical distance, and takes less time, than the downward half (from \(6.60\) m all the way down past \(1.5\) m to \(0\)) — the parabola is no longer symmetric about the moment of launch. The one formula that never depends on \(h_0\) is \(t_{\max}=\frac{v_0\sin\theta}{g}\), because it only asks when the vertical velocity is zero; the flight-time formula \(y(t)=0\) must always be solved on its own terms.

📊 Try It in Excel: A Time-Step Table for the Trajectory 15 min

Excel's trig functions expect radians, so convert degrees with the RADIANS() function. Rebuild Example 1 (\(v_0=96\), \(\theta=30^\circ\), \(g=32\)) as a time-step table:

  1. In A1 type t; in A2 type 0, in A3 type =A2+0.5, and fill down through row 8 (\(t=0\) to \(t=3\)).
  2. In B1 type x; in B2 type =96*COS(RADIANS(30))*A2 and fill down.
  3. In C1 type y; in C2 type =96*SIN(RADIANS(30))*A2-16*A2^2 and fill down.

Read down column C: \(0,\ 20,\ 32,\ 36,\ 32,\ 20,\ 0\) — height climbs, peaks at row \(t=1.5\) (matching Example 1's hand-computed maximum exactly), then falls back to \(0\) at \(t=3\), landing precisely on the flight time you already solved for algebraically. A table cannot prove a formula, but watching every row confirm it, one at a time, is its own kind of verification.

▶  Try it yourself: A ball launches from ground level at \(v_0=64\) ft/s, \(\theta=45^\circ\), \(g=32\) ft/s\(^2\). Find the flight time, maximum height, and range.
\(v_{0x}=v_{0y}=64\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2}=32\sqrt2\). Flight time: \(32\sqrt2\,t-16t^2=0 \Rightarrow t=2\sqrt2\approx2.83\) s. Max height at \(t=\sqrt2\approx1.41\) s: \(y=32\sqrt2\cdot\sqrt2-16(\sqrt2)^2=64-32=32\) ft. Range: \(x(2\sqrt2)=32\sqrt2\cdot2\sqrt2=128\) ft — matching \(R=\frac{64^2\sin90^\circ}{32}=128\) ft exactly, since \(h_0=0\) makes the shortcut valid here.
💡 CHECK YOURSELF WITH DESMOS
At desmos.com/calculator, switch to parametric mode (type a definition like (96cos(30°)t, 96sin(30°)t-16t²) — Desmos accepts the degree symbol directly) and set the \(t\)-slider's range to \([0,3]\). Trace the curve and confirm it peaks at \(36\) ft and lands exactly at \(t=3\), matching Example 1. Then adjust only the angle slider and watch the range change while the launch speed stays fixed — a fast, visual companion to the range formula's own \(\sin(2\theta)\) dependence.

Conclusion

Every number in today's lesson came from exactly two functions of one shared clock — nothing more exotic than the parametric habit the last two lessons built, now pointed at a real thrown object. Wonder, this unit's virtue, shows up here in an unassuming place: the same simple pair of equations describes a tossed ball, a fired arrow, a thrown shot-put, and (with the numbers scaled up enormously) an artillery shell, all obeying one shape of law. Tomorrow, that same time-shared-by-two-coordinates idea stretches further still — to two objects that might meet, a wheel that rolls, and a body that orbits — three more motions, one more habit of mind.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why can horizontal and vertical motion be modeled as two separate, independent functions of the same time variable \(t\)?

2. In Example 2, why did the flight time turn out to be more than double the time to maximum height, and what does that reveal about when the symmetric shortcut is valid?

3. Why must the range formula \(R=\frac{v_0^2\sin(2\theta)}{g}\) agree with directly evaluating \(x(\text{flight time})\) only when launch and landing heights are equal?

4. What does the Excel table's row of numbers reveal about the trajectory's shape that a single formula does not make as immediately visible?
