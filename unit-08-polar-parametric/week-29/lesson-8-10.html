🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to build a parametric model for a rendezvous (pursuit) scenario and determine whether, and when, two moving objects meet.

  • I will be able to apply the parametric equations of a cycloid to a rolling-wheel scenario and interpret the resulting height and span.

  • I will be able to build a parametric model for elliptical orbital motion, convert it to rectangular form, and identify the orbit's closest and farthest points from the center.

  • I will be able to use an AI tutor to generate additional motion-modeling scenarios, and independently verify its parametric equations by checking specific \(t\)-values by hand.

📚 VOCABULARY

Rendezvous (pursuit) problem: a problem asking whether, and when, two independently moving objects occupy the same point at the same time — requiring \(x_1(t)=x_2(t)\) and \(y_1(t)=y_2(t)\) for one shared value of \(t\), not merely a shared point traced at different times.

Cycloid: the curve traced by a fixed point on the rim of a circle of radius \(r\) as the circle rolls, without slipping, along a straight line — parametrized by \(x(t)=r(t-\sin t)\), \(y(t)=r(1-\cos t)\).

Orbital motion (parametrized): position modeled as \(x(t)=a\cos t\), \(y(t)=b\sin t\), tracing an ellipse of semi-axes \(a\) and \(b\) centered at the origin as \(t\) sweeps through one period.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

The cycloid — the curve traced by a nail on a rolling cart wheel — caused so much argument among the sharpest mathematicians of the 1600s that it earned an unofficial nickname: the Helen of Geometers, a curve so beautiful that it started fights. Pascal, Torricelli, Wren, and the Bernoulli brothers all took turns proving results about its arc length and the area beneath it, sometimes racing each other to publish first. Yesterday's lesson gave you the cycloid's parametric equations as one entry in a gallery of curves technology could trace. Today it, an orbit, and a rendezvous between two independently moving objects all get the same treatment: not curves to admire, but motions to model, solve, and interpret — the same one-clock, shared-parameter habit Lesson 8-9 built for a single thrown object, now applied to richer situations.

Same Clock, Different Paths

A rendezvous problem gives each object its own pair of parametric equations, both written in the same variable \(t\). Finding where the two objects' paths cross as bare curves — eliminating \(t\) from each and intersecting the resulting equations — only tells you where the two tracks overlap in space, the way two roads might cross at an intersection. It says nothing about whether the two objects are ever standing at that intersection at the same moment. A genuine meeting requires solving \(x_1(t)=x_2(t)\) and \(y_1(t)=y_2(t)\) simultaneously, for one common \(t\) — the parametric form's whole advantage is that it can ask this sharper, more honest question in the first place.

Example 1 — A Rendezvous

Two search-and-rescue boats' positions (in nautical miles from a fixed buoy, \(t\) in hours after they launch) are modeled by Boat A: \(x=5+3t,\ y=2+4t\), and Boat B: \(x=17-3t,\ y=16-3t\). Do the boats meet? If so, when and where?

Solution:

Set both coordinates equal at once. From \(x\): \(5+3t=17-3t \Rightarrow 6t=12 \Rightarrow t=2\). From \(y\): \(2+4t=16-3t \Rightarrow 7t=14 \Rightarrow t=2\). Both equations agree on the same \(t\) — that agreement is the whole test.

Answer: The boats meet at \(t=2\) hours, at position \((11,10)\) — check: Boat A gives \((5+6,\,2+8)=(11,10)\); Boat B gives \((17-6,\,16-6)=(11,10)\). Both agree, confirming a genuine rendezvous, not merely crossing tracks.

Key Fact: The Cycloid, Applied

Lesson 8-8 introduced the cycloid \(x(t)=r(t-\sin t)\), \(y(t)=r(1-\cos t)\) as the path of a point fixed to the rim of a circle of radius \(r\), rolling without slipping. Two facts follow directly from the formula and are worth carrying forward: the traced point reaches a maximum height of exactly \(2r\) (twice the wheel's diameter's radius, at the top of each arch, when \(t=\pi,3\pi,\ldots\)), and it returns to the ground — completing one full arch — every time \(t\) advances by \(2\pi\), covering a horizontal span of exactly \(2\pi r\), the wheel's own circumference. The point also comes to instantaneous rest each time it touches the ground, since the rim point at the bottom of a wheel rolling without slipping is not sliding relative to the surface at that instant.

Example 2 — A Rolling Wheel

A paint mark on the rim of a bicycle wheel of radius \(2\) ft traces a cycloid as the bicycle rolls forward. Find the mark's height above the ground after it has swept through \(t=\pi\) and \(t=2\pi\), and describe what each moment represents.

Solution:

With \(r=2\): \(x(t)=2(t-\sin t)\), \(y(t)=2(1-\cos t)\). At \(t=\pi\): \(x=2(\pi-0)=2\pi\approx6.28\) ft, \(y=2(1-(-1))=4\) ft. At \(t=2\pi\): \(x=2(2\pi-0)=4\pi\approx12.57\) ft, \(y=2(1-1)=0\) ft.

Answer: At \(t=\pi\), the mark is at the very top of its arch, \(4\) ft up (matching \(2r=4\) exactly) — the mark is momentarily moving at twice the bicycle's forward speed, straight ahead. At \(t=2\pi\), the mark has returned to the ground after one full revolution, having advanced \(4\pi\approx12.57\) ft — exactly the wheel's circumference \(2\pi r\), the mark momentarily at rest.

Key Fact: Orbital Motion, Parametrized

A body moving on an ellipse centered at the origin, with semi-axes \(a\) (horizontal) and \(b\) (vertical), can be parametrized \(x(t)=a\cos t\), \(y(t)=b\sin t\). Eliminating \(t\) with \(\cos^2t+\sin^2t=1\) recovers the familiar rectangular ellipse \(\left(\frac{x}{a}\right)^2+\left(\frac{y}{b}\right)^2=1\). One honest caution: this centered parametrization is a convenient model for practicing the algebra of translating between representations — it is not a true gravitational orbit, where the orbited body sits at one focus of the ellipse rather than at its center. Real orbital mechanics (Kepler's laws) is a richer problem for another course; today's version borrows the ellipse's shape without borrowing the full physics.

Example 3 — An Orbital Model

A satellite's position (in thousands of kilometers from a central point) is modeled by \(x(t)=8\cos t\), \(y(t)=5\sin t\). Find the rectangular equation of its path, and verify a specific point.

Solution:

\(\left(\dfrac{x}{8}\right)^2+\left(\dfrac{y}{5}\right)^2=\cos^2t+\sin^2t=1\). At \(t=\frac{\pi}{4}\): \(x=8\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2}=4\sqrt2\approx5.66\), \(y=5\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2}=\frac{5\sqrt2}{2}\approx3.54\). Check: \(\left(\frac{5.66}{8}\right)^2+\left(\frac{3.54}{5}\right)^2\approx0.5+0.5=1\). ✓

Answer: \(\dfrac{x^2}{64}+\dfrac{y^2}{25}=1\). Since \(a=8>b=5\), the modeled body is closest to the center (distance \(5\)) at \(t=\frac{\pi}{2}\) and \(\frac{3\pi}{2}\), and farthest (distance \(8\)) at \(t=0\) and \(\pi\).

⚠️ WHERE AI HELPS — AND WHERE IT DOESN'T
Ask your AI tutor: “Give me two more scenarios like today's lesson — one a pursuit or rendezvous problem, one an orbital-motion problem — with their parametric equations.” This is work AI does readily: it can generate plausible-sounding position functions all day. But a plausible-looking pair of parametric equations is not the same as a correct one. Before you trust anything it hands you, audit it: pick two or three specific \(t\)-values, compute both objects' positions by hand exactly as in Example 1, and confirm the AI's claimed meeting point (or orbital shape) actually holds up under those numbers. If the AI claims two objects meet, verify both coordinates agree at the same \(t\) — not just that the paths look like they cross on a sketch. The AI supplies scenarios quickly; you supply the check that makes them trustworthy.
▶  Try it yourself: Ship C is at \(x=4t,\ y=3t\); Ship D is at \(x=20-2t,\ y=5+4t\). Do they meet?
From \(x\): \(4t=20-2t \Rightarrow 6t=20 \Rightarrow t=\frac{10}{3}\approx3.33\). From \(y\): \(3t=5+4t \Rightarrow -t=5 \Rightarrow t=-5\). The two equations demand different, inconsistent values of \(t\) — there is no single moment at which both coordinates agree, so the ships do not meet, regardless of whether their plotted tracks happen to cross somewhere on a map.

Then graph Example 3's orbit as (8cos(t), 5sin(t)) and trace to confirm the closest and farthest points from the origin match your hand computation.

Conclusion

A rendezvous at sea, a mark on a rolling wheel, and a body sweeping out an orbit look like three unrelated stories. Written as parametric equations, they are one story: a position that answers to a shared clock. That is the whole discipline this lesson practiced — and the discipline this unit's wonder has been quietly building toward all along, from a single point on a circle to a full curriculum of motion. Tomorrow, the unit's last new content asks a different kind of question: given one curve or one motion, which of the three languages this unit has built — rectangular, polar, or parametric — tells its story best, and how do you move fluently among all three?

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why must a rendezvous problem require \(x_1(t)=x_2(t)\) and \(y_1(t)=y_2(t)\) at the same \(t\), rather than merely finding where the two paths cross as curves?

2. What does the cycloid's parametric formula reveal about a point on a rolling wheel that a single rectangular equation would not make as visible?

3. In the AI-tutor exercise, what specific \(t\)-values would you check to verify a claimed parametric model, and why does checking a few values disprove a wrong model faster than it can prove a correct one?

4. Why is the centered ellipse \(x=a\cos t,\ y=b\sin t\) an honest algebra exercise but not a fully honest picture of a real gravitational orbit?
