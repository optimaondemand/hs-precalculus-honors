🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to translate a single curve or motion among rectangular, polar, and parametric descriptions.

  • I will be able to explain what each representation reveals — and what it hides — about the same curve or moving point.

  • I will be able to write a short, reasoned explanation defending my choice of representation for a specific question about a curve.

📚 VOCABULARY

Representation (of a curve): one of the equivalent ways of describing the same set of points or motion — rectangular (an equation in \(x\) and \(y\)), polar (an equation in \(r\) and \(\theta\), measured from the pole), or parametric (a pair of functions \(x(t)\), \(y(t)\) of a shared parameter).

Translation (between representations): the algebraic process of rewriting a curve's description from one language into another while preserving every point it contains — not a new curve, the same curve, spoken differently.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

As the well-known story goes, René Descartes lay ill in bed one morning, watching a fly crawl across the ceiling tiles, when it struck him that he could name the fly's exact position with just two numbers — a distance along one wall, a distance along the other. Whether or not the fly ever actually landed, the invention it stands for is real and it is the one this whole unit has been quietly complicating: the plane does not owe its points to only one coordinate system. Lesson 8-1 gave the plane a second address system built from a distance and an angle. The last two lessons gave it a third: a curve traced not all at once but one instant of time after another. Today closes Unit 8 by putting all three languages on the table together, for the very same curve, and asking the only question that actually matters when you have more than one honest way to say something: which one answers your question best?

One Point, Three Languages

Consider a point \(P\) moving on a circle of radius \(5\), centered at the origin, completing one revolution every \(2\pi\) seconds, starting at \((5,0)\).

Parametric: \(x(t)=5\cos t,\ y(t)=5\sin t\), where \(t\) is time in seconds. This form answers where is the point right now? instantly — plug in a time, read off a position.

Rectangular: eliminate \(t\) using \(\cos^2t+\sin^2t=1\): \(x^2+y^2=25\). This form is the one to hand to algebra — intercepts, distance, intersection with a line — but it says nothing at all about time; a snapshot of the whole path, with the clock removed.

Polar: every point on this circle sits at distance \(5\) from the pole, regardless of angle — so the equation is simply \(r=5\). No \(\theta\) even appears. Polar reveals, in the shortest possible sentence, the one fact this circle is built entirely out of: constant distance from a center.

The centered circle above is almost too cooperative — every representation looks clean. A circle that happens to pass through the pole itself is a better test of real translation.

Example 1 — Translating a Circle Through the Pole

A curve is given in polar form: \(r=6\cos\theta\). Find its rectangular equation and a parametric form, and verify both at \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{4}\).

Solution:

To rectangular (Lesson 8-2's technique): multiply both sides by \(r\): \(r^2=6r\cos\theta \Rightarrow x^2+y^2=6x \Rightarrow (x-3)^2+y^2=9\) — a circle of radius \(3\) centered at \((3,0)\), passing through the pole.

To parametric: substitute \(r=6\cos\theta\) directly into \(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta\), using \(\theta\) itself as the shared parameter: \(x(\theta)=6\cos^2\theta=3+3\cos2\theta\) and \(y(\theta)=6\sin\theta\cos\theta=3\sin2\theta\), for \(0\le\theta<\pi\) (the curve closes after only half the usual sweep, since doubling the angle inside already completes a full lap by \(\theta=\pi\)).

Verify at \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{4}\): polar gives \(r=6\cos\frac{\pi}{4}=3\sqrt2\), point \(\left(3\sqrt2\cos\frac{\pi}{4},\,3\sqrt2\sin\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=(3,3)\). Rectangular check: \((3-3)^2+3^2=9\). ✓ Parametric check: \(x=3+3\cos\frac{\pi}{2}=3+0=3\), \(y=3\sin\frac{\pi}{2}=3\). ✓ All three agree.

Answer: \((x-3)^2+y^2=9\) (rectangular); \(x=3+3\cos2\theta,\ y=3\sin2\theta,\ 0\le\theta<\pi\) (parametric) — both describing exactly the curve \(r=6\cos\theta\) describes, with nothing added and nothing lost.

What Each Language Makes Easy to See

Language Makes easy to see…
Parametric Where a point is at a given time; direction of motion; whether two moving objects ever meet.
Polar Distance from one fixed pole; rotational symmetry; curves built around a center (circles through the pole, cardioids, roses).
Rectangular Intercepts, slopes, and direct algebraic manipulation; the natural form for solving systems and applying calculus later.
✍ IN YOUR OWN WORDS
In \(4\)–\(6\) sentences, answer: for the curve \(r=6\cos\theta\) from Example 1, which representation would you reach for first to answer each of these questions, and why — (a) "At what value of \(\theta\) does the traced point first pass through the pole?" (b) "What is the farthest this curve ever gets from the origin?" (c) "Is the point \((1,2)\) on this curve?" There is no single correct representation to name for all three — the point of the exercise is to defend why your choice fits each specific question.
💡 CHECK YOURSELF WITH DESMOS
At desmos.com/calculator, graph Example 1's polar form directly — type r=6cos(θ) and Desmos will offer polar mode. In a second graph, switch to parametric mode and type (3+3cos(2t), 3sin(2t)) with \(t\) restricted to \([0,\pi)\). The two curves should sit exactly on top of each other — the same circle, drawn by two different instructions.
▶  Try it yourself: Write the rectangular and parametric forms of the polar curve \(r=2\).
Rectangular: \(r^2=4 \Rightarrow x^2+y^2=4\). Parametric: \(x=2\cos t,\ y=2\sin t\), \(0\le t<2\pi\). This is the "cooperative" case from the lesson's opening — every language stays simple because the circle is centered at the pole.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Assuming every curve looks equally clean in all three languages, and translating out of habit rather than purpose. Take the rectangular parabola \(y=x^2\): substituting \(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta\) gives \(r\sin\theta=r^2\cos^2\theta\), and dividing by \(r\) (valid since we can treat \(r=0\), the pole, as a separate point already on the curve) leaves \(r=\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos^2\theta}\) — a technically correct but genuinely uglier description than \(y=x^2\) itself. Translation is a skill you reach for when a question calls for it — not a ritual to perform on every curve regardless of whether the result helps.

Conclusion

Rectangular, polar, and parametric are not competing theories about the plane — they are one plane, described three honest ways, and the whole of Unit 8 has been the project of building fluency in all three and the judgment to choose among them. That judgment is itself a small act of wonder: the same circle, spoken of as an equation, a distance, or a motion in time, does not become three circles. It reveals that the plane was always richer than any single language could show on its own. Thursday's test gathers this unit — circles, cardioids, limaçons, lemniscates, roses, parametric curves, and today's translations — into one sitting. And the plane itself is not finished revealing new languages: next unit, a number that "cannot" exist finds a home on this same plane, wearing both a rectangular coat and a polar one.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. In the \(r=6\cos\theta\) example, why does the parametric form use \(\theta\) itself as the parameter instead of introducing a fresh variable \(t\)?

2. Why is \(r=5\) alone a complete polar description of a circle centered at the pole, while the same circle's rectangular equation must include both \(x^2\) and \(y^2\)?

3. Give one example (from today's lesson or your own) of a curve whose polar form is messier than its rectangular form. What does that tell you about "translate between all three" as a skill versus a rule to apply blindly?

4. In your "In Your Own Words" response, which representation did you choose for each of the three questions, and why?
