Limaçons and Lemniscates

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to classify a limaçon \(r=a\pm b\cos\theta\) (or \(a \pm b\sin\theta\)) as having an inner loop, being a cardioid, being dimpled, or being convex, by reasoning from the ratio \(\dfrac{a}{b}\).

  • I will be able to graph a lemniscate \(r^2=a^2\cos2\theta\) (or \(a^2\sin2\theta\)), correctly identifying the angles where the curve is undefined and where its two loops cross the pole.

  • I will be able to build a short table of values and use symmetry to sketch a limaçon or lemniscate by hand, then confirm the sketch with a graphing calculator.

📚 VOCABULARY

Limaçon: a polar curve of the form \(r=a\pm b\cos\theta\) or \(r=a\pm b\sin\theta\), whose shape — a loop, a dimple, or a smooth convex curve — is governed entirely by the ratio of \(a\) to \(b\). French for “snail,” after its coiled, shell-like sweep.

Inner loop: a second, smaller loop that a limaçon draws inside its outer loop, produced whenever \(r\) becomes negative for some values of \(\theta\).

Dimpled limaçon: a limaçon with a shallow indentation near the pole, but no full inner loop — \(r\) stays positive throughout, but dips close enough to the pole to pinch the curve inward on one side.

Convex limaçon: a limaçon with no dimple and no loop at all — a smooth, egg-like curve that never indents.

Lemniscate: a polar curve of the form \(r^2=a^2\cos2\theta\) or \(r^2=a^2\sin2\theta\), tracing a figure-eight through the pole. From the Latin \(\textit{lemniscatus}\), “adorned with ribbons.”

Pole: the polar coordinate system's origin — the fixed point every value of \(r\) is measured from.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Yesterday's cardioid was a single, tidy special case: \(r=a\pm a\cos\theta\), the two constants forced into perfect balance. Loosen that balance — let \(a\) and \(b\) be any two positive numbers, equal or not — and the same family of equation opens onto a whole gallery of shapes: curves that swell into a smooth oval, curves that pinch into a shy little dimple, and curves that swallow themselves into a loop within a loop. The seventeenth-century French mathematician Étienne Pascal — father of the more famous Blaise — was among the first to study this family closely, and his colleague Gilles de Roberval gave it the name it still carries: limaçon, French for “snail,” for the way its outer curve coils back on itself like a shell. What determines which snail you get — loop, dimple, or smooth curve — turns out to be nothing more exotic than a single ratio.

The Limaçon Family: One Ratio, Four Shapes

For \(r = a \pm b\cos\theta\) or \(r = a \pm b\sin\theta\), with \(a,b>0\), the shape depends only on the ratio \(\dfrac{a}{b}\):

\(\dfrac{a}{b}<1\): inner loop   —   \(\dfrac{a}{b}=1\): cardioid   —   \(1<\dfrac{a}{b}<2\): dimpled limaçon   —   \(\dfrac{a}{b}\ge2\): convex limaçon

The reasoning behind the first boundary is direct: a limaçon draws an inner loop exactly when \(r\) can equal \(0\) or go negative for some \(\theta\) — that happens precisely when \(b>a\), since then \(a - b\cos\theta\) (say) can dip below zero as \(\cos\theta\) approaches \(1\). When \(a=b\), \(r\) touches \(0\) at exactly one angle without ever going negative — the cardioid's single sharp point. When \(a>b\), \(r\) never reaches \(0\) at all, and the curve stays a single, unbroken loop around the pole — smoothly dimpled if \(a\) is not too much larger than \(b\), fully convex once \(a\) is at least twice \(b\).

Example 1

Classify \(r=2+3\cos\theta\), and find the angles where the curve crosses the pole.

Solution:

Here \(a=2\), \(b=3\), so \(\dfrac{a}{b}=\dfrac23<1\): an inner loop. Find where \(r=0\):

\(2+3\cos\theta=0 \;\Rightarrow\; \cos\theta=-\dfrac23 \;\Rightarrow\; \theta\approx131.8^\circ,\ 228.2^\circ\)

Answer: Limaçon with an inner loop. Between \(\theta\approx131.8^\circ\) and \(\theta\approx228.2^\circ\), \(\cos\theta<-\frac23\), so \(r<0\) — those negative-\(r\) points plot on the opposite side of the pole from \(\theta\), and it is exactly this reflected stretch that draws the small loop nested inside the larger one.

Example 2

Classify \(r=3+2\cos\theta\).

Solution:

Here \(a=3\), \(b=2\), so \(\dfrac{a}{b}=1.5\), which sits strictly between \(1\) and \(2\). Check that \(r\) never reaches \(0\): the smallest possible value of \(r\) occurs at \(\theta=\pi\), where \(\cos\theta=-1\), giving \(r=3-2=1>0\).

Answer: Dimpled limaçon. Because \(r\) stays positive everywhere, there is no inner loop — but because \(a\) is not yet twice \(b\), the curve is not fully rounded either; it pinches into a shallow dimple near \(\theta=\pi\), where \(r\) is at its smallest.

Example 3

Classify \(r=2+\cos\theta\).

Solution:

Here \(a=2\), \(b=1\), so \(\dfrac{a}{b}=2\) — exactly the boundary value.

Answer: Convex limaçon. At the boundary \(\frac{a}{b}=2\), the curve is exactly rounded, with no trace of a dimple remaining — any larger ratio only smooths the curve further into an ever-more circular oval.

Key Fact: The Lemniscate

A lemniscate has the signature

\(r^2=a^2\cos2\theta \qquad \text{or} \qquad r^2=a^2\sin2\theta\)

Solving for \(r\) gives \(r=\pm a\sqrt{\cos2\theta}\) (or \(\pm a\sqrt{\sin2\theta}\)) — and because a square root of a negative number is not a real number, the curve is only defined where the expression under the root is nonnegative: \(\cos2\theta\ge0\) (respectively \(\sin2\theta\ge0\)). Outside those angles, the equation simply has no real points to plot — the curve does not exist there at all, rather than existing but being hard to see. The two \(\pm\) signs at each valid angle trace the curve's two lobes, meeting at the pole where \(r=0\).

Example 4

Graph \(r^2=4\cos2\theta\): find its domain, build a short table, and describe the resulting shape.

Solution:

Here \(a^2=4\), so \(a=2\). The curve is defined only where \(\cos2\theta\ge0\), which happens for \(\theta\in\left[-\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{\pi}{4}\right]\) and \(\theta\in\left[\frac{3\pi}{4},\frac{5\pi}{4}\right]\) within one full turn. A short table over the first interval:

\(\theta=0:\ r^2=4\cos0=4 \Rightarrow r=\pm2 \qquad \theta=\tfrac{\pi}{8}:\ r^2=4\cos\tfrac{\pi}{4}\approx2.83 \Rightarrow r\approx\pm1.68 \qquad \theta=\tfrac{\pi}{4}:\ r^2=4\cos\tfrac{\pi}{2}=0 \Rightarrow r=0\)

Answer: A horizontal figure-eight lying along the polar axis: one loop swept out by \(\theta\in\left[-\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{\pi}{4}\right]\) on the right, and by symmetry a second loop for \(\theta\in\left[\frac{3\pi}{4},\frac{5\pi}{4}\right]\) on the left, the two loops meeting only at the pole. No points exist for \(\theta\) between \(\frac{\pi}{4}\) and \(\frac{3\pi}{4}\) (or their reflections) — the curve is simply absent from those directions.

Example 5

For \(r^2=9\sin2\theta\), state the interval(s) of \(\theta\) (within \([0,2\pi)\)) where the curve is defined, without plotting points.

Solution:

The curve requires \(\sin2\theta\ge0\). Over \(2\theta\in[0,4\pi)\), sine is nonnegative on \([0,\pi]\) and \([2\pi,3\pi]\). Dividing back by \(2\):

Answer: \(\theta\in\left[0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right]\cup\left[\pi,\frac{3\pi}{2}\right]\). The two lobes of this lemniscate lie along the diagonal directions \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{4}\) and \(\theta=\frac{5\pi}{4}\) — the sine-based lemniscate is simply the cosine-based one from Example 4, rotated \(45^\circ\).

💻 On Your Calculator: Graphing in Polar Mode

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), type r = 2 + 3cos(theta) directly. Desmos recognizes an equation written in \(r\) and \(\theta\) (typed as theta) as a polar curve and plots it automatically — there is no separate mode to switch on. Open the graph settings (the wrench icon) to confirm the angle range runs from \(0\) to \(2\pi\), so the whole curve, including any inner loop, is drawn. Graph Example 1's limaçon and confirm by eye that the small inner loop appears between the two angles you calculated. Then graph r^2 = 4cos(2theta) for Example 4 — Desmos will plot both branches of the figure-eight and simply leave the undefined directions blank, exactly as the domain restriction predicted.

▶  Try it yourself: Classify \(r=1+4\sin\theta\), and state whether it has an inner loop.
\(a=1\), \(b=4\), so \(\frac{a}{b}=\frac14<1\): a limaçon with an inner loop.
▶  Try it yourself: For \(r^2=16\cos2\theta\), find \(a\) and state one value of \(\theta\) where \(r=0\).
\(a^2=16\), so \(a=4\). \(r=0\) when \(\cos2\theta=0\), e.g. \(2\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\Rightarrow\theta=\frac{\pi}{4}\) (also \(\theta=\frac{3\pi}{4}\), and their reflections).
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Classifying a limaçon by comparing \(a\) and \(b\) as raw numbers instead of forming the ratio \(\frac{a}{b}\). \(r=10+9\cos\theta\) has a large gap between \(a=10\) and \(b=9\), which looks dramatic — but \(\frac{a}{b}=\frac{10}{9}\approx1.11\) is barely above \(1\), so the curve is only slightly dimpled, nearly a cardioid. Compare that to \(r=2+1.9\cos\theta\): the two constants are close together, yet \(\frac{a}{b}\approx1.05\) is also barely above \(1\) — nearly the identical shape. Shape depends on the ratio, never on the size of the gap between \(a\) and \(b\).
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
In 1694, the Swiss mathematician Jacob Bernoulli — the same Bernoulli who would later ask for a logarithmic spiral engraved on his tombstone with the motto “Eadem mutata resurgo” (“Though changed, I rise again the same”) — described the figure-eight curve in the journal Acta Eruditorum and gave it the name it still carries. He reached for a Latin word usually used for a hanging ribbon or knot, \(\textit{lemniscus}\), because the curve's two crossed loops reminded him of exactly that shape. One mathematician, two curves born from the same appetite for a shape that returns to itself.

Conclusion

One family of equations, one ratio to test, and four genuinely different snails fall out of it — loop, cardioid, dimple, or smooth oval, each one a fact about \(\frac{a}{b}\) rather than a fact you have to memorize shape by shape. The lemniscate adds something new to this unit's gallery: a curve with gaps built into its own domain, present in some directions and simply absent in others. Tomorrow the gallery grows once more, into curves with even more delicate structure — roses whose petal count you will learn to predict before you ever plot a single point.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does the ratio \(\frac{a}{b}\), rather than the individual sizes of \(a\) and \(b\), determine whether a limaçon has a loop, a dimple, or neither?

2. Why does a limaçon draw an inner loop precisely when \(r\) is able to become negative, and what does a negative \(r\)-value actually do to a plotted point?

3. Why is a lemniscate undefined for some values of \(\theta\) rather than merely small or hard to see there?

4. In what sense are the four limaçon shapes and the lemniscate's two lobes all examples of the same underlying wonder — a single simple equation producing a strikingly varied family of curves?
