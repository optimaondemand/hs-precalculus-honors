Eliminating the Parameter; Parametric Graphs with Technology
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to eliminate the parameter from a pair of parametric equations to find an equivalent rectangular equation.

  • I will be able to identify a domain restriction that a parametrization imposes and that the bare rectangular equation would otherwise miss, and confirm it with a point check.

  • I will be able to use a graphing calculator's parametric mode to explore a cycloid and a Lissajous-style curve and describe each one's shape qualitatively.

📚 VOCABULARY

Eliminating the parameter: solving one parametric equation for \(t\) and substituting the result into the other, to produce a single equation relating \(x\) and \(y\) directly.

Domain restriction: a limitation on which \(x\)- or \(y\)-values actually occur, imposed by the parametrization itself, that the eliminated rectangular equation does not automatically carry.

Cycloid: the curve traced by a fixed point on the rim of a circle as the circle rolls without slipping along a straight line.

Lissajous curve: a curve traced by a point whose horizontal and vertical motions are each sinusoidal but run at different frequencies, producing a family of looping, woven shapes.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

A parametric equation tells a richer story than a rectangular one — but this entire course, before this week, has only ever asked you to work with the plainer story: one equation, \(x\) and \(y\) related directly, no clock running underneath. Today you learn to translate backward, from the richer story to the plainer one, and — just as carefully — to notice exactly what that translation quietly loses along the way.

Eliminating the Parameter, Without Losing the Restriction

To eliminate the parameter from \(x=f(t)\), \(y=g(t)\): solve one equation for \(t\), then substitute that expression into the other. The resulting equation in \(x\) and \(y\) alone describes the same set of points — provided you carry forward any restriction the original parametrization placed on \(x\) or \(y\). A square root, an even power, or a restricted range for \(t\) can each mean the parametrized curve is only part of what the bare rectangular equation would otherwise describe. Skipping that check does not produce a wrong equation — it produces a right equation describing the wrong (too large) set of points. Always confirm the result with an actual point from the original parametrization.

Example 1

Eliminate the parameter from \(x=t+2\), \(y=3t-1\), with \(t\) any real number.

Solution:

Solve the first equation for \(t\): \(t=x-2\). Substitute into the second:

\(y=3(x-2)-1=3x-7\)

Answer: \(y=3x-7\), with no restriction, since \(t\) ranges over all real numbers and so, therefore, do both \(x\) and \(y\). A full line, exactly as Example 1 of yesterday's lesson already suggested by its table of values.

Example 2

Eliminate the parameter from \(x=\sqrt{t}\), \(y=2t+1\), with \(t\ge0\), and state any domain restriction.

Solution:

Solve the first equation for \(t\): since \(x=\sqrt t\), squaring gives \(t=x^2\). But notice: \(\sqrt t\) is never negative, so \(x\ge0\) always, no matter what \(t\) is. Substitute \(t=x^2\) into the second equation:

\(y=2x^2+1, \qquad x\ge0\)

Check with a point: at \(t=4\), the parametric equations give \(x=\sqrt4=2\), \(y=2(4)+1=9\); the rectangular equation gives \(y=2(2)^2+1=9\). ✓

Answer: \(y=2x^2+1\) for \(x\ge0\) only — the right half of the parabola. Without the restriction, \(y=2x^2+1\) alone would also include a point like \((-2,9)\), but no value of \(t\ge0\) ever produces \(x=-2\), since a square root cannot be negative. The restriction is not optional bookkeeping; it is the difference between describing the actual curve and describing a larger curve the parametrization never draws.

Example 3

Eliminate the parameter from \(x=\cos\theta\), \(y=\sin\theta\), \(\theta\in[0,2\pi]\).

Solution:

Square both equations and add, using the Pythagorean identity:

\(x^2+y^2=\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1\)

Answer: \(x^2+y^2=1\), the full unit circle, with no restriction — every point on the circle is reached by some \(\theta\) in \([0,2\pi]\). Notice what was lost in the elimination, though: the rectangular equation no longer says which direction the circle was traced, or where the motion started. That information — orientation — lives only in the parametric form, never in the eliminated one.

Example 4

Eliminate the parameter from \(x=\cos\theta\), \(y=\sin\theta\), but now restricted to \(\theta\in[0,\pi]\) only.

Solution:

The algebra is identical to Example 3: \(x^2+y^2=1\). But for \(\theta\in[0,\pi]\), sine is never negative, so \(y=\sin\theta\ge0\) throughout.

Answer: \(x^2+y^2=1\) with \(y\ge0\) — the upper semicircle only. Check a point: at \(\theta=\frac{3\pi}{2}\) (outside this restricted domain), the full circle would include \((0,-1)\), but no \(\theta\) in \([0,\pi]\) produces that point, confirming the lower half is genuinely excluded.

Key Fact: Two Curves Technology Reveals Best

Cycloid: \(x=a(t-\sin t)\), \(y=a(1-\cos t)\) — the path traced by a single point on the rim of a circle of radius \(a\) as the circle rolls along a straight line. Every arch reaches a maximum height of \(2a\) and returns to the line, then repeats.

Lissajous curve: \(x=\sin(at+\delta)\), \(y=\sin(bt)\) — two sinusoidal motions at different frequencies \(a\) and \(b\), combined at right angles. When \(\frac{a}{b}\) is a ratio of whole numbers, the curve closes into a repeating woven pattern; change the ratio and the whole family of shapes changes with it.

Example 5

Without eliminating the parameter algebraically, describe qualitatively what the cycloid \(x=t-\sin t\), \(y=1-\cos t\) looks like for \(t\in[0,4\pi]\).

Solution:

Here \(a=1\). At \(t=0\): \((0,0)\). At \(t=\pi\): \(x=\pi-\sin\pi=\pi\), \(y=1-\cos\pi=2\) — the top of the first arch. At \(t=2\pi\): \(x=2\pi\), \(y=0\) — back down to the line. The pattern then repeats for \(t\in[2\pi,4\pi]\).

Answer: Two identical arches, each rising from the \(x\)-axis to a height of \(2\) and back down, side by side — exactly the path a reflector on a rolling bicycle wheel traces as the wheel completes two full turns.

💻 On Your Calculator: Exploring Cycloids and Lissajous Curves

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), type (t - sin(t), 1 - cos(t)). Accept the \(t\)-slider Desmos offers, then set its range to \(0\) to \(4\pi\) to see two full arches, matching Example 5. Press play and watch the traced point move exactly the way a valve stem on a rolling bicycle wheel would. Then, on a new line, type (sin(3t), sin(2t)) with \(t\) ranging over \(0\) to \(2\pi\), and observe the woven, ribbon-like Lissajous curve that results. Change the \(3\) to a \(4\) or a \(5\) and watch the pattern reorganize itself completely — a direct, visual way to explore how the frequency ratio controls the shape.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Eliminating the parameter correctly, then forgetting to check whether a square root, an even power, or a restricted \(t\)-range silently narrowed the domain or range. Example 2's algebra is fully correct without the restriction — \(y=2x^2+1\) is exactly the right shape of curve — but without \(x\ge0\) stated explicitly, the equation describes twice as much curve as the original parametrization ever draws. Always test at least one point from the original parametric equations against the final rectangular form, exactly as Examples 2 and 4 did, before calling the elimination finished.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
In 1673, the Dutch physicist Christiaan Huygens discovered that a pendulum swinging along a cycloidal path is tautochronous: it takes exactly the same time to complete a swing no matter how wide the swing is, a property no ordinary circular-arc pendulum has. Huygens built cycloidal-cheek pendulum clocks to try to exploit it. Then, in 1696, Johann Bernoulli publicly challenged Europe's mathematicians to find the curve of fastest descent between two points under gravity — the brachistochrone problem. Newton, Leibniz, l'Hôpital, and Johann's own brother Jacob Bernoulli each found the answer independently: the fastest path is not a straight line at all, but again a cycloid. The same humble curve traced by a point on a rolling wheel turned out to govern both perfectly equal time and least possible time — a coincidence too strange for the Bernoulli brothers to let go of, and part of why this unit's Bernoulli connection runs as deep as it does.

Conclusion

Eliminating the parameter closes the loop this week opened: a moving point's story, translated back into the single equation this course has used since Unit 1 — with one honest caveat, that a restriction hiding inside the original parametrization must travel with it, or the translation quietly says more than the truth. The cycloid and the Lissajous curve are exactly the kind of shape a parametrization was built to reach that a rectangular equation, on its own, would never have thought to write down. Unit 8 closes here with three languages now in hand for describing a curve — rectangular, polar, and parametric — each one true, and each one showing you something the other two do not.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why can eliminating the parameter produce a technically correct equation that still describes the wrong set of points?

2. What kind of information does a parametric curve carry that its eliminated rectangular equation cannot?

3. Why is a point check — substituting an actual value of \(t\) — the most reliable way to confirm a domain restriction was carried forward correctly?

4. What does it mean that the very same cycloid answers two different questions — equal time for every swing, and fastest possible descent — and why might that feel like more than a coincidence?
