Rose Curves and the Polar Garden
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to predict the number of petals a rose curve \(r=a\cos(n\theta)\) or \(r=a\sin(n\theta)\) will draw, directly from whether \(n\) is odd or even.

  • I will be able to graph a rose curve by hand from a short table of values and confirm the petal count and orientation with a graphing calculator.

  • I will be able to arrange and label the five special polar-curve families — circles, cardioids, limaçons, lemniscates, and roses — by the signature of their equations.

📚 VOCABULARY

Rose curve (rhodonea curve): a polar curve of the form \(r=a\cos(n\theta)\) or \(r=a\sin(n\theta)\), whose graph consists of a fixed number of symmetric, petal-shaped loops radiating from the pole. From the Greek/Latin rhodon, “rose.”

Petal: one complete loop of a rose curve, traced as \(r\) rises from \(0\) to a maximum and back to \(0\) over one interval of \(\theta\).

Petal count: the total number of distinct petals a rose curve draws over one full description of the curve — \(n\) petals when \(n\) is odd, \(2n\) petals when \(n\) is even.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Every curve this unit has drawn so far has been a single closed shape — one circle, one cardioid, one looping snail, one figure-eight. Today's family refuses that restraint entirely. Change nothing about the equation but a single whole number, and the curve blooms into three petals, or four, or eight, arranged with a symmetry so exact it looks designed rather than computed. In the 1720s, the Italian mathematician and Camaldolese monk Guido Grandi studied exactly this family, corresponded about it with Gottfried Leibniz, and gave it the name it still carries: rhodonea, curves shaped like roses. Few equations in this course reward wonder as directly as this one — a single integer, and an entire garden opens.

Predicting the Petal Count

For \(r=a\cos(n\theta)\) or \(r=a\sin(n\theta)\), with \(n\) a positive integer:

\(n\) odd \(\Rightarrow\) \(n\) petals   —   \(n\) even \(\Rightarrow\) \(2n\) petals

The parity matters because of what happens when \(r\) goes negative. A rose curve completes one full petal every time its argument \(n\theta\) advances by \(\pi\) (from one zero of \(r\) to the next). Sweep \(\theta\) through a full turn, \(0\) to \(2\pi\), and \(n\theta\) advances through \(2n\pi\) — enough room, in principle, for \(2n\) petals. When \(n\) is even, each of those \(2n\) petals lands in its own distinct direction, and all \(2n\) survive as separate loops. When \(n\) is odd, the stretches where \(r\) is negative reflect back through the pole onto directions a petal has already visited — so exactly half of the \(2n\) potential petals retrace petals already drawn, leaving only \(n\) genuinely distinct ones.

Example 1

Predict, then verify by table, the petal count of \(r=\cos(3\theta)\).

Solution:

Predict: \(n=3\) is odd, so the rule predicts \(3\) petals. Verify with a table over one petal-forming interval:

\(\theta=0:\ r=\cos0=1 \qquad \theta=\tfrac{\pi}{6}:\ r=\cos\tfrac{\pi}{2}=0 \qquad \theta=\tfrac{\pi}{3}:\ r=\cos\pi=-1 \qquad \theta=\tfrac{\pi}{2}:\ r=\cos\tfrac{3\pi}{2}=0\)

The first petal is traced as \(\theta\) runs from \(0\) to \(\frac{\pi}{6}\) (where \(r\) falls from \(1\) to \(0\)). From \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{6}\) to \(\frac{\pi}{2}\), \(r\) is negative; a negative \(r\) at angle \(\theta\) plots at the same point as a positive \(r\) at angle \(\theta+\pi\) — and checking those reflected points shows they fall exactly on top of the third petal, not a new one.

Answer: \(3\) petals, confirming the odd-\(n\) rule. As \(\theta\) continues from \(\frac{\pi}{2}\) to \(2\pi\), every subsequent negative-\(r\) stretch likewise retraces a petal already drawn rather than opening a new one.

Example 2

Predict, then verify by table, the petal count of \(r=\cos(4\theta)\).

Solution:

Predict: \(n=4\) is even, so the rule predicts \(2n=8\) petals. Verify with a table:

\(\theta=0:\ r=\cos0=1 \qquad \theta=\tfrac{\pi}{8}:\ r=\cos\tfrac{\pi}{2}=0 \qquad \theta=\tfrac{\pi}{4}:\ r=\cos\pi=-1 \qquad \theta=\tfrac{3\pi}{8}:\ r=\cos\tfrac{3\pi}{2}=0\)

The first petal runs from \(\theta=0\) to \(\frac{\pi}{8}\), pointing along the polar axis. From \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{8}\) to \(\frac{3\pi}{8}\), \(r\) is negative, and this time the reflected points (adding \(\pi\) to the angle) land in a direction none of the positive-\(r\) petals have reached yet — a genuinely new petal.

Answer: \(8\) petals, confirming the even-\(n\) rule. Unlike the odd case, every one of the \(2n=8\) potential petal-forming intervals in \(0\le\theta<2\pi\) opens a direction no earlier interval had already visited.

Example 3

Without building a table, state the petal count of \(r=5\sin(6\theta)\) and of \(r=5\sin(7\theta)\).

Solution:

For \(r=5\sin(6\theta)\): \(n=6\) is even, so \(2n=12\) petals. For \(r=5\sin(7\theta)\): \(n=7\) is odd, so \(n=7\) petals.

Answer: \(12\) petals and \(7\) petals, respectively. Notice the amplitude \(a=5\) never entered the petal count at all — \(a\) controls only how long each petal is, exactly the same job it does in every other polar and rectangular family this course has studied.

Example 4

Compare \(r=\cos(2\theta)\) and \(r=\sin(2\theta)\). How are their graphs related?

Solution:

Both have \(n=2\) (even), so both draw \(4\) petals. \(r=\cos(2\theta)\) reaches its first maximum, \(r=1\), at \(\theta=0\), so one petal points directly along the polar axis. \(r=\sin(2\theta)\) reaches its first maximum at \(2\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\), i.e. \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{4}\), so its petals point along the diagonals instead.

Answer: Both are four-petaled roses of the identical size and shape; \(r=\sin(2\theta)\) is simply \(r=\cos(2\theta)\) rotated \(45^\circ\) — the same relationship you already saw between cosine- and sine-based lemniscates yesterday.

🥽 Your VR Session: The Polar Garden 30 min

What you're doing. Lesson 7-2's Wave Machine let a rotating arm trace a sine wave onto a wall graph in real time. This Session extends that idea into the polar plane: five garden beds, each holding a mechanism that sweeps a radius \(r\) through the angle \(\theta\) and traces the resulting curve in space as it turns. Before each rose-curve trace runs, you predict its petal count on a whiteboard; then you run it and check your prediction against what actually appears. Once the roses are traced, you arrange all five special families — circles, cardioids, limaçons, lemniscates, and roses — into one labeled garden.

What you'll need. Your VR headset (preferred) or the ENGAGE desktop app; read this lesson's Exploring the Math section first, so the petal-count rule already means something concrete before you test it.

How to join.

  • Session type: solo, persistent — your labeled garden and whiteboard remain if you return.
  • Session ID: posted beside this lesson in the Canvas module.
  • Location: The Polar Garden.
  • On arrival: you spawn on a central path with a whiteboard at your left holding today's presets and first instruction, and five garden beds ahead of you, each already labeled with a blank nameplate and holding a sweeping-radius mechanism, currently at rest.

What to do, in order.

  1. At the rose-curve bed, read the whiteboard's overview, then write your first prediction: how many petals will \(r=\cos(3\theta)\) draw as \(\theta\) sweeps one full turn?
  2. Run the n = 3 preset. Watch the mechanism sweep and trace the curve in the air, then sticky-note the actual petal count next to your prediction — confirmed, or corrected.
  3. Predict again: how many petals will \(r=\cos(4\theta)\) draw?
  4. Run the n = 4 preset and sticky-note the outcome next to this second prediction.
  5. Walk to the four remaining garden beds — circles, cardioids, limaçons, and lemniscates — where each mechanism has already traced its curve. At each bed, place the correct nameplate IFX naming the family and write its defining equation form on the attached sticky note, arranging the complete polar garden.
  6. Return to the rose bed and run one full, undisturbed trace of \(r=\cos(3\theta)\). Start a spatial recording and narrate, in thirty to sixty seconds, why \(n=3\) produced only \(3\) petals while \(n=4\) produced \(8\) — then stop the recording.
  7. Take a snapshot of the whiteboard showing both predictions beside their actual outcomes, with the five labeled garden beds visible in the background.

What to submit. Your whiteboard predictions-vs-outcomes snapshot, included as a page in your Assignment 8-6 PDF, with the shareable link to your narrated spatial recording pasted on that same page. About 30 minutes in-headset.

💻 On Your Calculator: Watching Petals Appear

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), type r = cos(n theta). Desmos will offer to build a slider for \(n\) — accept it, then restrict the slider to whole-number steps in its settings. Drag \(n\) from \(1\) up through \(8\) one step at a time, counting petals aloud as you go, and confirm the pattern holds: every odd \(n\) matches its own value, every even \(n\) doubles it. Then switch the equation to r = sin(n theta) and drag \(n\) again to confirm the petal count never changes — only the petals' orientation rotates.

▶  Try it yourself: How many petals does \(r=2\sin(5\theta)\) draw?
\(n=5\) is odd, so \(5\) petals. The amplitude \(2\) sets each petal's length, not its count.
▶  Try it yourself: How many petals does \(r=4\cos(8\theta)\) draw, and how is that different from what \(n=4\) alone would give?
\(n=8\) is even, so \(2n=16\) petals — twice as many as the \(8\) petals \(n=4\) alone produces, since doubling an even \(n\) doubles its (already doubled) petal count.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Assuming every rose has \(2n\) petals, doubling the odd cases along with the even ones. \(r=\cos(3\theta)\) does not have \(6\) petals — it has \(3\), because half of the \(6\) potential petal-forming stretches retrace petals the curve has already drawn. The doubling rule is a fact about even \(n\) specifically, not a universal rule for every rose curve.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Guido Grandi was not only a mathematician but a Camaldolese monk, and he took evident delight in finding devotional meaning in his curves — he once described a related family of loops as symbolizing the mystery of the Trinity, three petals from one equation. Whatever one makes of that reading, it captures something real about this lesson's virtue: a rose curve is wonder in equation form, a fixed rule producing an endlessly varied, endlessly symmetric bloom.

Conclusion

One integer, tested for parity, tells you exactly how many petals a rose will bloom before you plot a single point — the same kind of clean, predictive power this unit has found again and again in the ratio \(\frac{a}{b}\) and in the domain of a lemniscate. With circles, cardioids, limaçons, lemniscates, and roses now arranged in one garden, this unit's polar equations have given up the whole of their shape-language. What they have not yet given up is motion: every curve so far has been a static picture, a single sweep of \(\theta\) frozen on the page. Tomorrow, a second variable — time itself — enters the picture, and a curve becomes something a point actually travels along.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does a rose curve's petal count depend on whether \(n\) is odd or even, rather than on \(n\) alone?

2. What is happening geometrically when \(r\) becomes negative during a rose curve's sweep, and why does that explain why odd-\(n\) roses have fewer distinct petals than the \(2n\) intervals would suggest?

3. How are \(r=\cos(n\theta)\) and \(r=\sin(n\theta)\) related, and what does that relationship tell you about what \(a\), \(n\), and the choice of sine versus cosine each control?

4. In the Polar Garden, what did seeing five different curve families traced by the very same kind of sweeping mechanism reveal about what all of polar graphing actually is?
