Parametric Equations and Direction of Motion
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to sketch a parametric curve from a table of values for \(x(t)\) and \(y(t)\), placing arrows on the graph to show the direction of motion as \(t\) increases.

  • I will be able to interpret the parameter \(t\) as time in an applied setting, stating exactly where a moving point is located at a given instant.

  • I will be able to use a graphing calculator's parametric mode to trace a curve and confirm, by watching the trace itself, which direction it actually travels.

📚 VOCABULARY

Parameter: a third variable — here called \(t\) — that both \(x\) and \(y\) are written in terms of, rather than being written in terms of each other directly.

Parametric equations: a pair of equations \(x=f(t)\) and \(y=g(t)\) that together describe a curve, with \(t\) ranging over some domain.

Orientation: the direction a parametric curve is traced as \(t\) increases, typically marked on a graph with small arrows along the path.

Parametrize: to describe a curve or motion using parametric equations, in terms of a parameter such as time.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Every equation this unit has drawn so far — polar or rectangular — has described a curve as a fixed set of points, a picture with no sense of when any particular point was visited. A popular story about René Descartes holds that he arrived at the idea of coordinate geometry while lying ill in bed, watching a fly crawl across his ceiling, and realizing he could pin down the insect's changing position with two numbers measured from two fixed edges of the room. Whatever the truth of that particular story, it captures something today's lesson makes precise: a moving point's position is a pair of numbers that changes, and describing that change — not just the shape it eventually traces — is exactly what a parametric equation is built to do. The idea is in fact far older than Descartes. The Greek sophist Hippias of Elis, in the fifth century BC, defined a curve called the quadratrix as the path traced by the intersection of two things moving at once: a radius sweeping uniformly around a circle, and a line segment descending uniformly across it. A curve drawn by motion, coordinated in time — that is the oldest form of the very idea you are about to formalize.

What a Parametric Equation Actually Says

A pair of parametric equations

\(x=f(t) \qquad y=g(t)\)

describes a moving point whose horizontal and vertical positions are each separately controlled by the same third variable \(t\), often standing for time. At any particular value of \(t\), the point is at the single location \(\big(f(t),g(t)\big)\) — not somewhere on the curve in general, but at one specific spot, at one specific moment. As \(t\) increases steadily through its domain, the point traces a definite path in a definite direction, called its orientation. Two different pairs of parametric equations can trace the exact same set of points and still describe two entirely different motions, if they travel that path in opposite directions or at different speeds.

Example 1

Sketch the curve given by \(x=t-1\), \(y=2t+3\) for \(t\in[-2,3]\), and mark its orientation.

Solution:

\(t=-2:\ (-3,-1) \quad t=-1:\ (-2,1) \quad t=0:\ (-1,3) \quad t=1:\ (0,5) \quad t=2:\ (1,7) \quad t=3:\ (2,9)\)

Plotting these six points in the order \(t\) produced them, each successive point sits up and to the right of the one before it.

Answer: A line segment from \((-3,-1)\) to \((2,9)\), oriented up and to the right as \(t\) increases — the arrows on the graph point from the lower-left endpoint toward the upper-right one, never the reverse.

Example 2

Sketch the curve given by \(x=\cos t\), \(y=\sin t\) for \(t\in[0,2\pi]\), and mark its orientation.

Solution:

\(t=0:\ (1,0) \quad t=\tfrac{\pi}{2}:\ (0,1) \quad t=\pi:\ (-1,0) \quad t=\tfrac{3\pi}{2}:\ (0,-1) \quad t=2\pi:\ (1,0)\)

The point starts on the polar axis at \((1,0)\), then moves to the top of the circle at \((0,1)\), then to the left at \((-1,0)\), then to the bottom at \((0,-1)\), and back to where it started.

Answer: The unit circle, traced counterclockwise — moving from \((1,0)\) up into the first quadrant first, rather than down into the fourth, is exactly what counterclockwise motion means. This is the same unit-circle definition of sine and cosine from Unit 6, now watched as motion instead of read as a static coordinate.

Example 3

Sketch the curve given by \(x=t^2\), \(y=t\) for \(t\in[-2,2]\), and mark its orientation.

Solution:

\(t=-2:\ (4,-2) \quad t=-1:\ (1,-1) \quad t=0:\ (0,0) \quad t=1:\ (1,1) \quad t=2:\ (4,2)\)

From \(t=-2\) to \(t=0\), \(x\) decreases from \(4\) to \(0\) while \(y\) increases from \(-2\) to \(0\): the point moves up and to the left, down toward the vertex. From \(t=0\) to \(t=2\), \(x\) increases back to \(4\) while \(y\) continues increasing to \(2\): the point now moves up and to the right.

Answer: A sideways parabola opening to the right, traced starting from the point \((4,-2)\), sweeping down and to the left into the vertex at \((0,0)\), then sweeping back up and to the right out to \((4,2)\). The curve itself bends, but the point's motion along it is a single, continuous sweep — the direction changes from “up-left” to “up-right,” but \(t\) never once decreases or doubles back.

Example 4

A small object's position \(t\) seconds after launch is given by \(x(t)=20t\) and \(y(t)=-16t^2+40t\), with \(x\) and \(y\) in feet, for \(t\in[0,2.5]\). Find the object's position at \(t=1\) second, and describe what \(x(t)\) and \(y(t)\) each represent physically.

Solution:

\(x(1)=20(1)=20 \qquad y(1)=-16(1)^2+40(1)=-16+40=24\)

Answer: At \(t=1\) second, the object is at the point \((20,24)\): \(20\) feet downrange and \(24\) feet above the ground. Here \(x(t)\) tracks horizontal distance traveled and \(y(t)\) tracks height above the ground — two separate physical quantities, each governed by its own equation, but linked by sharing the same clock \(t\). This is exactly the situation next week's lesson on projectile motion will build on directly.

💻 On Your Calculator: Parametric Mode

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), type an ordered pair using \(t\), such as (t - 1, 2t + 3) for Example 1. Desmos recognizes this as a parametric curve and offers to add a slider for \(t\) automatically — accept it, then click the slider's bounds to set the domain to match the problem (here, \(t\) from \(-2\) to \(3\)). Press play on the slider and watch the point actually travel along the graph in real time; the direction it moves is the orientation. Do the same with (cos(t), sin(t)), setting \(t\) from \(0\) to \(2\pi\), and confirm by eye that the traced point moves counterclockwise, exactly as Example 2 predicted.

▶  Try it yourself: Build a table for \(x=3t\), \(y=t^2-1\) at \(t=-2,-1,0,1,2\), and state the orientation.
\(t=-2:(-6,3)\), \(t=-1:(-3,0)\), \(t=0:(0,-1)\), \(t=1:(3,0)\), \(t=2:(6,3)\). The point moves down-and-right into the vertex at \((0,-1)\), then up-and-right away from it — a left-opening-to-right parabola traced from left to right overall, dipping to its lowest point at \(t=0\).
▶  Try it yourself: Does \(x=\sin t\), \(y=\cos t\) for \(t\in[0,2\pi]\) trace the unit circle in the same direction as Example 2? Check with a table at \(t=0\) and \(t=\frac{\pi}{2}\).
At \(t=0\): \((0,1)\). At \(t=\frac{\pi}{2}\): \((1,0)\). The point moves from the top of the circle toward the right side — that is clockwise, the opposite orientation from Example 2, even though both parametrizations trace the identical circle.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Reading a table of \((x,y)\) points left to right by their \(x\)-coordinate, the way you would read a rectangular function's graph, instead of in the order \(t\) actually produced them. Example 3's parabola has \(x\)-values that decrease and then increase — sorting the table by \(x\) instead of by \(t\) would scramble the true order of the motion and could easily produce exactly the wrong arrows. Always trace a parametric curve in the order \(t\) increases, never in the order the coordinates happen to sort.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Hippias's quadratrix was built for a purpose far removed from graphing a moving point: he and later Greek geometers used it to attempt two of antiquity's most famous unsolved problems, trisecting an arbitrary angle and squaring the circle, using a curve that ordinary compass-and-straightedge methods could never draw. A curve defined by motion, in other words, could reach constructions a static curve could not — a very early hint of the power that describing a path as it is traveled, rather than only as it ends up looking, would eventually unlock.

Conclusion

A parametric curve is not merely a shape; it is a shape with a clock built into it, and reading that clock correctly — in the order \(t\) actually runs, not the order the coordinates happen to fall on the page — is the one habit today's lesson asked you to build. The unit circle traced one direction by \((\cos t,\sin t)\) and the opposite direction by \((\sin t,\cos t)\) is proof that the same picture can hide two different stories about how it was drawn. Tomorrow you learn to translate a parametric story back into the single rectangular equation this course has used all year — and to notice, carefully, when that translation quietly leaves part of the original story behind.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does a parametric curve carry a direction of motion, when a rectangular equation like \(y=x^2\) does not?

2. Why must a table of parametric values always be read in the order \(t\) increases, rather than in the order the \(x\)- or \(y\)-values happen to sort?

3. How can two different pairs of parametric equations trace the exact same set of points yet describe two genuinely different motions?

4. In what sense does interpreting \(t\) as time turn a parametric equation into a story about where something is at a given moment, rather than just a picture of where it has been?
