🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to convert a rectangular equation into polar form using \(x=r\cos\theta\) and \(y=r\sin\theta\).

  • I will be able to convert a polar equation into rectangular form using \(r^2=x^2+y^2\), \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\), and \(\tan\theta=\frac{y}{x}\).

  • I will be able to recognize when multiplying both sides of a polar equation by \(r\) is a legitimate step that reveals a hidden rectangular form.

📚 VOCABULARY

Rectangular equation: an equation relating the variables \(x\) and \(y\).

Polar equation: an equation relating the variables \(r\) and \(\theta\).

Equivalent equations: two equations, one written in each system, that describe the exact same set of points — the exact same curve, in two different vocabularies.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Yesterday you learned to translate a single point between the rectangular and polar languages. A single point, however, is a small thing to translate. The real payoff of having two languages appears when you translate an entire equation — and discover that the very same curve can look almost embarrassingly simple in one language and unreasonably complicated in the other. A circle centered at the origin, for instance, is \(x^2+y^2=25\) in rectangular form: two variables, a sum of squares, one constant. In polar form, that same circle is simply \(r=5\) — one variable, no arithmetic at all, because a circle centered at the pole is nothing more than “every point exactly \(5\) units out, in every direction.” The circle did not change. Only the sentence describing it changed, and one sentence happens to be shorter.

The dictionary you already built — the four relationships connecting \((x,y)\) to \((r,\theta)\) — is every tool this translation requires. Today the task is simply to apply that dictionary to a whole equation, one substitution at a time, and to develop an instinct for which direction of substitution actually simplifies the sentence you started with.

The Conversion Dictionary, Applied to a Whole Equation

To convert a rectangular equation to polar form, substitute \(x=r\cos\theta\) and \(y=r\sin\theta\) everywhere \(x\) and \(y\) appear, then simplify. To convert a polar equation to rectangular form, look for the pattern that matches one of \(r^2=x^2+y^2\), \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\), or \(\tan\theta=\frac{y}{x}\) — and if the equation is not already in one of those patterns, the single most useful move is often to create the pattern by multiplying both sides of the equation by \(r\).

Example 1

Convert \(x^2+y^2=49\) to polar form.

Solution:

The left side is exactly \(r^2\).

Answer: \(r^2=49\), or simply \(r=7\).

Example 2

Convert \(y=3\) to polar form.

Solution:

Substitute \(y=r\sin\theta\):

\(r\sin\theta = 3\)

Answer: \(r=\dfrac{3}{\sin\theta}=3\csc\theta\). A perfectly ordinary horizontal line becomes, in polar form, an equation that depends on \(\theta\) — a case where rectangular form was the simpler sentence.

Example 3

Convert \(y=-x\) to polar form.

Solution:

\(r\sin\theta = -r\cos\theta \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}=-1 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \tan\theta=-1\)

Answer: \(\theta=\dfrac{3\pi}{4}\) (equivalently \(-\frac{\pi}{4}\)). Notice that \(r\) disappeared from the equation entirely — because every point on this line, at every possible distance from the pole, shares the identical angle. A line through the pole is, in polar form, simply a statement about direction.

Example 4

Convert \(r=4\) to rectangular form.

Solution:

Square both sides: \(r^2=16\), and \(r^2=x^2+y^2\).

Answer: \(x^2+y^2=16\).

Example 5

Convert \(r=6\sin\theta\) to rectangular form.

Solution:

The equation is not yet in a recognizable pattern — multiply both sides by \(r\) to create one:

\(r\cdot r = 6r\sin\theta \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; r^2 = 6r\sin\theta \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x^2+y^2=6y\)

Complete the square in \(y\) to see the shape hiding inside this equation:

\(x^2+y^2-6y=0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x^2+(y^2-6y+9)=9 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x^2+(y-3)^2=9\)

Answer: \(x^2+(y-3)^2=9\), a circle centered at \((0,3)\) with radius \(3\). Multiplying by \(r\) looks like it might invent new solutions out of nowhere, since \(r=0\) now satisfies the new equation for every \(\theta\) — but the pole was already on the original curve (at \(\theta=0\), \(r=6\sin0=0\)), so nothing new was added: the two equations describe exactly the same circle.

Example 6

Convert \((x-2)^2+y^2=4\) to polar form.

Solution:

Expand first:

\(x^2-4x+4+y^2=4 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x^2+y^2=4x \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; r^2=4r\cos\theta\)

Answer: \(r=4\cos\theta\). Compare this to Example 5’s circle: the same reasoning, run in the opposite direction, turns an off-center rectangular circle into a strikingly short polar sentence.

💻 On Your Calculator: Confirming an Equivalence

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), type Example 5’s original equation directly — r = 6sin(theta) — on one line, and its rectangular twin, x^2 + (y-3)^2 = 9, on the next. Desmos recognizes an expression written in \(r\) and \(\theta\) as polar automatically, and the two graphs should sit exactly on top of each other: one circle, drawn from two completely different sentences. Try the same check with Example 6.

▶  Try it yourself: Convert \(x=5\) to polar form.
\(r\cos\theta=5\), so \(r=\dfrac{5}{\cos\theta}=5\sec\theta\).
▶  Try it yourself: Convert \(r=-2\sin\theta\) to rectangular form, and complete the square to name its center and radius.
Multiply by \(r\): \(r^2=-2r\sin\theta \Rightarrow x^2+y^2=-2y \Rightarrow x^2+y^2+2y=0 \Rightarrow x^2+(y+1)^2=1\). Center \((0,-1)\), radius \(1\).
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Dividing an equation like \(r^2=6r\sin\theta\) by \(r\) as a reflex, arriving at \(r=6\sin\theta\), and stopping there when the goal was actually to eliminate \(r\) and \(\theta\) altogether. That division is not wrong — \(r=6\sin\theta\) is a perfectly good polar equation — but it undoes the very substitution you were trying to make. When your goal is a rectangular equation, replace \(r^2\) with \(x^2+y^2\) and \(r\sin\theta\) with \(y\) before touching a division sign, or the pole's variables never leave the page.

Conclusion

The same four relationships that translated a point yesterday just translated an entire equation today — in both directions, and with one extra trick, multiplying by \(r\), that turns an unrecognizable polar sentence into a rectangular one you already know how to read. Circles, in particular, have now shown their hand twice: off-center in rectangular form, remarkably plain in polar form. That is no accident. Tomorrow you stop converting equations one at a time and start graphing them directly in the polar plane — building a table, testing for symmetry, and discovering that some of the most strikingly beautiful curves in this unit have polar equations shorter than the sentence you just used to describe a plain circle.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why can the very same curve be a short sentence in one coordinate system and a longer one in the other, when the curve itself never changes?

2. Why is multiplying a polar equation by \(r\) a legitimate step here, rather than one that risks inventing points that were never on the original curve?

3. What tells you, just by looking at a rectangular equation, roughly how complicated its polar form is going to be?

4. How does graphing both forms of the same equation on one screen serve as an honest check that a conversion was actually done correctly?
