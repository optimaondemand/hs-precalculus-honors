Circles and Cardioids
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to identify a circle in polar form (\(r=a\), \(r=2a\cos\theta\), or \(r=2a\sin\theta\)) and state its center and radius without building a table.

  • I will be able to identify a cardioid in polar form (\(r=a\pm a\cos\theta\) or \(r=a\pm a\sin\theta\)) and state the direction of its cusp and the location of its farthest point.

  • I will be able to distinguish a circle’s polar signature from a cardioid’s at a glance, without plotting either one.

📚 VOCABULARY

Cardioid: a heart-shaped polar curve with a single sharp cusp at the pole; algebraically, \(r=a\pm a\cos\theta\) or \(r=a\pm a\sin\theta\).

Cusp: the sharp point of a cardioid, located exactly at the pole, where \(r=0\).

Equation signature: the distinguishing algebraic pattern of an equation that reveals its graph’s family instantly, without plotting.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Yesterday’s table-and-symmetry method is a promise every polar equation can trust: it will always, eventually, reveal the true shape of a curve, however unfamiliar. But a promise that always works is not the same as a promise that is always the fastest way to work — and two shapes turn up so often in this unit’s equations that building a fresh table for each one, every time, would be a kind of willful slowness. Today those two shapes — the circle and the cardioid — earn a shortcut: an equation signature you can read on sight, the way a practiced reader recognizes a word without sounding out each letter.

Key Fact: The Circle Signatures

\(r=a\)   —   \(r=2a\cos\theta\)   —   \(r=2a\sin\theta\)

\(r=a\) is a circle centered at the pole, radius \(|a|\) — every direction, same distance. \(r=2a\cos\theta\) is a circle of radius \(|a|\), centered at \((a,0)\), passing through the pole. \(r=2a\sin\theta\) is a circle of radius \(|a|\), centered at \((0,a)\), also passing through the pole. Notice what all three share: no constant term is added to the cosine or sine term. That absence is the signature’s whole secret.

The Cardioid Signature

\(r=a+a\cos\theta \qquad r=a-a\cos\theta \qquad r=a+a\sin\theta \qquad r=a-a\sin\theta\)

A cardioid’s signature is a constant term added to a cosine or sine term of the exact same size — “\(a\) plus or minus that same \(a\).” The cusp sits wherever \(r=0\); the farthest point from the pole, at distance \(2a\), sits at the opposite angle. For \(r=a+a\cos\theta\): \(r=0\) when \(\cos\theta=-1\) (\(\theta=\pi\)), so the cusp points left, and the curve reaches its farthest point, \(2a\), at \(\theta=0\) — the rounded bulge opens right, toward the polar axis.

Example 1

Identify and describe \(r=6\cos\theta\).

Solution:

No constant term — this is a circle. Here \(2a=6\), so \(a=3\). Verify by converting: multiply by \(r\), \(r^2=6r\cos\theta \Rightarrow x^2+y^2=6x \Rightarrow (x-3)^2+y^2=9\).

Answer: Circle, center \((3,0)\), radius \(3\), passing through the pole.

Example 2

Identify and describe \(r=-4\sin\theta\).

Solution:

No constant term — a circle. Here \(2a=-4\), so \(a=-2\); center \((0,a)=(0,-2)\), radius \(|a|=2\). Verify: \(r^2=-4r\sin\theta \Rightarrow x^2+y^2=-4y \Rightarrow x^2+(y+2)^2=4\).

Answer: Circle, center \((0,-2)\), radius \(2\), passing through the pole. A negative coefficient does not create a strange new shape; it only moves the circle's center to the opposite side of the pole.

Example 3

Identify and describe \(r=3+3\cos\theta\).

Solution:

Constant term equals the coefficient of cosine (\(3\) and \(3\)) — a cardioid. Cusp where \(r=0\): \(3+3\cos\theta=0 \Rightarrow \cos\theta=-1 \Rightarrow \theta=\pi\). Farthest point: \(r=6\) at \(\theta=0\).

Answer: Cardioid; cusp at the pole pointing toward \(\theta=\pi\) (left); farthest point \(6\) units out along the polar axis, symmetric about the polar axis (since cosine is even).

Example 4

Identify and describe \(r=5-5\sin\theta\).

Solution:

Constant equals the coefficient of sine — a cardioid. Cusp: \(5-5\sin\theta=0 \Rightarrow \sin\theta=1 \Rightarrow \theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\). Farthest point: at \(\theta=\frac{3\pi}{2}\), \(\sin\theta=-1\), so \(r=5-5(-1)=10\).

Answer: Cardioid; cusp points up (\(\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\)); farthest point \(10\) units straight down, at \(\theta=\frac{3\pi}{2}\). The cusp and the farthest point are always a half-turn (\(\pi\)) apart.

Example 5

Contrast \(r=4\cos\theta\) with \(r=4+4\cos\theta\): same coefficient, two entirely different curves. Find where each one crosses the polar axis in each direction.

Solution:

\(r=4\cos\theta\) (circle, no constant term): at \(\theta=0\), \(r=4\); at \(\theta=\pi\), \(r=4\cos\pi=-4\), which plots — by the negative-\(r\) rule — at distance \(4\) out along \(\theta=0\), the identical point. This circle touches the polar axis at exactly two points: the pole itself (\(\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\)) and \((4,0)\).

\(r=4+4\cos\theta\) (cardioid): at \(\theta=0\), \(r=8\); at \(\theta=\pi\), \(r=0\), the pole.

Answer: The circle spans the polar axis from the pole to \((4,0)\) — a diameter of \(4\). The cardioid spans the polar axis from the pole to \((8,0)\) — a diameter of \(8\), twice as far, even though both equations share the number \(4\). One added constant term is the entire difference between these two spans.

⚠️ AI CHECKPOINT: WHERE IS THE CUSP?

A student asked an AI to locate the cusp of \(r=4+4\sin\theta\). It answered:

“The cusp is at the top of the graph, at \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\), because that is where the sine term is largest.”

The AI confused two opposite features. A cusp occurs where \(r=0\), not where \(r\) is largest. Here, \(r=0\) when \(\sin\theta=-1\), at \(\theta=\frac{3\pi}{2}\) (pointing down) — and \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\), where the sine term truly is largest, is where \(r=8\), the curve’s farthest point from the pole, the rounded bulge, not the cusp.

Before trusting any claim about a cardioid’s cusp — yours or a machine’s — check which question you are actually answering: “where does \(r\) equal zero” (the cusp) is not the same question as “where is \(r\) largest” (the farthest point), and the two answers are always a half-turn apart.

💻 On Your Calculator: Reading a Signature Fast

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), type r = 4cos(theta) and, on the next line, r = 4 + 4cos(theta). Desmos recognizes both as polar automatically. Watching the two graphs side by side — one circle through the pole, one cardioid, from equations that differ by a single added term — is the fastest way to make Example 5’s contrast permanent. Then graph Example 4’s cardioid and confirm by tracing that \(r=0\) exactly at \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\), not at \(\theta=\frac{3\pi}{2}\) where the AI checkpoint above went wrong.

▶  Try it yourself: Identify \(r=10\sin\theta\), stating its center and radius.
No constant term — a circle. \(2a=10 \Rightarrow a=5\). Center \((0,5)\), radius \(5\), passing through the pole.
▶  Try it yourself: Identify \(r=2-2\cos\theta\), stating the cusp’s direction and the farthest point’s distance.
Constant equals the coefficient — a cardioid. Cusp: \(2-2\cos\theta=0 \Rightarrow \cos\theta=1 \Rightarrow \theta=0\) (cusp points right). Farthest point at \(\theta=\pi\): \(r=2-2(-1)=4\).
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The cardioid’s single-point cusp is more than a mathematical curiosity — a satellite dish’s microphone pickup pattern, and certain studio microphones used for exactly this reason, are built to have a cardioid-shaped sensitivity: strongly “heart-shaped” toward the front, and nearly silent from directly behind. The engineers who name that pattern “cardioid” are reaching for the same curve, and the same word, that you graphed today.

Conclusion

Two families, two signatures, and neither one required a single plotted point: a circle’s equation names a coefficient and nothing more; a cardioid’s equation names a constant that exactly matches that coefficient. The whole difference between an off-center circle and a heart-shaped curve with a single sharp point turns out to live in one added term — a small algebraic fact carrying a large geometric consequence. The cardioid you met today was a balanced special case, its two constants forced to match exactly. Tomorrow, that balance is deliberately broken — letting the two constants differ opens the cardioid into an entire family of related curves, some looping back on themselves in a shape too delightful to spoil before you have earned it honestly, table cell by table cell.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. What distinguishes a circle’s polar equation from a cardioid’s, given how similar the two forms look at first glance?

2. Why does the value of \(\theta\) where \(r=0\) tell you exactly where a cardioid’s cusp points, while the value where \(r\) is maximum tells you the opposite end of the curve?

3. How does converting \(r=2a\cos\theta\) back to rectangular form confirm, independently of the signature shortcut, that it truly is a circle passing through the pole?

4. Why is it faster — and just as reliable — to recognize an equation’s family from its signature than to build a full table of values for it, as you did yesterday?
