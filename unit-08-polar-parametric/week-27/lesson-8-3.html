Graphing in the Polar Plane
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to graph a polar equation by constructing a table of \((\theta,r)\) values and plotting the resulting points.

  • I will be able to apply the three symmetry tests — about the polar axis, about the line \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\), and about the pole — to predict features of a polar graph before plotting a single point.

  • I will be able to graph a polar equation using Desmos’s automatic polar recognition and verify the result against a hand-built table.

📚 VOCABULARY

Symmetry about the polar axis: a graph is symmetric about the polar axis if replacing \(\theta\) with \(-\theta\) leaves the equation unchanged — the graph is a mirror image across the horizontal axis.

Symmetry about the line \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\): a graph is symmetric about this line if replacing \(\theta\) with \(\pi-\theta\) leaves the equation unchanged — the graph is a mirror image across the vertical axis.

Symmetry about the pole: a graph is symmetric about the pole if replacing \(\theta\) with \(\theta+\pi\) (or \(r\) with \(-r\)) leaves the equation unchanged — the graph maps onto itself under a half-turn about the origin.

Archimedean spiral: a curve traced by a point moving away from the pole at a steady rate as \(\theta\) increases without bound; \(r=\theta\) is its simplest equation.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Long before anyone had a coordinate system at all, the Greeks were already fascinated by curves that a point traces out through motion — among them a curve called the quadratrix, generated by two things moving at once, a rotating ray and a sliding line, crossing paths to draw an entirely new shape neither motion could have drawn alone. That same appetite — a curve as the record of a motion, rather than a shape you simply recognize on sight — is exactly what today asks of you, three lessons before you meet motion-generated curves by name. Here, the motion is simpler: a point creeping outward from the pole while its angle sweeps steadily around it. The Greek mathematician Archimedes, in a treatise called On Spirals written in the third century BC, was the first to study this curve rigorously — a point moving away from a fixed pole at a constant speed while the ray to that point rotates at a constant angular speed. Its equation, in the language you learned this week, is almost absurdly short: \(r=\theta\).

What makes today’s method powerful is that it works for any polar equation, not just the friendly ones. You do not need to already know a curve is a spiral, a circle, or something stranger still, to graph it — a table of values and two or three symmetry checks will always get you there.

The Table Method: A Universal Starting Point

To graph any polar equation \(r=f(\theta)\): choose a spread of \(\theta\)-values (multiples of \(\frac{\pi}{6}\) or \(\frac{\pi}{4}\) across at least one full turn, \([0,2\pi]\), work well), compute \(r\) at each one, plot every resulting point \((r,\theta)\) — remembering last lesson’s rule for a negative \(r\) — and connect them in the order \(\theta\) increases. No pattern-recognition is required; the table always tells the truth about the curve, one point at a time.

Example 1

Build a table for the Archimedean spiral \(r=\theta\) over \(\theta\in[0,2\pi]\), and describe the resulting shape.

Solution:

\(\theta=0:\ r=0 \qquad \theta=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\approx1.57:\ r\approx1.57 \qquad \theta=\pi\approx3.14:\ r\approx3.14\)

\(\theta=\tfrac{3\pi}{2}\approx4.71:\ r\approx4.71 \qquad \theta=2\pi\approx6.28:\ r\approx6.28\)

Answer: As \(\theta\) sweeps counterclockwise from \(0\) to \(2\pi\), \(r\) grows right along with it — the point spirals outward, never retracing itself, tracing exactly one loop of an ever-widening coil. Let \(\theta\) continue past \(2\pi\), and the spiral simply keeps opening outward forever.

Building a full table every time is reliable, but it is also slow — and often unnecessary. The three symmetry tests let you predict, algebraically, that half (or more) of your table is a mirror image of the other half, before you compute a single extra point.

Example 2

Test \(r=3+3\cos\theta\) for symmetry about the polar axis.

Solution:

Replace \(\theta\) with \(-\theta\): since cosine is an even function, \(\cos(-\theta)=\cos\theta\), so the equation becomes \(r=3+3\cos\theta\) — identical to the original.

Answer: Symmetric about the polar axis. Only the table from \(\theta=0\) to \(\theta=\pi\) needs to be built by hand; the rest is that same table, reflected across the horizontal axis.

Example 3

Test \(r=2\sin\theta\) for symmetry about the line \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\).

Solution:

Replace \(\theta\) with \(\pi-\theta\): since \(\sin(\pi-\theta)=\sin\theta\), the equation becomes \(r=2\sin\theta\) — identical to the original.

Answer: Symmetric about the line \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\) (the vertical axis). This is a circle passing through the pole, centered above it — a shape you will name precisely tomorrow.

Example 4

Test \(r^2=9\cos2\theta\) for symmetry about the pole.

Solution:

Replace \(\theta\) with \(\theta+\pi\): \(\cos\big(2(\theta+\pi)\big)=\cos(2\theta+2\pi)=\cos2\theta\), so the equation is unchanged.

Answer: Symmetric about the pole. This particular equation graphs as a lemniscate, a figure-eight curve outside today’s scope — but notice that the symmetry test needed no special knowledge of that shape to work. The algebra alone was enough.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Concluding that a curve is not symmetric just because one substitution test fails. Because a single point has infinitely many polar names, a symmetry test failing tells you only that this particular substitution did not confirm the symmetry — the graph might still be symmetric, provable by a different equivalent representation. Treat a passed test as proof; treat a failed test as inconclusive, not as disproof.

Example 5

Build a table for \(r=1+2\cos\theta\) at \(\theta=0,\ \frac{\pi}{3},\ \frac{\pi}{2},\ \frac{2\pi}{3},\ \pi,\ \frac{4\pi}{3},\ \frac{5\pi}{3},\ 2\pi\), and describe anything unexpected.

Solution:

\(\theta=0:r=3 \quad \tfrac{\pi}{3}:r=2 \quad \tfrac{\pi}{2}:r=1 \quad \tfrac{2\pi}{3}:r=0 \quad \pi:r=-1 \quad \tfrac{4\pi}{3}:r=0 \quad \tfrac{5\pi}{3}:r=2 \quad 2\pi:r=3\)

Answer: Between \(\theta=\frac{2\pi}{3}\) and \(\theta=\frac{4\pi}{3}\), \(r\) turns negative — at \(\theta=\pi\), \(r=-1\) plots (by last lesson’s rule) at the point one unit out along \(\theta=0\), not out along \(\theta=\pi\) at all. That negative stretch folds a small second loop inside the larger one — a shape with a name, limaçon (French for “snail”), that you will meet in full later this unit. Nothing about today’s table-and-plot method broke; a genuinely new shape simply fell out of it honestly, cell by cell.

💻 On Your Calculator: Polar Graphing in Desmos

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), type r = theta directly into the expression list. Desmos recognizes any equation written in \(r\) and \(\theta\) (typed as theta) as a polar curve automatically — there is no separate mode to switch on. Open the graph settings (the wrench icon) to confirm \(\theta\) sweeps from \(0\) to at least \(2\pi\) (extend it further to watch the spiral keep opening). Then graph r = 1 + 2cos(theta) from Example 5, and trace along the curve near \(\theta=\pi\) to watch Desmos draw the inner loop — the same loop your table predicted from the negative \(r\)-values alone.

▶  Try it yourself: Test \(r=4-4\sin\theta\) for symmetry about the line \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\).
Replace \(\theta\) with \(\pi-\theta\): \(\sin(\pi-\theta)=\sin\theta\), so the equation becomes \(r=4-4\sin\theta\) — unchanged. Symmetric about the line \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\).
▶  Try it yourself: Build a table for \(r=2\theta\) at \(\theta=0,\frac{\pi}{2},\pi\), and describe how it compares to Example 1’s spiral.
\(\theta=0:r=0\); \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}:r=\pi\approx3.14\); \(\theta=\pi:r=2\pi\approx6.28\). Same spiral shape as Example 1, but every value of \(r\) is doubled — the coil opens twice as fast for the same rotation.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
A coiled garden hose, the groove cut into a vinyl record, and a rolled-up scroll of parchment are all, geometrically, close cousins of Archimedes’s spiral — a curve that keeps its distance from the center growing at exactly the same rate no matter how far around it has already gone. Simple, disciplined growth, wound patiently around a single point: a small, quiet kind of beauty that a table of nine numbers was enough to reveal.

Conclusion

A table, built without any prior expectation of shape, and three symmetry tests that can cut that table’s work in half or more — together, these are a genuinely universal method: they will graph a spiral, a circle, a limaçon with a hidden loop, or a curve you have never seen before, with equal reliability. Tomorrow, two of the shapes you met only by accident today — a circle passing through the pole, and a cardioid’s single clean loop — get a shortcut of their own: a signature you can read directly off the equation, no table required at all.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does building a table of \((\theta,r)\) values work as a universal method for graphing any polar equation, even one whose shape you cannot predict in advance?

2. How does confirming a symmetry algebraically let you cut your table-building work in half or more, without any loss of accuracy?

3. Why should a failed symmetry test be read as inconclusive rather than as proof that no symmetry exists?

4. What must happen at the values of \(\theta\) where \(r\) turns negative, and why can that create a feature — like an inner loop — that a rectangular graph never would?
