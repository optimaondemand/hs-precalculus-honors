Polar Coordinates
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to plot a point given in polar form \((r,\theta)\), including a point whose \(r\)-value is negative.

  • I will be able to convert a point from rectangular form \((x,y)\) to polar form \((r,\theta)\), and from polar form back to rectangular form.

  • I will be able to name several different polar representations of the very same point, and explain why the polar system allows this while the rectangular system does not.

📚 VOCABULARY

Pole: the fixed reference point of a polar coordinate system — the polar system's counterpart to the rectangular origin.

Polar axis: the fixed ray, drawn horizontally to the right of the pole, from which every angle \(\theta\) is measured.

Polar coordinates \((r,\theta)\): an ordered pair that locates a point by a directed distance \(r\) from the pole and an angle \(\theta\) measured counterclockwise from the polar axis.

Directed distance: the signed value of \(r\) — positive when measured along the terminal ray of \(\theta\) itself, negative when measured along the ray directly opposite it.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

The story is told, and told a little too tidily to be entirely certain in every detail, that René Descartes, lying ill in bed one morning, watched a fly crawling across the ceiling and realized he could pin down its exact position with only two numbers — its distance from one wall and its distance from an adjoining wall. Whether or not a fly was truly involved, the insight behind the story is real and it is Descartes’s: two perpendicular measurements, an \(x\) and a \(y\), are enough to name any point in a plane. That single idea is the rectangular coordinate system you have used in every math course since, and it is one of the most productive ideas in the history of mathematics.

But notice what the fly’s two wall-distances are quietly assuming: that the most natural way to describe a position is by two straight-line measurements at right angles. For a great many things in nature, that assumption is simply the wrong one. A radar operator does not describe an aircraft by how far east and how far north it sits; the screen reports a bearing and a range — a direction and a distance from the radar dish itself. A planet does not orbit by creeping so many miles “across” and so many miles “up”; it sweeps around a central point at a constantly turning angle. Whenever a phenomenon is organized around a center — something rotating, radiating, or spiraling outward from a single fixed point — describing it with distance-and-direction from that point is not a mathematical curiosity. It is simply the truer description. Today you meet the coordinate system built for exactly that purpose.

The Polar Address: Pole, Polar Axis, and \((r,\theta)\)

A polar coordinate system starts from a single fixed point called the pole and a single fixed ray from that point called the polar axis, drawn horizontally to the right, exactly where the positive \(x\)-axis would sit in a rectangular grid. Every point in the plane is then named by an ordered pair \((r,\theta)\): \(\theta\) is the angle, measured counterclockwise from the polar axis, that tells you which direction to face, and \(r\) is the distance you walk in that direction to reach the point. To plot \((3,\frac{\pi}{3})\), rotate counterclockwise from the polar axis through an angle of \(\frac{\pi}{3}\), then walk \(3\) units straight out along that new ray. The angle sets the direction; the distance sets how far.

A ray, once you have turned to face it, points only one way — but \(r\) is allowed to be negative, and a negative directed distance is not simply “fewer units.” It sends you the other way entirely.

Plotting a Negative \(r\)

To plot \((r,\theta)\) when \(r<0\): first face the direction of \(\theta\), exactly as before — then, instead of walking forward, walk \(|r|\) units backward, through the pole and out along the ray directly opposite \(\theta\). The point you land on is identical to the point named by \((|r|,\,\theta+\pi)\). A negative \(r\) does not shrink the distance; it reverses the direction.

Key Fact: Converting Between the Two Systems

\(x = r\cos\theta \qquad y = r\sin\theta \qquad r^2 = x^2+y^2 \qquad \tan\theta = \dfrac{y}{x}\)

The first two formulas turn a polar point into a rectangular one directly — no thought required about signs or quadrants. The last two turn a rectangular point into a polar one, but they demand a moment of judgment: \(r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) is always nonnegative, and \(\tan\theta=\frac{y}{x}\) only ever hands you a reference angle. You must still look at the actual signs of \(x\) and \(y\) to decide which quadrant \(\theta\) truly belongs in — the formula cannot see the point’s location; only you, looking at the point, can.

Example 1

Plot the point \(\left(4,\dfrac{\pi}{3}\right)\), and convert it to rectangular form.

Solution:

Face the direction \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{3}\) (60° above the polar axis) and walk \(4\) units out along that ray. To convert:

\(x = 4\cos\dfrac{\pi}{3} = 4\left(\dfrac12\right) = 2 \qquad y = 4\sin\dfrac{\pi}{3} = 4\left(\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)=2\sqrt3\)

Answer: Rectangular form \((2,\,2\sqrt3)\).

Example 2

Plot the point \(\left(-2,\dfrac{\pi}{6}\right)\), and give an equivalent representation with a positive \(r\).

Solution:

Face \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{6}\), but since \(r=-2\) is negative, walk \(2\) units backward — along the ray opposite \(\frac{\pi}{6}\), namely \(\frac{\pi}{6}+\pi=\frac{7\pi}{6}\).

Answer: \(\left(-2,\dfrac{\pi}{6}\right)\) names the identical point as \(\left(2,\dfrac{7\pi}{6}\right)\).

Example 3

Convert the rectangular point \((-1,\sqrt3)\) to polar form, with \(r>0\) and \(0\le\theta<2\pi\).

Solution:

\(r=\sqrt{(-1)^2+(\sqrt3)^2}=\sqrt{1+3}=2\)

\(\tan\theta=\dfrac{\sqrt3}{-1}=-\sqrt3\), giving a reference angle of \(\frac{\pi}{3}\). Since \(x<0\) and \(y>0\), the point lies in Quadrant II, so \(\theta=\pi-\frac{\pi}{3}=\frac{2\pi}{3}\).

Answer: \(\left(2,\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\right)\). Check: \(2\cos\frac{2\pi}{3}=2\left(-\frac12\right)=-1\) ✓ and \(2\sin\frac{2\pi}{3}=2\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)=\sqrt3\) ✓.

Example 4

Convert the rectangular point \((0,-6)\) to polar form, with \(r>0\) and \(0\le\theta<2\pi\).

Solution:

This point lies directly on the negative \(y\)-axis, so \(\tan\theta\) is undefined (\(x=0\)) — reason from the picture instead of the formula. The distance from the pole is \(r=\sqrt{0^2+(-6)^2}=6\), and the direction straight down the negative \(y\)-axis is \(\theta=\frac{3\pi}{2}\).

Answer: \(\left(6,\dfrac{3\pi}{2}\right)\). Check: \(6\cos\frac{3\pi}{2}=6(0)=0\) ✓ and \(6\sin\frac{3\pi}{2}=6(-1)=-6\) ✓.

Example 5

Give three different polar representations of the point \(\left(4,\dfrac{\pi}{3}\right)\).

Solution:

Winding a full \(2\pi\) around, in either direction, always returns to the same ray, and reversing both the direction and the sign of \(r\) also lands on the same point.

Answer: \(\left(4,\dfrac{\pi}{3}+2\pi\right)=\left(4,\dfrac{7\pi}{3}\right)\);   \(\left(4,\dfrac{\pi}{3}-2\pi\right)=\left(4,-\dfrac{5\pi}{3}\right)\);   \(\left(-4,\dfrac{\pi}{3}+\pi\right)=\left(-4,\dfrac{4\pi}{3}\right)\). Every one of these four ordered pairs, plus the original, names the exact same location in the plane.

💻 On Your Calculator: Checking a Conversion

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), you can check any polar-to-rectangular conversion instantly by typing the point as (4cos(pi/3), 4sin(pi/3)) directly into the expression list — Desmos computes both coordinates and plots the resulting rectangular point for you, no matter whether \(r\) is positive or negative. Type Example 2’s point as (-2cos(pi/6), -2sin(pi/6)) and confirm it lands in exactly the same place as (2cos(7pi/6), 2sin(7pi/6)) — two very different-looking ordered pairs, one identical point.

▶  Try it yourself: Convert \(\left(3,\dfrac{5\pi}{4}\right)\) to rectangular form.
\(x=3\cos\frac{5\pi}{4}=3\left(-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)=-\frac{3\sqrt2}{2}\), \(y=3\sin\frac{5\pi}{4}=3\left(-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)=-\frac{3\sqrt2}{2}\). Rectangular form: \(\left(-\frac{3\sqrt2}{2},-\frac{3\sqrt2}{2}\right)\).
▶  Try it yourself: Convert the rectangular point \((2,2)\) to polar form, with \(r>0\) and \(0\le\theta<2\pi\).
\(r=\sqrt{2^2+2^2}=\sqrt8=2\sqrt2\). \(\tan\theta=\frac{2}{2}=1\), and since both coordinates are positive (Quadrant I), \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{4}\). Polar form: \(\left(2\sqrt2,\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\).
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Trusting \(\tan\theta=\frac{y}{x}\) to hand back the correct angle on its own. A calculator’s inverse tangent only ever returns an angle in Quadrant I or IV — it has no way of knowing whether the original point actually sat in Quadrant II or III. Example 3’s point \((-1,\sqrt3)\) has \(\tan\theta=-\sqrt3\), and a calculator’s \(\tan^{-1}(-\sqrt3)\) reports \(-\frac{\pi}{3}\) — a Quadrant IV angle, which is simply wrong for a point with negative \(x\) and positive \(y\). Always find the reference angle from the tangent, then place \(\theta\) in the correct quadrant by looking at the actual signs of \(x\) and \(y\), never by trusting the inverse tangent alone.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
A ship’s radar screen, a weather station tracking a storm, and an air-traffic controller watching a runway approach all read the same way: a green dot glowing at some bearing and some range from the dish at the center of the screen. None of them think in terms of \(x\) and \(y\). They think in terms of a direction and a distance from where they are standing — which is to say, every radar screen ever built is, quietly, a polar coordinate system.

Conclusion

A point now has two complete addresses: the rectangular pair you have used for years, and the new polar pair — a direction and a distance from a single center. Neither address is more correct than the other; each simply tells the truth about the point in a different language, and today you learned to translate fluently between them, in both directions, including the perfectly ordinary trick of a negative distance sending you the other way. Tomorrow that same dictionary — \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\), \(r^2=x^2+y^2\), \(\tan\theta=\frac{y}{x}\) — stops translating single points and starts translating whole equations, so that an entire curve can be described in whichever language suits it best.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why might a phenomenon organized around a single center — radar, orbits, spirals — be described more naturally by a distance and a direction than by two perpendicular measurements?

2. What does it mean, precisely, for a negative value of \(r\) to send you in the opposite direction rather than simply a shorter distance in the same direction?

3. Why does a single point in the polar system have infinitely many valid names, when a point in the rectangular system has exactly one?

4. Why must you check the actual signs of \(x\) and \(y\) before trusting an angle produced by \(\tan\theta=\frac{y}{x}\)?
