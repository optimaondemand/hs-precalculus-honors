🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to trace the semester's five units as one connected geometry of the plane, naming the specific mechanisms — not just the vocabulary — that carry the unit circle into graphs, polar coordinates, complex numbers, and vectors.

  • I will be able to solve mixed-review problems that require translating fluently among rectangular, polar, parametric, trigonometric (complex), and vector descriptions of the same point or quantity.

  • I will be able to rehearse the semester's standing disciplines on demand — reference-angle-and-quadrant, guarding against the ambiguous SSA case, and round-trip verification of every form conversion — ready for the final.

  • I will be able to state, in my own words, how this entire course's arc points toward two specific ideas — a limit of partial sums, and a limit of a difference quotient — that the next mathematics course will take up in earnest.

📚 VOCABULARY, REVISITED

These terms did not stay in the units where you met them. Watch how far each one traveled.

Reference angle: The acute angle between an angle's terminal ray and the \(x\)-axis, named in Unit 6 as the key that unlocks exact trigonometric values in any quadrant. Unit 7 leans on it constantly to solve equations and evaluate identities away from the first quadrant, and Unit 9 calls on it again to find the argument of a complex number that does not happen to sit in Quadrant I.

Inverse trigonometric function (\(\arcsin\), \(\arccos\), \(\arctan\)): Built in Unit 7 by restricting a periodic function's domain until it earns an inverse — the same restriction move from the prior semester's work on functions. From that point on, \(\arctan\) with a quadrant check becomes the standing tool for recovering an angle: from a rectangular point in Unit 8's polar conversions, from a complex number's argument in Unit 9, and from a vector's direction angle in Unit 10.

Amplitude, period, and midline: Named in Unit 7 to describe a sinusoid's height, repeat-length, and center line. The same three ideas do identical work, unnamed, in Unit 8: a polar rose's “amplitude” is the farthest \(r\) ever reaches, and its “period” is how much of a full turn is needed before the petal pattern retraces itself.

Modulus and argument: A complex number's distance from the origin and its angle from the positive real axis (Unit 9) — and, one unit later, exactly what a vector's magnitude and direction angle are, wearing different clothing (Unit 10).

Resultant: In Unit 10, the single vector that replaces two vectors added tip-to-tail. Find its length without a picture, and you are running Unit 7's Law of Cosines under another name — the same subtraction of a doubled product of sides and a cosine.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Eighteen weeks ago this semester began with Hipparchus building the first table of chords so that astronomers could stop guessing, and Eratosthenes measuring the whole Earth from two shadows and a well. In between: Ptolemy's theorem quietly hiding the sum formulas two thousand years before anyone named them; Fourier's astonishing claim that every periodic motion, however jagged, is built from sums of sines; Bernoulli asking that a spiral be carved on his tombstone with the motto eadem mutata resurgo — “though changed, I rise again the same”; Cardano enduring what he called “mental tortures” over a number whose square is negative, and Bombelli daring to compute with it anyway; and Hamilton, out walking, seeing the multiplication rule for space itself and carving it into a bridge before he could forget it. It can feel like six separate countries: triangles, waves, spirals, an impossible number, arrows. It is not. This week — finals week, and the last week of this course — there is no new mathematics. There is instead the work of seeing the whole semester as one geometry, because the final will hand you problems without unit labels, and because every one of these six countries turns out to describe the same plane.

The week has exactly three parts: this review lesson, a paired review activity (Focus-style — your teacher method-grades its two starred problems), and the Semester 2 final. Here is the story so far, unit by unit, followed by the threads that stitch it together and three worked examples that each live in two or more units at once.

Unit 6 — Right Triangle Trigonometry: What You Must Be Able to Do

  • Convert between degrees and radians, and compute arc length and sector area for any angle.
  • Define the six trigonometric functions from a right triangle and from the unit circle alike, and find missing sides or angles.
  • Derive exact values at the special angles from the 30-60-90 and 45-45-90 triangles, and generate the complete unit circle from those seeds.
  • Evaluate any of the six functions at any angle using reference angles and quadrant sign analysis, choosing wisely among the unit circle, identities, and technology.
  • Solve elevation, depression, and multi-station surveying problems — carrying, in the unit whose virtue is responsibility, real weight for precise measurement.

Unit 7 — Trigonometric Functions: What You Must Be Able to Do

  • Graph sine, cosine, and the other four functions under full transformations, reading amplitude, period, midline, and phase shift from an equation or a graph in either direction.
  • Build and interpret sinusoidal models of real periodic phenomena — tides, daylight, wheels — choosing sine, cosine, or tangent appropriately.
  • Solve trigonometric equations exactly, including those requiring an identity substitution first, both on an interval and in general form.
  • Solve any triangle with the Law of Sines or the Law of Cosines, correctly resolving the ambiguous SSA case and computing area two ways.
  • Prove and strategically deploy the full identity toolbox — Pythagorean, sum/difference, double- and half-angle — writing verifications as disciplined, one-side-at-a-time logical chains, the ordered self-government this unit is named for.

Unit 8 — Polar Coordinates & Parametric Equations: What You Must Be Able to Do

  • Plot points and rewrite equations between the rectangular and polar systems, including points with a negative \(r\).
  • Graph the special polar families — circles, cardioids, limaçons, lemniscates, roses — and classify a limaçon or predict a rose's petal count directly from its equation.
  • Sketch parametric curves with correct orientation, and convert fluently between parametric and rectangular form.
  • Build parametric models of real motion (projectiles, pursuit, orbits) and answer questions about range, height, and timing.
  • Choose among rectangular, polar, and parametric descriptions of the same curve and translate between all three — wonder at the many shapes one radius-and-angle rule can draw.

Unit 9 — Complex Numbers: What You Must Be Able to Do

  • Plot complex numbers and perform arithmetic — add, subtract, multiply, conjugate — both algebraically and as geometric transformations of the plane.
  • Compute distance and midpoint between complex numbers, and solve applied and quadratic problems with complex solutions.
  • Convert a complex number fluently between rectangular, polar, and trigonometric form.
  • Multiply and divide complex numbers in trigonometric form by combining moduli and arguments, and apply De Moivre's Theorem to powers and to finding roots.
  • Narrate the historical argument — Cardano's torment to Bombelli's daring to Argand and Gauss's plane — that finally legitimized \(i\); this is the unit named for courage.

Unit 10 — Vectors: What You Must Be Able to Do

  • Represent vectors in component, linear (\(a\mathbf{i} + b\mathbf{j}\)), and trigonometric (magnitude-direction) form, and convert fluently among all three.
  • Add, subtract, and scale vectors both graphically (tip-to-tail, parallelogram) and algebraically, and find the magnitude and direction of a scalar multiple.
  • Solve velocity/wind/current and force-equilibrium problems by resolving vectors into components.
  • Compute the dot product, use it to find the angle between two vectors and to test orthogonality, and compute projections, including the work interpretation.
  • Translate fluently among vector, complex-number, and polar descriptions of the same planar quantity — magnitudes and directions cooperating toward a common resultant, the service this unit is named for.

The Threads That Tie the Semester

These are the connections the final will reward you for seeing. Each one is a fact, not a slogan — and each is rehearsed in the worked examples below.

  • Unit circle → graphs → polar coordinates: three unrollings of one idea. The Unit 6 unit circle defines sine and cosine as coordinates on a circle. Unit 7 unrolls that same circle along a time axis to produce the sine and cosine graphs — the Wave Machine VR session made the mechanism literal, a rotating arm tracing the very wave a wall graph drew in sync. Unit 8 puts the circle back at the center of its own coordinate system, describing every point by the same radius-and-angle pair the circle had all along.
  • Complex multiplication in trigonometric form is rotation-and-scaling — and that rotation is exactly what the sum formulas compute. Multiply two complex numbers in trig form and their moduli multiply while their arguments add; expand that product by hand and the real and imaginary parts that fall out are precisely Unit 7's cosine and sine sum formulas. De Moivre's Theorem is that same rule applied to a number multiplied by itself, over and over.
  • The polar form of a complex number and the trigonometric form of a vector are one idea in two uniforms. Both describe a planar quantity by a distance from the origin and an angle from the positive axis — modulus and argument in Unit 9, magnitude and direction in Unit 10. Unit 10's closing synthesis lesson says this outright.
  • Parametric motion and velocity vectors describe the same plane from two directions. Parametric equations answer “where is it, at this moment?”; a velocity vector answers “how fast, and which way, is it changing?” Both questions are read off the same pair of components.
  • The Law of Cosines, derived in Unit 7 to solve triangles, reappears without announcement inside the dot-product angle formula in Unit 10 — the same triangle, the same subtraction of a doubled product of sides and a cosine, now wearing vector notation.

Now the rehearsal: three worked examples, each deliberately straddling two or more units, presented exactly as your final-exam work should look.

Example 1 (Unit 6 meets Unit 7)

A Ferris wheel has center height 15 m and radius 12 m. It completes one full revolution every 8 seconds, and a rider boards at the bottom of the wheel at \(t = 0\).

① Using right-triangle trigonometry, find the rider's height above the ground at \(t = 1\) second. ② Write the sinusoidal height model \(h(t)\) and confirm it agrees with ①. ③ Find the first time the rider reaches a height of 21 m, using an exact special-angle value. ④ Name the thread this problem walks.

Solution:

① In one second the wheel sweeps through an angle \(\varphi = \dfrac{2\pi}{8}(1) = \dfrac{\pi}{4} = 45^\circ\), measured from the downward vertical. The spoke to the rider is the hypotenuse of a right triangle of length \(R = 12\) m; the leg parallel to the vertical — the rider's drop below the center — is the side adjacent to \(\varphi\):

drop below center \(= R\cos\varphi = 12\cos 45^\circ = 12\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2} = 6\sqrt2 \approx 8.49\text{ m}\)

height above ground \(= 15 - 6\sqrt2 \approx 6.51\text{ m}\)

② The same rotation, tracked continuously, gives the sinusoidal model:

\(h(t) = 15 - 12\cos\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{4}t\right)\)

At \(t=1\): \(h(1) = 15 - 12\cos\dfrac{\pi}{4} = 15 - 6\sqrt2 \approx 6.51\) m — the same number as ①, because the model is the right triangle, tracked as \(t\) varies.

③ Set \(h(t) = 21\):

\(15 - 12\cos\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{4}t\right) = 21 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \cos\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{4}t\right) = -\dfrac12\)

Cosine equals \(-\tfrac12\) first at a reference angle of \(60^\circ\) in Quadrant II, i.e. at \(120^\circ = \dfrac{2\pi}{3}\). So:

\(\dfrac{\pi}{4}t = \dfrac{2\pi}{3} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; t = \dfrac{2\pi}{3}\cdot\dfrac{4}{\pi} = \dfrac{8}{3} \approx 2.67\text{ s}\)

Answer: The rider is at \(6 - 6\sqrt2 + 15 \approx 6.51\) m at \(t=1\) s, and first reaches 21 m at \(t = \dfrac{8}{3}\) s. ④ The thread: a right triangle frozen at one instant and a sinusoid tracked over all instants are the same rotation — and the special-angle values Unit 6 taught you to read off a 30-60-90 triangle are exactly the exact values Unit 7 needs to solve the equation without a calculator.

Example 2 (Unit 7 meets Unit 9)

① Use the sum formulas to find the exact value of \(\cos 75^\circ\) and \(\sin 75^\circ\). ② Let \(z_1 = \operatorname{cis}45^\circ\) and \(z_2 = \operatorname{cis}30^\circ\) (both modulus 1). Multiply \(z_1 z_2\) in trigonometric form, expand the product by hand, and show it produces the identical real and imaginary parts found in ①.

Solution:

① With \(75^\circ = 45^\circ + 30^\circ\):

\(\cos 75^\circ = \cos45^\circ\cos30^\circ - \sin45^\circ\sin30^\circ = \dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2} - \dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\dfrac12 = \dfrac{\sqrt6 - \sqrt2}{4} \approx 0.2588\)

\(\sin 75^\circ = \sin45^\circ\cos30^\circ + \cos45^\circ\sin30^\circ = \dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2} + \dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\dfrac12 = \dfrac{\sqrt6 + \sqrt2}{4} \approx 0.9659\)

② Trigonometric-form multiplication says: multiply moduli, add arguments. Both moduli are 1, so \(z_1 z_2 = \operatorname{cis}(45^\circ+30^\circ) = \operatorname{cis}75^\circ\) — but watch why that rule is true by expanding the product directly:

\(z_1 z_2 = (\cos45^\circ + i\sin45^\circ)(\cos30^\circ + i\sin30^\circ)\)

\(= (\cos45^\circ\cos30^\circ - \sin45^\circ\sin30^\circ) + i(\sin45^\circ\cos30^\circ + \cos45^\circ\sin30^\circ)\)

The real part is exactly the cosine sum formula from ①; the imaginary part is exactly the sine sum formula. So \(z_1 z_2 = \dfrac{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}{4} + i\,\dfrac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4} = \cos75^\circ + i\sin75^\circ\) — matching ① term for term, not merely in decimal.

Answer: \(\cos75^\circ = \dfrac{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}{4}\), \(\sin75^\circ = \dfrac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}\); multiplying \(\operatorname{cis}45^\circ\) by \(\operatorname{cis}30^\circ\) reproduces both, because the “add the arguments” rule for trigonometric-form multiplication is the angle-sum identities, discovered from the other direction.

Example 3 (the grand arc: Units 8, 9, and 10 together)

A point traces the circle \(x(\theta) = 6\cos\theta,\ y(\theta) = 6\sin\theta\) (a parametric description, Unit 8). At \(\theta = \dfrac{2\pi}{3}\) (\(120^\circ\)), express the point's location in every language this semester built: rectangular coordinates, polar coordinates, a complex number in trigonometric form, and a vector in magnitude-direction form — verifying that every conversion round-trips exactly.

Solution:

Rectangular, from the parametric equations (Unit 8):

\(x = 6\cos120^\circ = 6\left(-\dfrac12\right) = -3, \qquad y = 6\sin120^\circ = 6\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2} = 3\sqrt3\)

The point is \((-3,\ 3\sqrt3)\).

Polar (Unit 8): the radius was built into the parametrization: \(r = 6\), \(\theta = 120^\circ\). Check by the distance formula, ignoring where the point came from:

\(r = \sqrt{(-3)^2 + (3\sqrt3)^2} = \sqrt{9+27} = \sqrt{36} = 6\) ✓, reference angle \(\arctan\!\left(\dfrac{3\sqrt3}{3}\right) = \arctan\sqrt3 = 60^\circ\), Quadrant II \(\Rightarrow \theta = 180^\circ - 60^\circ = 120^\circ\) ✓

Complex trigonometric form (Unit 9): the same number, \(z = -3 + 3\sqrt3\,i\), rewritten with the modulus and argument just found:

\(z = 6(\cos120^\circ + i\sin120^\circ)\)

Round-trip check: \(6\cos120^\circ = -3\) ✓ and \(6\sin120^\circ = 3\sqrt3\) ✓ — the trigonometric form regenerates the original real and imaginary parts exactly.

Vector, magnitude-direction form (Unit 10): the same ordered pair, read as a vector from the origin, \(\mathbf{v} = \langle -3,\ 3\sqrt3\rangle\), has magnitude \(|\mathbf{v}| = 6\) and direction angle \(120^\circ\) — identical numbers to the modulus and argument above, because a vector's magnitude-direction form is polar form wearing an arrow instead of an \(i\).

Round-trip check: \(6\cos120^\circ = -3\) and \(6\sin120^\circ = 3\sqrt3\) recover \(\mathbf{v} = \langle -3, 3\sqrt3\rangle\) exactly ✓.

Answer: One location, four names, all consistent: rectangular \((-3, 3\sqrt3)\), polar \((6, 120^\circ)\), complex trigonometric form \(6(\cos120^\circ+i\sin120^\circ)\), and vector \(\langle-3,3\sqrt3\rangle\) with magnitude 6 and direction \(120^\circ\). This is the grand arc of the semester: rectangular, polar, parametric, complex, and vector descriptions are five languages for one plane, and every conversion between them is required, by the geometry itself, to come back to where it started.

The Semester's Standing Disciplines — Gathered in One Place

Five habits have followed you since Unit 6 began. They are not slogans; each is a method with a reason, and the final assumes all five.

  • Reference angle plus quadrant sign — never a memorized table alone. Every exact value this semester, from a unit-circle coordinate to a complex number's argument, was found this way, not recalled by rote.
  • Guard against the ambiguous case before you trust a single Law-of-Sines answer. Given two sides and a non-included angle, check for zero, one, or two triangles before reporting a solution as final.
  • Verify every trigonometric-form, polar, or vector conversion by converting back. The round trip is the proof; Example 3 above is nothing but four round trips in a row.
  • “The calculator confirms; your algebra convinces” — carried over from last semester, now adapted to Desmos's polar and parametric modes: switching modes correctly, and reading degrees versus radians correctly, is part of the discipline, not an afterthought.
  • Name the domain of honesty for any periodic or vector model. A sinusoidal daylight model, a projectile's flight, a wind-and-current heading — each is honest only within the interval or context it was built to describe.

▶  Try it yourself: A vector has magnitude 8 and direction angle \(210^\circ\). Write it in component form, write the corresponding complex number in trigonometric form, and use De Moivre's Theorem to find its square — then verify that answer by squaring the rectangular form directly. Work it on paper before you peek.
Component form: \(\cos210^\circ = -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\), \(\sin210^\circ=-\tfrac12\) (reference angle \(30^\circ\), Quadrant III), so \(\mathbf{v} = \langle 8\cos210^\circ,\ 8\sin210^\circ\rangle = \langle -4\sqrt3,\ -4\rangle\). Trigonometric form: \(z = 8(\cos210^\circ+i\sin210^\circ) = -4\sqrt3 - 4i\). By De Moivre, \(z^2 = 8^2(\cos420^\circ + i\sin420^\circ) = 64(\cos60^\circ+i\sin60^\circ) = 64\left(\tfrac12+i\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) = 32 + 32\sqrt3\,i\). Direct check: \((-4\sqrt3-4i)^2 = (4\sqrt3)^2 + 2(4\sqrt3)(4i)\cdot(-1)(-1)+(4i)^2\)... expanded carefully as \((a+bi)^2=a^2-b^2+2abi\) with \(a=-4\sqrt3, b=-4\): \(a^2-b^2 = 48-16=32\) and \(2ab = 2(-4\sqrt3)(-4)=32\sqrt3\), giving \(32+32\sqrt3\,i\) — matching De Moivre's answer exactly.

Conclusion

This course began with Fibonacci's rabbits and a sequence approaching a number it could never quite reach. It found the truth-telling of end behavior and asymptotes, the strange constancy of \(e\), functions undoing each other with courtesy, and Apollonius's cone yielding four curves that still serve as satellite dishes and whispering galleries. Then this semester rebuilt the entire plane in five languages: a circle unrolled into waves, waves solving triangles, triangles opening onto polar gardens, gardens hiding an impossible number that Cardano suffered over and Gauss finally gave a home, and that number's plane turning out to be the same plane a vector already lived on. Ten units, one geometry, examined from every angle mathematics has invented for looking at it. Two ideas from this course are not finished business — they are doors left open on purpose. The partial sums you tested for convergence in Unit 1, getting closer and closer to a limiting value without ever being told to stop early, are exactly the objects that a limit will one day turn into an integral. The difference quotient you built and simplified in Unit 4, a secant's slope closing in on a single point, is exactly the expression that a limit will one day turn into a derivative. You do not need to know what a limit is yet to have already done the work a limit will complete. Rest well, work the review activity with full method, and then write a final that shows — to your teacher, and to yourself — that this geometry of the plane, in all five of its languages, is genuinely yours.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. In what precise sense are the unit circle, the sine graph, and a polar rose curve the same mathematical object, examined at three different distances?

2. Why does multiplying two complex numbers in trigonometric form amount to adding their angles — and why is that the very same fact as the trigonometric sum formulas?

3. Vectors, complex numbers, and polar coordinates all reduce a planar quantity to a magnitude and an angle. What is gained, and what is lost, by choosing one of these languages over another for a given problem?

4. A cumulative final asks you to solve problems without being told which unit they come from. Which habits of mind — reference angles, guard-and-judge for the ambiguous case, round-trip verification of a conversion — let you recognize, unaided, which of the plane's five languages a problem is quietly asking for?
