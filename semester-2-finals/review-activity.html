Review Activity: Mixed Set & the Five Languages of the Plane
Assignment · 16 pts · Focus-style (teacher-graded on the two method problems)
📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
This is finals week's review activity — your dress rehearsal for the Semester 2 final, in two parts. Part A is a mixed problem set that ranges across all five units of this semester with no unit labels attached: recognizing which tool a problem wants is itself the skill being rehearsed. Part B is the one-page, hand-drawn Five Languages of the Plane Field Guide — the semester's ways of writing down the same plane, gathered onto a single sheet you will wish you could bring to the final (you cannot; making it puts it where you can bring it). The two gold-badged ⭐ METHOD PROBLEMS are the ones your teacher grades for method: one full ambiguous-case trial from Unit 7's discipline, and one complete five-language conversion proved by converting every form back to where it started. Give every problem honest work, and give the method problems the complete procedure with nothing skipped because it felt obvious.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
Stripped of its unit label, how does a problem tell you which of the semester's tools it needs — and which of the standing disciplines must ride along with that tool?
📝 PART A — MIXED PROBLEM SET (all five units, no labels)

Show your work for every problem in neat vertical form — one step per line, equals signs aligned, final answers labeled. Draw graphs and diagrams large, with anchor points labeled. Exact values before decimals, throughout.

1
Line of sight: From a point 120 feet from the base of a lighthouse, the angle of elevation to the top of the lighthouse is \(27^\circ\). Find the height of the lighthouse to the nearest foot. Then state the angle of depression an observer at the top of the lighthouse would measure looking back down to that same point, and explain in one sentence why it must equal the angle of elevation.
2
Two exact values, two arguments: Find the exact value of \(\tan\!\left(\dfrac{7\pi}{6}\right)\) and the exact value of \(\sec\!\left(-\dfrac{5\pi}{3}\right)\). For each, name the reference angle, identify the quadrant (or the coterminal first-quadrant angle), and justify the sign of your answer from that quadrant — do not simply state the value.
3
Building the wave: A sinusoidal function reaches a maximum value of 9 at \(x=0\), a minimum value of \(-3\), and completes one full cycle every 8 units of \(x\). Find the amplitude, the midline, and the period, and write the function as a cosine equation of the form \(y = A\cos(Bx) + D\). Verify your equation by checking that it produces both the given maximum and the given minimum.
⭐ METHOD PROBLEM — your teacher grades this one for method
4
A triangle has \(a = 15\), \(b = 20\), and \(A = 40^\circ\). Solve the triangle with the complete guard-and-judge discipline of the ambiguous case, every step labeled: ① post the guard — compute the height \(h = b\sin A\) and compare \(h\), \(a\), and \(b\) to determine in advance how many triangles exist, with a one-sentence reason; ② solve every case the guard allows — find both possible values of \(B\) from the Law of Sines (or state clearly why only one survives), then find the matching \(C\) and side \(c\) for each surviving case, all work shown; ③ compute the area of each surviving triangle using \(\text{Area} = \frac12 ab\sin C\); ④ verdict sentence — state exactly how many triangles this data produces and defend the count by naming which comparison (\(h\) vs. \(a\) vs. \(b\)) decided it.
5
An identity does the heavy lifting: Solve \(\cos(2x) + \sin x = 0\) on \([0, 2\pi)\). You will need to rewrite \(\cos(2x)\) using an identity before the equation becomes solvable by factoring — name the identity you use, show the substitution, factor completely, and list every solution in exact form.
6
Two curves, two habits: ① Convert the polar equation \(r = 6\cos\theta\) to rectangular form (multiply both sides by \(r\) before substituting) and identify the resulting curve completely — center and radius. ② Classify \(r = 3\sin(4\theta)\): is it a rose, and if so, how many petals does it have and how long is each one? State the rule you used to get the petal count.
7
Erasing the clock: A path is traced by \(x(t) = 1 + 2t\), \(y(t) = 4 - t\) for \(0 \le t \le 3\). Eliminate the parameter to find a single equation in \(x\) and \(y\), and identify the resulting curve. Then describe the motion: where does the path start and end (give both \(t\)-values and the corresponding points), and does the object move left-to-right or right-to-left, upward or downward, as \(t\) increases?
⭐ METHOD PROBLEM — your teacher grades this one for method
8
One point, five languages. You are given the point \(P = (2,\, 2\sqrt{3})\) in rectangular form. ① Convert to polar form \((r, \theta)\), giving \(\theta\) as an exact special angle. ② Convert to complex trigonometric form \(z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)\). ③ Convert to vector magnitude-direction form — state \(|\vec{v}|\) and the direction angle, and also write \(\vec{v}\) in component form \(\langle a, b\rangle\). ④ Verify every conversion by converting each of your three answers from ①–③ back to rectangular form and confirming each one reproduces \(P = (2, 2\sqrt{3})\) exactly — show the back-conversion arithmetic for all three, not just one. ⑤ Close with 2–3 sentences explaining why the polar pair, the complex number, and the vector are the same underlying object wearing three different uniforms, and name one operation each uniform makes easier than the others.
9
Honors stretch (three units at once): Find all three cube roots of \(z = 8i\) using De Moivre's Theorem. Give each root in trigonometric form and in exact rectangular form (your reference angles are all special angles). Then treat each root as a position vector from the origin: verify that the three vectors sum to the zero vector, and verify that the three endpoints are mutually equidistant — that is, that they form an equilateral triangle inscribed in a circle centered at the origin. Close with one sentence connecting the "vectors summing to zero" fact here to what it means for three forces of equal magnitude, spaced \(120^\circ\) apart, to hold an object in equilibrium.
📑 PART B — THE FIVE LANGUAGES OF THE PLANE FIELD GUIDE (one hand-drawn page)

On one single page, build a naturalist's field guide to every way you learned this semester to write down a point, a quantity, or a motion in the plane. Rule it as a chart — one row per language, four columns — drawn and lettered by hand. A field guide is meant for identification at a glance in the wild, so keep every entry compact and honest.

The rows (five): rectangular / Cartesian · polar · parametric · complex / trigonometric form · vector / magnitude-direction form.

The columns (four):

  • Notation — how you actually write it down (e.g. \((x,y)\), \((r,\theta)\), \(x(t), y(t)\), \(r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)\), \(\langle a,b\rangle\)).
  • What It's Built For — which operation this language makes easy: rectangular for reading coordinates directly; polar for distance-and-direction and for equations of circles, roses, and cardioids; parametric for motion over time; complex trigonometric form for multiplication and rotation (moduli multiply, arguments add); vector form for addition and composition (resultants add tip-to-tail).
  • Converting to Rectangular — the formula that takes this language back to \((x,y)\) (for rectangular itself, note that it is the home base every other row converts to).
  • A Worked Micro-Example — the same single point in every row, so the chart proves its own self-consistency at a glance. Use the point corresponding to \((3,4)\) throughout: show it as \((3,4)\) in the rectangular row, as \((r,\theta) = (5, 53.13^\circ)\) in the polar row (since \(r=\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5\) and \(\theta = \tan^{-1}(4/3)\)), as \(x(t)=3t,\ y(t)=4t\) at \(t=1\) in the parametric row, as \(z = 3+4i = 5(\cos 53.13^\circ + i\sin 53.13^\circ)\) in the complex row, and as \(\vec v = \langle 3,4\rangle\), \(|\vec v| = 5\), direction angle \(53.13^\circ\) in the vector row. Check each row's own conversion formula actually reproduces \((3,4)\).

This page is Part B of your PDF — and the single best study artifact you will carry into the final. Make it the page you would want back.

✅ SUCCESS CRITERIA
☐

☐

☐

☐

📊 GRADING RUBRIC

Your teacher applies this rubric to the two ⭐ method problems (the rest of the set and the Field Guide are checked for honest completion). This same four-criterion standard governs every hand-graded item in this course, all year — including this final.

Criteria Excellent (4) Proficient (3) Developing (2) Beginning (1) Pts
Understanding of the material
4 pts		 Method fits the problem exactly; definitions, notation, and results are used correctly throughout. Method is sound with a minor conceptual slip that does not derail the solution. Partial grasp; the approach starts correctly but loses the thread. The method does not match the mathematics being asked. 4 pts
Quality of reasoning
4 pts		 Every step follows from the last; the why is stated, and verification is actually performed. Reasoning is mostly explicit; one link in the chain is asserted rather than shown. Steps are present but read as recipe-following; explanations restate what without why. Answer appears with little or no supporting reasoning. 4 pts
Presentation & precision
4 pts		 Clean vertical layout, aligned equals signs, labeled answers, exact values; graphs and diagrams read as visual arguments. Organized and legible with small lapses in labeling or alignment. Readable but disorderly; the grader must hunt for the thread. Work is scattered or illegible; steps cannot be followed. 4 pts
Intellectual honesty
4 pts		 All work shown faithfully as done; uncertainty and gaps are named plainly; nothing is dressed up. Work is genuine; one rough patch is smoothed over rather than acknowledged. Some steps appear reverse-engineered from the answer rather than worked. The page misrepresents how the work was actually done. 4 pts
Total: 16 pts
