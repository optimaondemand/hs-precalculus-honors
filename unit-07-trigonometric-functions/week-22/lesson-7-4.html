Graphs of the Other Four Functions
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to graph \(y=\tan x\), \(y=\cot x\), \(y=\sec x\), and \(y=\csc x\), correctly placing every vertical asymptote.

  • I will be able to state the period and domain of each of the four functions, and explain why each period and domain follows directly from sine and cosine's own zeros.

  • I will be able to graph simple transformations of these four functions, using the period formula \(\dfrac{\pi}{|B|}\) for tangent and cotangent — not the \(\dfrac{2\pi}{|B|}\) formula that governs sine and cosine.

📚 VOCABULARY

Vertical asymptote: a vertical line \(x=a\) that a graph approaches without ever crossing or touching, because the function is undefined at \(a\) and grows without bound on either side of it.

Reciprocal trigonometric function: a function built by taking \(1\) divided by sine or cosine — \(\sec\theta = \dfrac{1}{\cos\theta}\) and \(\csc\theta = \dfrac{1}{\sin\theta}\) — or by dividing cosine by sine instead of sine by cosine, as cotangent does: \(\cot\theta = \dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta} = \dfrac{1}{\tan\theta}\).

Domain restriction: the requirement that a function's input avoid any value that would make a denominator zero — today's reason that four of the six trigonometric functions cannot accept every real number, the way sine and cosine do.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

The names of today's four functions are older than their graphs. Tangent comes from the Latin tangere, "to touch": drop a line tangent to a unit circle at the point \((1,0)\), and the length of that line up to where it meets the terminal side of angle \(\theta\) is literally \(\tan\theta\). Secant comes from secare, "to cut": the line from the origin through the terminal point, extended until it cuts that same tangent line, has length \(\sec\theta\). Long before either function had a graph, it was a literal piece of geometry sitting outside a circle — which is the first hint of what makes today's four functions behave so differently from sine and cosine. Sine and cosine are coordinates; they can never leave the circle, so they can never misbehave. Tangent and secant are lengths of lines built outside the circle, and a line built to meet another line that it happens to be running parallel to has nowhere to go — it simply fails to meet it at all.

Algebraically, that failure is a division by zero. Every one of today's four functions is defined as a quotient of sine and cosine:

\(\tan\theta=\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} \qquad \cot\theta=\dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta} \qquad \sec\theta=\dfrac{1}{\cos\theta} \qquad \csc\theta=\dfrac{1}{\sin\theta}\)

A quotient is undefined the instant its denominator hits zero. Sine and cosine, being coordinates, sweep through zero regularly and without incident — but the moment one of them sits in a denominator, that same zero becomes a place the new function cannot go. Even here, though, the discipline of this unit does not disappear; it changes shape. These four functions still keep their own law with total reliability — they simply include, as part of that law, an honest declaration of exactly where they refuse to answer.

Where Each Function Breaks, and How Often It Repeats

Tangent and cotangent are undefined wherever their own denominator is zero; secant and cosecant borrow their asymptotes directly from cosine's and sine's zeros:

\(\tan\theta,\ \sec\theta\ \text{undefined at}\ \theta=\dfrac{\pi}{2}+k\pi \qquad \cot\theta,\ \csc\theta\ \text{undefined at}\ \theta=k\pi\)

(\(k\) any integer.) Notice \(\tan\) and \(\sec\) share a denominator — both are built from \(\cos\theta\) — so they share the very same asymptotes, even though \(\sec\) never involves sine at all. The period tells a similarly split story. Sine, cosine, secant, and cosecant repeat every \(2\pi\); but \(\tan(\theta+\pi)=\tan\theta\) for every \(\theta\) — tangent (and cotangent alongside it) completes its entire pattern in half the distance, because flipping the sign of both coordinates at once (which is exactly what adding \(\pi\) does) leaves their ratio unchanged. That is why the period formula for tangent and cotangent is \(\dfrac{\pi}{|B|}\), not \(\dfrac{2\pi}{|B|}\).

Example 1

Graph \(y=\tan x\) on \(\left(-\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)\) and state its period and asymptotes.

Solution:

\(\tan\left(-\dfrac{\pi}{4}\right)=-1 \qquad \tan(0)=0 \qquad \tan\left(\dfrac{\pi}{4}\right)=1\)

As \(x\) approaches \(\frac{\pi}{2}\) from the left, \(\cos x\) approaches \(0\) through positive values while \(\sin x\) approaches \(1\), so \(\tan x=\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\) grows without bound. By symmetry, \(\tan x\) plunges toward \(-\infty\) as \(x\) approaches \(-\frac{\pi}{2}\) from the right.

Answer: The graph rises smoothly and steeply through \((-\frac{\pi}{4},-1)\), \((0,0)\), and \((\frac{\pi}{4},1)\), with vertical asymptotes at \(x=\pm\frac{\pi}{2}\); period \(=\pi\); this same shape repeats every \(\pi\) units forever, at \(x=\frac{\pi}{2}+k\pi\).

Example 2

Graph \(y=\cot x\) on \((0,\pi)\) and state its period and asymptotes.

Solution:

\(\cot\dfrac{\pi}{6}=\dfrac{\sqrt3/2}{1/2}=\sqrt3 \qquad \cot\dfrac{\pi}{4}=1 \qquad \cot\dfrac{\pi}{3}=\dfrac{1/2}{\sqrt3/2}=\dfrac{\sqrt3}{3} \qquad \cot\dfrac{\pi}{2}=\dfrac{0}{1}=0\)

Answer: Unlike tangent, cotangent decreases across its interval — falling from \(+\infty\) just right of \(x=0\) (where \(\sin x\) is small and positive) down through \(\sqrt3\), \(1\), \(\frac{\sqrt3}{3}\), and \(0\) at \(x=\frac{\pi}{2}\), continuing down to \(-\infty\) as \(x\) approaches \(\pi\). Vertical asymptotes at \(x=0\) and \(x=\pi\); period \(=\pi\); asymptotes recur at every integer multiple of \(\pi\).

Example 3

Graph \(y=\sec x\) using its reciprocal relationship to \(\cos x\), and state its period, asymptotes, and range.

Solution:

\(\sec 0=\dfrac{1}{1}=1 \qquad \sec\dfrac{\pi}{3}=\dfrac{1}{1/2}=2 \qquad \sec\dfrac{\pi}{2}\ \text{undefined} \qquad \sec\dfrac{2\pi}{3}=\dfrac{1}{-1/2}=-2 \qquad \sec\pi=\dfrac{1}{-1}=-1\)

Answer: Wherever \(\cos x\) is near its own maximum of \(1\), \(\sec x\) is near its own minimum of \(1\); wherever \(\cos x\) shrinks toward \(0\), \(\sec x\) grows without bound — the two functions move oppositely because they are reciprocals. The graph forms a series of U-shaped arcs opening upward wherever \(\cos x>0\) and downward wherever \(\cos x<0\), never entering the strip \(-1

Example 5

State the period and every vertical asymptote of \(y=3\cot(2x)\).

Solution:

Cotangent's period formula uses \(\pi\), not \(2\pi\):

Period \(=\dfrac{\pi}{|B|}=\dfrac{\pi}{2}\)

Asymptotes occur where the original cotangent's denominator, \(\sin(2x)\), equals zero:

\(2x=k\pi \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x=\dfrac{k\pi}{2}\)

Answer: Period \(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\); asymptotes at \(x=\dfrac{k\pi}{2}\) — spaced exactly one period apart, as they must be. Applying the sine/cosine formula \(\frac{2\pi}{|B|}\) here would have (incorrectly) predicted a period of \(\pi\), doubling the true value and misplacing every asymptote after the first.

⚠️ AI CHECKPOINT: THE BORROWED PERIOD FORMULA

A student asked an AI for the period of \(y=\tan(3x)\). It answered:

“Using period \(= \frac{2\pi}{B}\), the period of \(\tan(3x)\) is \(\frac{2\pi}{3}\).”

The AI applied the sine-and-cosine period formula to a tangent function — a reasonable-looking shortcut that is simply the wrong rule for this family. Tangent's fundamental period is \(\pi\), not \(2\pi\), because \(\tan(\theta+\pi)=\tan\theta\) already holds without waiting for a full \(2\pi\). The correct formula is period \(=\frac{\pi}{|B|}\), giving \(\frac{\pi}{3}\) — exactly half of the AI's answer.

Notice the shape of the error: it is not an arithmetic slip, but a rule imported from the wrong family of functions. Before trusting a period — yours or a machine's — ask which family the function actually belongs to: sine, cosine, secant, and cosecant all use \(\frac{2\pi}{|B|}\); tangent and cotangent, alone among the six, use \(\frac{\pi}{|B|}\).

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Applying \(\dfrac{2\pi}{|B|}\) to tangent or cotangent out of habit, since every other trigonometric period formula this course has used so far has taken that exact form. Always check which of the six functions is on the page before reaching for a period formula: tangent and cotangent alone use \(\dfrac{\pi}{|B|}\), a fact that follows directly from \(\tan(\theta+\pi)=\tan\theta\) and has nothing to do with sine or cosine's own \(2\pi\) period.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Georg Joachim Rheticus, the sixteenth-century mathematician best known as the only student to visit Copernicus and help bring his heliocentric theory into print, spent much of his later career on a different, quieter project: constructing trigonometric tables of unprecedented precision, including extensive tables of tangent and secant values, published (after his death) as the Opus Palatinum de Triangulis. A function whose graph includes genuine breaks turned out to need the most patient hand-computation of the era to tame — centuries before anyone could type tan( into a calculator and receive an instant, disciplined answer.

Conclusion

All six trigonometric functions now have graphs: two that never break, defined for every real number, and four that break in exactly the places their own denominator hits zero — a discipline that includes knowing its own limits, not merely a defect. Every asymptote you placed today traces back to a single fact about sine and cosine's zeros; nothing about tangent, cotangent, secant, or cosecant needed inventing from scratch. This closes the graphing arc this unit opened: from the unit circle, to the plain sine and cosine wave, to a fully parameterized wave with its own reach, rate, and starting point, to the four reciprocal functions those two waves generate. What remains for this unit is to put these graphs to work — modeling real periodic phenomena, and uncovering the identities that let all six functions speak to one another in a single equation.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why do tangent, cotangent, secant, and cosecant have places where they are undefined, while sine and cosine never do?

2. Why do tangent and secant share the exact same set of vertical asymptotes, even though secant is built only from cosine and never involves sine at all?

3. Why is the period of tangent and cotangent \(\pi\) rather than \(2\pi\), and how does the identity \(\tan(\theta+\pi)=\tan\theta\) prove it?

4. In what sense do these four functions still practice self-government, even though each one includes places it refuses to answer?
