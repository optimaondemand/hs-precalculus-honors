Amplitude, Period, and Midline
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to graph \(y = A\sin(Bx) + D\) by identifying its amplitude, period, and midline from the parameters \(A\), \(B\), and \(D\).

  • I will be able to explain what each parameter physically does to the parent wave — \(A\) a vertical stretch (and possible reflection), \(B\) a horizontal stretch or compression, and \(D\) a vertical shift.

  • I will be able to determine the equation of a transformed sine or cosine function directly from its graph or from a verbal description of its maximum, minimum, and period.

📚 VOCABULARY

Parameter: a constant in a function's equation that controls one specific feature of its graph — in \(y = A\sin(Bx) + D\), the numbers \(A\), \(B\), and \(D\) are parameters.

Vertical stretch / compression: multiplying every output of a function by a constant \(A\), scaling the graph's height (and, if \(A\) is negative, flipping it across the midline) without touching its period.

Horizontal stretch / compression: multiplying the input \(x\) by a constant \(B\) before applying the function, scaling how quickly the input completes one cycle — a larger \(|B|\) speeds the wave up (shorter period), a smaller \(|B|\) slows it down (longer period).

Vertical shift: adding a constant \(D\) to every output of a function, sliding the entire graph up or down and relocating its midline to \(y=D\).

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Yesterday's wave was disciplined but plain: reaching only \(1\) unit above and below its midline of \(y=0\), completing its cycle only every \(2\pi\) units, resting only on the \(x\)-axis. No real Ferris wheel, no real tide, and no real vibrating string is built to those exact specifications. A wheel might have a \(20\)-meter radius and a hub \(22\) meters off the ground; a tuning fork might complete a vibration in a tiny fraction of a second, not in \(2\pi\) seconds. What stays fixed, no matter the wheel or the fork, is the wave's self-government — it will keep whatever rate you set for it, without fail, forever. Today's three parameters let you set that rate to match something real, while the wave itself keeps its own law exactly as reliably as before.

Three Parameters, Three Separate Jobs

For \(y = A\sin(Bx) + D\) (and identically for cosine in place of sine):

Amplitude \(= |A| \qquad\) Period \(= \dfrac{2\pi}{|B|} \qquad\) Midline: \(y = D\)

Each parameter answers one question and only one. \(A\) answers how far the wave reaches above and below its resting line — and if \(A\) is negative, it also answers whether the wave is flipped, opening downward where the parent wave opens upward. \(B\) answers how often the wave repeats: a larger \(|B|\) crowds more cycles into the same stretch of the \(x\)-axis (compression), a smaller \(|B|\) stretches one cycle out over more of it. \(D\) answers where the wave rests vertically, sliding the entire graph — peaks, troughs, and midline alike — up or down by \(D\) units. None of the three can answer another's question; changing \(D\) never changes how far the wave reaches, and changing \(A\) never changes how often it repeats.

Example 1

State the amplitude, period, and midline of \(y = 3\sin(2x)\), then build a table of values across one full period.

Solution:

Amplitude \(=|3|=3\). Period \(=\dfrac{2\pi}{2}=\pi\). Midline: \(y=0\) (no \(D\) term). One period runs from \(x=0\) to \(x=\pi\):

\(x=0:\ 3\sin(0)=0 \qquad x=\tfrac{\pi}{4}:\ 3\sin\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)=3(1)=3 \qquad x=\tfrac{\pi}{2}:\ 3\sin(\pi)=0\)

\(x=\tfrac{3\pi}{4}:\ 3\sin\left(\tfrac{3\pi}{2}\right)=3(-1)=-3 \qquad x=\pi:\ 3\sin(2\pi)=0\)

Answer: Amplitude \(3\), period \(\pi\), midline \(y=0\). The wave rises to \(3\), falls to \(-3\), and completes the whole trip in only \(\pi\) units — half the parent wave's period, because doubling \(B\) doubles how fast the input "uses up" one lap of the circle.

Example 2

State the amplitude, period, and midline of \(y = -2\cos\left(\dfrac{x}{2}\right) + 1\), and find its range.

Solution:

Amplitude \(=|-2|=2\). Period \(=\dfrac{2\pi}{1/2}=4\pi\). Midline: \(y=1\). Because \(A=-2\) is negative, the graph is reflected: it starts at a minimum where the parent cosine would start at a maximum.

\(x=0:\ -2\cos(0)+1=-2(1)+1=-1\ \text{(minimum)} \qquad x=2\pi:\ -2\cos(\pi)+1=-2(-1)+1=3\ \text{(maximum)}\)

Answer: Range \([-1,3]\), matching midline \(\pm\) amplitude: \(1\pm2\). The graph completes one full, reflected cycle over the unusually long stretch of \(4\pi\) units, because dividing the input by \(2\) stretches the wave horizontally instead of compressing it.

Example 3

A sinusoidal graph has a maximum of \(7\), a minimum of \(-1\), and a period of \(\dfrac{\pi}{3}\). Assuming it is a sine function with no phase shift, write its equation.

Solution:

Midline is the average of the max and min: \(D = \dfrac{7+(-1)}{2} = 3\). Amplitude is half the distance between them: \(A = \dfrac{7-(-1)}{2} = 4\). Solve the period formula for \(B\):

\(\dfrac{2\pi}{B} = \dfrac{\pi}{3} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; B = 2\pi \cdot \dfrac{3}{\pi} = 6\)

Answer: \(y = 4\sin(6x) + 3\). Check: this predicts a maximum of \(4(1)+3=7\) and a minimum of \(4(-1)+3=-1\) — both match the given graph.

Example 4

Concert pitch A vibrates at \(440\) cycles every second (\(440\) hertz). If a point on the tuning fork swings with amplitude \(0.02\) mm about its resting position, model its displacement \(y\) (in mm) as a function of time \(t\) (in seconds).

Solution:

Completing \(440\) cycles per second means one cycle takes \(\dfrac{1}{440}\) second — that is the period. Amplitude is given directly as \(0.02\) mm, and the fork rests at \(y=0\), so \(D=0\).

\(\dfrac{2\pi}{B} = \dfrac{1}{440} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; B = 2\pi(440) = 880\pi\)

Answer: \(y = 0.02\sin(880\pi t)\). The parameter \(B\) is doing exactly the job Fourier's insight depends on: a musical pitch is a period, and \(880\pi\) is what it costs, in this equation, to vibrate four hundred forty times every single second without ever missing a beat.

💻 On Your Calculator: Sliders for A, B, and D

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), type y = A sin(Bx) + D. Desmos will offer to create sliders for \(A\), \(B\), and \(D\) automatically — accept all three. Drag each slider one at a time, watching which feature of the graph moves: \(A\) changes only the height of the peaks and troughs; \(B\) changes only how tightly the waves are packed; \(D\) changes only how high the whole wave sits. Set \(A=4\), \(B=6\), \(D=3\) and confirm the graph matches Example 3's predicted maximum of \(7\) and minimum of \(-1\) exactly.

▶  Try it yourself: State the amplitude, period, and midline of \(y = 6\cos(4x) - 5\).
Amplitude \(=|6|=6\); period \(=\frac{2\pi}{4}=\frac{\pi}{2}\); midline \(y=-5\).
▶  Try it yourself: A sinusoidal graph has maximum \(4\), minimum \(0\), and period \(4\pi\). Assuming a sine function with no phase shift, write its equation.
\(D = \frac{4+0}{2}=2\); \(A=\frac{4-0}{2}=2\); \(\frac{2\pi}{B}=4\pi \Rightarrow B=\frac{1}{2}\). Equation: \(y = 2\sin\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)+2\).
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Writing "amplitude \(= A\)" instead of "amplitude \(= |A|\)," and forgetting that a negative \(A\) still reports a positive amplitude — the size of the reach can never be negative, even when the wave is flipped. A second common slip: applying the period formula backward, treating a larger \(B\) as producing a longer period. Because \(B\) sits in the denominator of \(\frac{2\pi}{B}\), a larger \(|B|\) always produces a shorter period (the wave repeats faster), and a \(B\) between \(0\) and \(1\) always produces a longer one.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The unit "hertz" — one cycle per second — honors Heinrich Hertz, who in 1887 was the first to conclusively generate and detect radio waves in a laboratory, confirming James Clerk Maxwell's prediction that light itself is an electromagnetic wave. Frequency and period are simply reciprocals of each other, \(f = \frac{1}{\text{period}}\), which is exactly why Example 4's \(440\) hertz tuning fork produced a period of \(\frac{1}{440}\) second — the same relationship silently sitting inside every radio station's dial number.

Conclusion

Three parameters, three separate jobs: \(A\) for reach, \(B\) for rate, \(D\) for resting height. You can now build a sine or cosine wave to match a wheel, a tide table, or a tuning fork's exact pitch — and in the Wave Machine, you watched a physical crank obey those same three parameters without being told to. One control is still missing, and it is the one every one of today's graphs quietly assumed away: every wave you built today started its cycle exactly at \(x=0\), the way the crank always started its swing from the same resting position. Real waves rarely cooperate that neatly — a tide is high at 6 a.m., not at midnight. Tomorrow's lesson gives the wave permission to start its cycle wherever the real world actually starts it.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why do \(A\), \(B\), and \(D\) each answer a completely different question about a sinusoidal graph, such that changing one never affects what another controls?

2. Why does amplitude have to be written as \(|A|\) rather than \(A\), and what does a negative \(A\) actually do to the graph if not change how far it reaches?

3. Why does a larger value of \(B\) produce a shorter period rather than a longer one?

4. In the Wave Machine, which of the three settings you tested changed the wave's shape, and which only changed where it sat — and how does that distinction map onto \(A\), \(B\), and \(D\)?
