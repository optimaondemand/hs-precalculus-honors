📋 THE SCENARIO

In 1822, Joseph Fourier made an audacious claim: almost any periodic phenomenon — no matter how jagged or lopsided it looks — can be built from nothing but sums of sines and cosines. Lesson 7-7 gave you the simplest possible test case: a single sine wave, fit to a single real rhythm. Daylight is one of the cleanest examples nature offers. Every point on Earth's surface is governed by the very same orbital law — the same 365-day trip around the sun, the same 23.5° tilt of the axis — and yet no two latitudes experience that law identically. That is this unit's virtue in miniature: self-government is not chaos held in check by force; it is disciplined, periodic order that governs itself, the same law expressed differently depending on where you stand to observe it.

Your task: pull a real year of day-length data for two cities at genuinely different latitudes, build a sinusoidal model for each by hand — the honors move, since Excel has no built-in sinusoidal trendline — and then explain, in the language of amplitude, midline, and phase shift, exactly what the clockwork sky is doing differently at each location.

🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
Two cities, one orbit. What in your two sinusoidal models has to come out nearly identical because the law governing both is the same — and what has to come out different, and why, because of where each city stands on the globe?
📝 THE TASK

Part 1 — Get the data on record. Choose two cities: City A, a natural default being your own home city, and City B, a city at a genuinely different latitude — one much closer to the equator than City A, or much closer to a pole (for example, a city near the equator like Quito versus a city near a pole like Tromsø illustrates the kind of contrast you are after; these are only examples of the range of latitudes to consider, not data sources). For each city, find a real, public source for a year of daylight data — a national weather service, a government astronomical almanac, or a reputable sunrise–sunset reference site — and record at least 12 data points per city (day length in hours, or sunrise and sunset time converted to day length, on the 1st of each month is the natural sampling; a comparable regular sampling is fine too). On a sheet named Data, enter day-of-year in one column and day length in hours in the next, for both cities, and cite your source for each city in a labeled cell at the top. Real data only: no invented numbers, no smoothing away a wobble your source actually reports.

Part 2 — Fit sinusoidal models, twice. On a sheet named Model, make a scatter chart of each city's day length against day-of-year. Excel has no built-in sinusoidal trendline, so you will build this model the honors way — by hand, straight from your own data's key features, exactly as you did in Lesson 7-7:

  • Amplitude \(A\) = half the swing between your longest and shortest day: \(A = \dfrac{\text{max} - \text{min}}{2}\).
  • Midline \(D\) = the average of the longest and shortest day: \(D = \dfrac{\text{max} + \text{min}}{2}\).
  • Period: a full cycle of seasons takes one year, so \(B = \dfrac{2\pi}{365}\) (radians per day) — this should come out essentially the same for both cities, since both orbit the same sun on the same schedule.
  • Phase shift \(C\) = the day-of-year on which each city reaches its maximum day length (read straight off your data, or off the equinox crossing if you prefer to anchor there instead).

Write each city's model as \(y = A\cos\big(B(x - C)\big) + D\), where \(x\) is day-of-year and \(y\) is day length in hours. Put \(A\), \(D\), \(B\), and \(C\) in labeled cells for each city, then add a model column that computes \(y\) for every day-of-year with one formula copied down — for example, if your amplitude, midline, and phase-shift cells for City A are named or referenced as $F$1, $F$2, and $F$3, the model column reads =$F$2 + $F$1*COS((2*PI()/365)*(A2-$F$3)) (note that \(B\) is already in radians per day, so no separate degrees-to-radians conversion is needed here). Plot that smooth model curve on the same chart as your raw data points, for both cities, side by side, so you can see with your own eyes how closely the hand-built curve tracks the real record.

Part 3 — Interpret the parameters physically. On a sheet named Answers, in full sentences citing your own cells, state each city's amplitude, midline, and phase shift. Then explain, physically:

  • Why the higher-latitude city has the larger amplitude — a bigger swing between its longest summer day and shortest winter day — while the lower-latitude city has the smaller amplitude, its day length staying much closer to 12 hours all year. (The closer a location sits to the equator, the more evenly the axial tilt divides its daylight across the seasons; the closer to a pole, the more extreme that division becomes.)
  • Why both cities' midlines should land very close to 12 hours regardless of latitude — averaged across a full year, every point on Earth receives almost exactly half a day of sunlight and half a night, tilt notwithstanding.
  • Why both cities should have very nearly the same period (365 days) — the same orbit, the same clock, for everyone on the planet.
  • Why the two curves' peaks and troughs should land on nearly the same day of year — both hemispheres' seasons are driven by the same orbital mechanics. Exception: if you chose a Southern Hemisphere city, note that its peak day length falls roughly six months out of phase from a Northern Hemisphere city's peak (its summer is the other city's winter), and explain why that phase difference is exactly what the shared orbital law predicts, not a contradiction of it.

Part 4 — The AI round. On the Answers sheet, write a short paragraph (4–6 sentences) defending why your model's period and phase-shift choices are physically reasonable — not just numerically fitted, but justified by what you know about Earth's orbit and axial tilt. Paste that paragraph to your AI tutor and ask it to critique your argument. Then write the final entry: a critique of the critique — what the AI got right, what it missed or confidently invented, and your final verdict, signed with your name. The same supervision discipline you have practiced all unit — catching an AI that skips a conversion step or assumes what it must prove — applies here to your own physical reasoning and to your critic's.

Honors stretch (optional): The amplitude of a location's day-length curve grows with distance from the equator — roughly zero amplitude at the equator, growing toward its largest values near the poles. Using only your City A amplitude, and whatever relationship between latitude and day-length amplitude you can find or reason toward (ask your AI tutor to help you locate or derive an approximate relationship, and show your work), estimate City A's latitude. Compare your estimate to the city's actual latitude (easy to look up) and discuss, in two or three sentences, why they likely do not match exactly — real day-length curves are not perfectly sinusoidal near the poles, atmospheric refraction bends light enough to extend apparent day length slightly everywhere, and any approximate formula trades some accuracy for simplicity.
