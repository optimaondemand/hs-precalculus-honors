Simplifying and Verifying Identities
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to verify a trigonometric identity by transforming one side alone — never both sides at once — until it matches the other side exactly.

  • I will be able to simplify a trigonometric expression to its simplest equivalent form using the reciprocal, quotient, and Pythagorean identities.

  • I will be able to name the fallacy of assuming the conclusion (begging the question) when a "proof" manipulates both sides of an unproven equation at once — whether the proof is mine, a classmate's, or an AI's.

  • I will be able to choose a productive first move — converting to sine and cosine, multiplying by a conjugate form of \(1\), or splitting a fraction — when an identity resists the obvious approach.

📚 VOCABULARY

Identity: An equation between two trigonometric expressions that is true for every value of the variable where both sides are defined — not just for a few special values, the way an ordinary equation might be.

Verifying an identity: Demonstrating that an identity holds by transforming one side alone, using known identities and legal algebra, until it matches the other side exactly — never operating on both sides at once.

Simplifying a trigonometric expression: Rewriting an expression as an equivalent one with fewer terms or a single named function, usually by converting everything to sine and cosine first.

Begging the question (circular reasoning): A logical fallacy in which an argument's conclusion is smuggled into its own premises — so the "proof" never actually establishes anything the reader did not already have to grant at the start.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Lesson 7-12 proved that \(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1\) and its two companions hold for every angle, not just the special ones — and it proved them properly, from the unit circle outward, never assuming the very fact being established. Today that discipline becomes a general habit. An identity is a promise that two expressions describe the same quantity everywhere both are defined, and honoring that promise takes a specific kind of self-restraint: you may transform one side of the page as much as you like, but the moment you touch both sides of an unproven equation in the same step, you have quietly assumed the two sides were already equal — which is the one thing a proof is not allowed to assume. This is self-government in its most literal classroom form: not "don't cheat," but "don't let the shortcut that would make this easy be the one move that secretly begs the question."

The One-Side Rule

To verify an identity, pick the side that looks more complicated (usually) and rewrite it — using reciprocal, quotient, and Pythagorean identities, and ordinary algebra (factoring, combining fractions, multiplying by a clever form of \(1\)) — until it becomes, exactly, the other side. The other side is never touched. To simplify an expression with no claimed equal side at all, the same toolbox applies: convert everything to sine and cosine first, then look for cancellation.

Why the restriction matters: an equation (like \(2x+1=7\)) is an open question — you may operate on both sides to find which \(x\) makes it true. An identity is a closed claim, already asserted to be true for every allowed input. Treating it like an equation — cross-multiplying, squaring both sides, adding the same thing to both sides — only shows that if the identity holds, some other true statement follows. It never shows the identity itself holds. That gap is exactly where this lesson's checkpoint lives.

Example 1

Simplify \(\sin\theta \cot\theta\).

Solution:

Convert to sine and cosine first — the reliable opening move. Since \(\cot\theta = \dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\):

\(\sin\theta\cot\theta = \sin\theta\cdot\dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta} = \cos\theta\)

Answer: \(\cos\theta\). The \(\sin\theta\) factors cancel — legal because we are transforming a single expression, not an equation, so nothing about "both sides" applies here at all.

Example 2

Verify: \(\sec x - \cos x = \sin x\tan x\).

Solution:

The left side has more structure, so transform it alone. Convert to cosine, then combine over a common denominator:

\(\sec x - \cos x = \dfrac{1}{\cos x} - \cos x = \dfrac{1-\cos^2 x}{\cos x}\)

By the Pythagorean identity, \(1-\cos^2x = \sin^2x\):

\(=\dfrac{\sin^2 x}{\cos x} = \sin x\cdot\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x} = \sin x\tan x\)

Answer: The left side now reads exactly as the right side, \(\sin x\tan x\) — verified. The right side was never touched.

Example 3

Verify: \(\dfrac{1+\cos\theta}{\sin\theta} = \dfrac{\sin\theta}{1-\cos\theta}\).

Solution:

Transform the left side only, by multiplying it by a conjugate form of \(1\) — a legal move because multiplying anything by \(\dfrac{1-\cos\theta}{1-\cos\theta}\) does not change its value:

\(\dfrac{1+\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\cdot\dfrac{1-\cos\theta}{1-\cos\theta} = \dfrac{(1+\cos\theta)(1-\cos\theta)}{\sin\theta(1-\cos\theta)} = \dfrac{1-\cos^2\theta}{\sin\theta(1-\cos\theta)}\)

By the Pythagorean identity, \(1-\cos^2\theta=\sin^2\theta\), so the fraction becomes:

\(\dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{\sin\theta(1-\cos\theta)} = \dfrac{\sin\theta}{1-\cos\theta}\)

Answer: The left side now matches the right side exactly — verified, with the right side never once touched. Numerically, at \(\theta=60^\circ\): left side \(=\dfrac{1+0.5}{0.8660}\approx1.7321\); right side \(=\dfrac{0.8660}{1-0.5}\approx1.7321\). They agree — and notice this identity is exactly the one the checkpoint below borrows.

⚠️ AI CHECKPOINT: THE PROOF THAT ASSUMES ITSELF

A student asked an AI to verify Example 3's identity, \(\dfrac{1+\cos\theta}{\sin\theta} = \dfrac{\sin\theta}{1-\cos\theta}\). It answered:

“Cross-multiply both sides: \((1+\cos\theta)(1-\cos\theta) = \sin\theta\cdot\sin\theta\). Expand: \(1-\cos^2\theta = \sin^2\theta\). This is true by the Pythagorean identity, so the original identity is verified.”

Every line of arithmetic is correct — and the conclusion happens to be true — but the method is invalid, and it is invalid in a way arithmetic checking can never catch. Cross-multiplying an equation is only a legal move when you already know both sides are equal (or you are solving for an unknown and will check the solution afterward); here, "both sides are equal" is the very claim on trial. The AI operated on both sides simultaneously, which quietly assumes what it needed to prove — the fallacy of begging the question (assuming the conclusion). Reaching a true statement (\(1-\cos^2\theta=\sin^2\theta\)) at the end does not rescue the argument: if the identity holds, that true statement must follow — but plenty of false identities would also lead to a true statement after cross-multiplying, so arriving at one proves nothing about where you started.

The disciplined fix is Example 3 above: transform the left side alone, using a legal move (multiplying by a conjugate form of \(1\), which changes nothing about the left side's value), until it becomes the right side — which is never touched. Same true identity, same true Pythagorean fact buried inside it — but only one of the two arguments actually earns the conclusion. Before accepting any identity "proof" — yours, a classmate's, or an AI's — ask exactly one question: did this argument ever perform the same operation on both sides of the thing it was trying to prove?

Example 4

Simplify \(\tan x + \cot x\) to a single named function or product.

Solution:

\(\tan x+\cot x = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}+\dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x} = \dfrac{\sin^2x+\cos^2x}{\sin x\cos x} = \dfrac{1}{\sin x\cos x}\)

Answer: \(\dfrac{1}{\sin x\cos x}\), which can also be written \(\csc x\sec x\) since \(\dfrac{1}{\sin x}\cdot\dfrac{1}{\cos x}=\csc x\sec x\). Notice the Pythagorean identity appeared again, uninvited, the moment everything was rewritten in sine and cosine — that identity is the single most useful tool in this entire lesson.

Example 5

Verify: \(\dfrac{\cos x}{1+\sin x} = \dfrac{1-\sin x}{\cos x}\).

Solution:

Transform the left side by multiplying by the conjugate form of \(1\), \(\dfrac{1-\sin x}{1-\sin x}\):

\(\dfrac{\cos x}{1+\sin x}\cdot\dfrac{1-\sin x}{1-\sin x} = \dfrac{\cos x(1-\sin x)}{1-\sin^2x} = \dfrac{\cos x(1-\sin x)}{\cos^2x} = \dfrac{1-\sin x}{\cos x}\)

Answer: The left side now reads exactly as the right side — verified. Check at \(x=30^\circ\): left side \(=\dfrac{0.8660}{1.5}\approx0.5774\); right side \(=\dfrac{0.5}{0.8660}\approx0.5774\). Matches.

▶  Try it yourself: Verify \(\tan\theta\cos\theta = \sin\theta\).
\(\tan\theta\cos\theta = \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}\cdot\cos\theta = \sin\theta\). The right side, \(\sin\theta\), was never touched.
▶  Try it yourself: Simplify \(\dfrac{1-\sin^2x}{\cos x}\) (assume \(\cos x\ne0\)).
By the Pythagorean identity, \(1-\sin^2x=\cos^2x\), so \(\dfrac{1-\sin^2x}{\cos x}=\dfrac{\cos^2x}{\cos x}=\cos x\).

👀 What "Never Touch the Other Side" Actually Buys You

If both sides could be touched, any claimed identity could be pushed toward "true" — cross-multiply, expand, and you will often land on some real Pythagorean fact, whether or not the original claim was ever correct. Restricting yourself to one side is not a bureaucratic rule; it is the only thing standing between "I verified this identity" and "I performed some arithmetic that felt related to this identity." That distinction is the entire content of today's virtue.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Writing the identity you are trying to prove at the top of your page, then performing the same operation to both sides, line after line, until you reach something true — and calling that a proof. This is not only an AI's mistake; it is the single most common habit students bring from solving equations, where operating on both sides is exactly the right move. An identity is not an equation to be solved; it is a claim to be verified, and verifying it means moving only one side of the page at a time.

Conclusion

Every identity in this lesson traced back to the same short list from 7-12 — the reciprocal, quotient, and Pythagorean identities — applied with one rule enforced without exception: transform one side, leave the other alone, and never let the goal itself become a tool in the proof. That is what self-government looks like inside an algebra step, not just inside a life. Next lesson turns from a single angle's identities to two angles at once: the sum and difference formulas, which let you find the exact value of an angle like \(15^\circ\) that no unit-circle seed ever gave you directly — and which, as it happens, were first hidden inside a theorem about circles nearly two thousand years ago.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why is it legal to operate on both sides of an equation you are solving, but not legal to operate on both sides of an identity you are trying to verify?

2. In Example 3 and Example 5, what made multiplying by a "conjugate form of 1" a legitimate move, when cross-multiplying the same identity was not?

3. The AI's cross-multiplication reached a true statement. Why does reaching a true statement not prove the argument that led there was valid?

4. What does this lesson's one-side rule have to do with the unit's virtue of self-government?
