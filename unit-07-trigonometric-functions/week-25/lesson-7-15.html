Applying Sum and Difference; Cofunctions
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to apply the sum and difference formulas to simplify expressions, verify identities, and evaluate exact values.

  • I will be able to derive the complete family of cofunction identities from the sum and difference formulas, and state what each one means about complementary angles.

  • I will be able to recognize the pattern \(\sin A\cos B\pm\cos A\sin B\) inside an unfamiliar expression and collapse it back to a single sum or difference formula.

📚 VOCABULARY

Cofunction identity: An identity relating a trigonometric function of an angle to the "co-" function — sine with cosine, tangent with cotangent, secant with cosecant — of that angle's complement.

Complementary angles: Two angles whose measures sum to \(90^\circ\) (\(\frac{\pi}{2}\) radians).

Applying an identity: Using a proven identity as a working tool mid-computation — substituting a known equivalent expression to simplify, evaluate, or solve — as distinct from proving the identity itself, which is 7-13 and 7-14's job.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Lesson 7-14 proved four formulas and, almost as a side effect, one more: substituting \(\alpha=\frac{\pi}{2}\) into the cosine-difference formula produced \(\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta\right)=\sin\theta\) for free, and a second substitution produced \(\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta\right)=\cos\theta\) right alongside it. Today those two lines become a complete family, and the four sum and difference formulas themselves go to work — not being proved, but being applied, the way a carpenter who has just forged a set of tools finally gets to build something with them.

Building the Complete Cofunction Family

From 7-14's two established lines, \(\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta\right)=\sin\theta\) and \(\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta\right)=\cos\theta\), every other cofunction pairing follows by simply dividing or reciprocating:

\(\tan\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}-\theta\right)=\dfrac{\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta\right)}{\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta\right)}=\dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}=\cot\theta\)

\(\cot\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}-\theta\right)=\tan\theta \qquad \sec\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}-\theta\right)=\dfrac{1}{\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta\right)}=\dfrac{1}{\sin\theta}=\csc\theta \qquad \csc\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}-\theta\right)=\sec\theta\)

Every pairing says the same geometric thing: in a right triangle, the two acute angles are complementary, so the "side over hypotenuse" ratio that is sine from one angle's point of view is cosine from the other's. The sum-formula proof simply makes that true for every real \(\theta\), not just the acute angles a triangle can hold.

Example 1 — an exact value by a different decomposition

Find the exact value of \(\sin15^\circ\).

Solution:

\(\sin15^\circ=\sin(45^\circ-30^\circ)=\sin45^\circ\cos30^\circ-\cos45^\circ\sin30^\circ=\left(\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)\!\left(\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)-\left(\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)\!\left(\dfrac12\right)=\dfrac{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}{4}\)

Verify: \(\dfrac{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}{4}\approx\dfrac{2.4495-1.4142}{4}\approx0.2588\), matching a calculator's \(\sin15^\circ\approx0.2588\).

Answer: \(\sin15^\circ=\dfrac{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}{4}\). Also worth noticing: by the cofunction identity above, \(\sin15^\circ\) must equal \(\cos75^\circ\) — a second, independent way to check this value if you ever doubt it.

Example 2 — verifying by recognizing the pattern

Verify: \(\sin(\alpha+\beta)+\sin(\alpha-\beta) = 2\sin\alpha\cos\beta\).

Solution:

Transform the left side alone, expanding both terms with the sum and difference formulas:

\(\sin(\alpha+\beta)+\sin(\alpha-\beta) = (\sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta)+(\sin\alpha\cos\beta-\cos\alpha\sin\beta)\)

The \(\cos\alpha\sin\beta\) terms cancel, leaving:

\(= 2\sin\alpha\cos\beta\)

Answer: Verified — the right side was never touched. Check numerically at \(\alpha=50^\circ,\beta=20^\circ\): left side \(=\sin70^\circ+\sin30^\circ\approx0.9397+0.5=1.4397\); right side \(=2\sin50^\circ\cos20^\circ\approx2(0.7660)(0.9397)\approx1.4397\). Matches.

Example 3 — two paths, one answer

Simplify \(\sin\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}-x\right)\cos x+\cos\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}-x\right)\sin x\).

Solution (path A — recognize the pattern): the expression has exactly the shape \(\sin A\cos B+\cos A\sin B=\sin(A+B)\) with \(A=\frac{\pi}{2}-x\) and \(B=x\):

\(=\sin\!\left(\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}-x\right)+x\right)=\sin\dfrac{\pi}{2}=1\)

Solution (path B — substitute the cofunction identities first): since \(\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-x\right)=\cos x\) and \(\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-x\right)=\sin x\):

\(=\cos x\cdot\cos x+\sin x\cdot\sin x = \cos^2x+\sin^2x = 1\)

Answer: \(1\), by two independent routes — pattern-recognition and direct substitution — which is exactly the kind of cross-check a disciplined proof invites rather than avoids.

Example 4

Verify: \(\cos(\alpha+\beta)\cos(\alpha-\beta)=\cos^2\alpha-\sin^2\beta\).

Solution:

Transform the left side alone. Expand both factors, and multiply as a difference of squares:

\(\cos(\alpha+\beta)\cos(\alpha-\beta) = (\cos\alpha\cos\beta-\sin\alpha\sin\beta)(\cos\alpha\cos\beta+\sin\alpha\sin\beta) = \cos^2\alpha\cos^2\beta-\sin^2\alpha\sin^2\beta\)

Replace \(\cos^2\beta=1-\sin^2\beta\) and \(\sin^2\alpha=1-\cos^2\alpha\) (Pythagorean identity), then expand and cancel:

\(=\cos^2\alpha(1-\sin^2\beta)-(1-\cos^2\alpha)\sin^2\beta = \cos^2\alpha-\cos^2\alpha\sin^2\beta-\sin^2\beta+\cos^2\alpha\sin^2\beta = \cos^2\alpha-\sin^2\beta\)

Answer: Verified — the two \(\cos^2\alpha\sin^2\beta\) terms cancel exactly, leaving the right side untouched throughout.

▶  Try it yourself: Find the exact value of \(\tan15^\circ\) using \(15^\circ=45^\circ-30^\circ\), and verify it numerically.
\(\tan15^\circ=\dfrac{\tan45^\circ-\tan30^\circ}{1+\tan45^\circ\tan30^\circ}=\dfrac{1-\frac{1}{\sqrt3}}{1+\frac{1}{\sqrt3}}=\dfrac{\sqrt3-1}{\sqrt3+1}=2-\sqrt3\approx0.2679\), matching a calculator's \(\tan15^\circ\approx0.2679\).
▶  Try it yourself: Simplify \(\dfrac{\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-x\right)}{\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-x\right)}\) using the cofunction identities.
\(\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-x\right)=\cos x\) and \(\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-x\right)=\sin x\), so the ratio is \(\dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}=\cot x\) — matching the general cofunction rule \(\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-x\right)=\cot x\) directly.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Assuming a cofunction identity introduces a sign flip, writing \(\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta\right)=-\cos\theta\). Check at \(\theta=30^\circ\): \(\sin60^\circ\approx0.8660\), and \(\cos30^\circ\approx0.8660\) — equal, with no sign change at all. A second common slip is mixing up which function pairs with which; the rule is always sine–cosine, tangent–cotangent, secant–cosecant — never sine–tangent or any other cross-pairing.

Conclusion

The sum and difference formulas turned out to do double duty today: finding exact values for angles the unit circle never handed us directly, and — almost as a bonus — completing an entire family of cofunction identities that a right triangle's two acute angles had only hinted at. One family of formulas, applied four different ways, and no new proof required. Lesson 7-16 takes the same four formulas one step further: instead of two different angles \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), what happens when you let them be the same angle?

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why do the two solution paths in Example 3 — pattern recognition and direct substitution — have to agree, and what would it mean if they didn't?

2. How does the cofunction identity \(\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta\right)=\cos\theta\) generalize a fact you already knew about complementary angles in a right triangle?

3. In Example 4, why is the difference-of-squares factoring the key move that makes the rest of the simplification possible?

4. What is the difference between applying an identity (today's lesson) and verifying one (7-13)—and why does today's lesson still have to obey the one-side rule in Examples 2 and 4?
