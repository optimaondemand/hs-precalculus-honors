Double-Angle Formulas
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to prove the double-angle formulas for sine, cosine, and tangent by setting both angles of the sum formulas equal, without introducing any new geometric argument.

  • I will be able to choose among the three equivalent forms of \(\cos2\theta\) based on which single function's value is known.

  • I will be able to apply the double-angle formulas to find exact function values and to solve trigonometric equations involving \(2\theta\).

📚 VOCABULARY

Double-angle formula: An identity expressing \(\sin(2\theta)\), \(\cos(2\theta)\), or \(\tan(2\theta)\) in terms of trigonometric functions of \(\theta\) alone — the special case of the sum formulas obtained by setting both angles equal.

Triple-angle formula: An identity for \(\sin(3\theta)\) or \(\cos(3\theta)\) obtained by iterating the sum and double-angle formulas — an honors extension of the same doubling idea.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Every identity so far this week has come from a proof that refused to take a shortcut: 7-13 refused to touch both sides of an unproven claim, 7-14 refused to assume anything beyond the distance formula, and 7-15 refused to assume a cofunction relationship that hadn't actually been earned. Today's formulas ask for no new refusal at all — they are the sum formulas, entirely unchanged, with one small and completely legal substitution: let \(\beta=\alpha\). Self-government, it turns out, sometimes means recognizing when the disciplined thing to do is simply reuse what you already proved, rather than manufacturing a new argument where none is needed.

Setting β = α in the Sum Formulas

Starting from \(\sin(\alpha+\beta)=\sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta\), substitute \(\beta=\alpha\):

\(\sin(2\alpha) = \sin\alpha\cos\alpha+\cos\alpha\sin\alpha = 2\sin\alpha\cos\alpha\)

Starting from \(\cos(\alpha+\beta)=\cos\alpha\cos\beta-\sin\alpha\sin\beta\), substitute \(\beta=\alpha\):

\(\cos(2\alpha) = \cos^2\alpha-\sin^2\alpha\)

Two more forms follow immediately by substituting the Pythagorean identity (\(\sin^2\alpha=1-\cos^2\alpha\) or \(\cos^2\alpha=1-\sin^2\alpha\)) into this one:

\(\cos(2\alpha) = \cos^2\alpha-(1-\cos^2\alpha) = 2\cos^2\alpha-1 \qquad\text{or}\qquad \cos(2\alpha)=(1-\sin^2\alpha)-\sin^2\alpha=1-2\sin^2\alpha\)

And from \(\tan(\alpha+\beta)=\dfrac{\tan\alpha+\tan\beta}{1-\tan\alpha\tan\beta}\), substituting \(\beta=\alpha\): \(\tan(2\alpha)=\dfrac{2\tan\alpha}{1-\tan^2\alpha}\). Nothing here required a new proof — every line is a corollary of a formula already established.

Example 1 — choosing the right form

Given \(\cos\theta=\dfrac{5}{13}\) with \(\theta\) in Quadrant IV, find \(\sin2\theta\), \(\cos2\theta\), and \(\tan2\theta\).

Solution:

In QIV, cosine is positive and sine is negative. From the \(5\text{-}12\text{-}13\) triangle, \(\sin\theta=-\dfrac{12}{13}\).

\(\sin2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta = 2\!\left(-\dfrac{12}{13}\right)\!\left(\dfrac{5}{13}\right) = -\dfrac{120}{169}\)

For \(\cos2\theta\), the form \(2\cos^2\theta-1\) is fastest since \(\cos\theta\) is already known:

\(\cos2\theta = 2\!\left(\dfrac{5}{13}\right)^{\!2}-1 = \dfrac{50}{169}-\dfrac{169}{169} = -\dfrac{119}{169}\)

Verify: the other two forms should agree: \(\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta=\dfrac{25}{169}-\dfrac{144}{169}=-\dfrac{119}{169}\) ✓; and \(1-2\sin^2\theta=1-\dfrac{288}{169}=-\dfrac{119}{169}\) ✓. All three forms of \(\cos2\theta\) agree, as they must.

\(\tan2\theta = \dfrac{\sin2\theta}{\cos2\theta} = \dfrac{-120/169}{-119/169} = \dfrac{120}{119}\)

Answer: \(\sin2\theta=-\dfrac{120}{169}\), \(\cos2\theta=-\dfrac{119}{169}\), \(\tan2\theta=\dfrac{120}{119}\). Since \(2\theta\) lands in Quadrant III here (double a QIV angle between \(270^\circ\) and \(360^\circ\) can land back in QIII), both sine and cosine of \(2\theta\) being negative is exactly what ASTC predicts — a reasonableness check worth making every time.

Example 2 — a callback to last week's exact values

Use the double-angle formula for cosine, together with 7-14 and 7-15's exact values for \(15^\circ\), to find \(\cos30^\circ\) — and confirm it against the value you already know.

Solution:

\(\cos30^\circ = \cos(2\cdot15^\circ) = \cos^2 15^\circ - \sin^2 15^\circ\)

Using \(\cos15^\circ=\dfrac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}\approx0.9659\) and \(\sin15^\circ=\dfrac{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}{4}\approx0.2588\):

\(\cos^2 15^\circ - \sin^2 15^\circ \approx (0.9659)^2-(0.2588)^2 \approx 0.9330-0.0670 \approx 0.8660\)

Answer: \(0.8660\), which is exactly \(\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\) — the familiar exact value of \(\cos30^\circ\) from the unit circle, arrived at by an entirely different road. Two lessons' worth of formulas, checking each other.

Example 3 — solving an equation with 2θ

Solve \(\cos2\theta=\cos\theta\) on \([0,2\pi)\).

Solution:

To get one function of one angle, rewrite \(\cos2\theta\) using the form built from \(\cos\theta\) alone, \(2\cos^2\theta-1\):

\(2\cos^2\theta-1=\cos\theta \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; 2\cos^2\theta-\cos\theta-1=0\)

Let \(c=\cos\theta\) and factor the quadratic:

\(2c^2-c-1=0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; (2c+1)(c-1)=0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; c=-\dfrac12 \;\;\text{or}\;\; c=1\)

\(\cos\theta=1\) gives \(\theta=0\). \(\cos\theta=-\dfrac12\) gives \(\theta=\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\) and \(\theta=\dfrac{4\pi}{3}\).

Verify: at \(\theta=\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\): \(\cos\dfrac{4\pi}{3}=-\dfrac12\) and \(\cos\dfrac{2\pi}{3}=-\dfrac12\) ✓. At \(\theta=\dfrac{4\pi}{3}\): \(\cos\dfrac{8\pi}{3}=\cos\dfrac{2\pi}{3}=-\dfrac12\) and \(\cos\dfrac{4\pi}{3}=-\dfrac12\) ✓.

Answer: \(\theta \in \left\{0,\ \dfrac{2\pi}{3},\ \dfrac{4\pi}{3}\right\}\).

▶  Try it yourself: Given \(\sin\theta=\dfrac{3}{5}\) with \(\theta\) in Quadrant I, find \(\sin2\theta\) and \(\cos2\theta\).
\(\cos\theta=\dfrac45\) (from the \(3\text{-}4\text{-}5\) triangle, QI so positive). \(\sin2\theta=2\!\left(\frac35\right)\!\left(\frac45\right)=\dfrac{24}{25}\). \(\cos2\theta=1-2\!\left(\frac35\right)^{\!2}=1-\dfrac{18}{25}=\dfrac{7}{25}\).
▶  Try it yourself: Solve \(\sin2\theta=\sin\theta\) on \([0,2\pi)\).
\(2\sin\theta\cos\theta-\sin\theta=0 \Rightarrow \sin\theta(2\cos\theta-1)=0\). So \(\sin\theta=0\) (\(\theta=0,\pi\)) or \(\cos\theta=\frac12\) (\(\theta=\frac{\pi}{3},\frac{5\pi}{3}\)). Solution set: \(\left\{0,\frac{\pi}{3},\pi,\frac{5\pi}{3}\right\}\).

📈 Honors Extension: the Triple-Angle Formula

Nothing stops the same idea from going further. Write \(\sin3\theta=\sin(2\theta+\theta)\) and expand with the sum formula, then substitute the double-angle results:

\(\sin3\theta = \sin2\theta\cos\theta+\cos2\theta\sin\theta = (2\sin\theta\cos\theta)\cos\theta + (1-2\sin^2\theta)\sin\theta\)

\(= 2\sin\theta\cos^2\theta+\sin\theta-2\sin^3\theta = 2\sin\theta(1-\sin^2\theta)+\sin\theta-2\sin^3\theta = 3\sin\theta-4\sin^3\theta\)

Check at \(\theta=30^\circ\): \(\sin90^\circ=1\), and \(3\sin30^\circ-4\sin^330^\circ = 3(0.5)-4(0.125)=1.5-0.5=1\) ✓. One more angle, tripled instead of doubled, and the same disciplined chain of substitution reaches it — no new geometry required, ever.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Writing \(\sin(2\theta)=2\sin\theta\) — treating the \(2\) as though it distributes into the function the way it would distribute into a linear expression. Check at \(\theta=30^\circ\): the true value is \(\sin60^\circ\approx0.8660\), but \(2\sin30^\circ=2(0.5)=1\) — not equal. The correct formula, \(2\sin\theta\cos\theta\), needs both functions; dropping the \(\cos\theta\) factor is the single most common error with this identity.

Conclusion

This week traced one continuous argument: 7-13 established the rule that a proof may only move one side of the page; 7-14 proved the sum and difference formulas from the distance formula alone, without borrowing a single unproven fact; 7-15 put those formulas to work and let the cofunction family fall out as a byproduct; and today, setting \(\beta=\alpha\) turned the same four formulas into the double-angle family, with the triple-angle formula one more disciplined substitution beyond that. Week 26 asks the natural next question — if doubling an angle is one substitution away, what does halving one require? — and closes the unit with an honors capstone that asks you to choose, strategically, among every identity this unit has built.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does setting \(\beta=\alpha\) in an already-proven sum formula count as a legitimate proof of a new identity, rather than a coincidence that happens to work?

2. In Example 1, why does choosing the right form of \(\cos2\theta\) (rather than any of the other two, valid, equivalent forms) save work — and why must all three still agree?

3. What does the triple-angle derivation reveal about how far a small set of proven identities, applied and reapplied, can reach without ever requiring new geometry?

4. This whole week has been an argument about what a proof is allowed to assume. State, in your own words, what the unit's virtue of self-government has meant in the context of an identity proof.
