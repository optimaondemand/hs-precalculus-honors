Sum and Difference Formulas
Lesson · ⭐ This Week's Focus Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to prove the angle sum and difference formulas for cosine, sine, and tangent from first principles, without assuming any of them along the way.

  • I will be able to derive each new formula as a disciplined corollary of the one proved before it, rather than borrowing an unproven shortcut.

  • I will be able to use a sum or difference formula to compute the exact value of a non-special angle, such as \(15^\circ\) or \(105^\circ\), and verify that value numerically.

📚 VOCABULARY

Sum formula / difference formula: An identity expressing \(\sin(\alpha\pm\beta)\), \(\cos(\alpha\pm\beta)\), or \(\tan(\alpha\pm\beta)\) in terms of the trigonometric functions of \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) individually — the tool that reaches angles no unit-circle seed ever gave directly.

Cyclic quadrilateral: A four-sided figure whose four vertices all lie on a single circle.

Chord: A line segment joining two points on a circle — the ancestor of the sine function, more than a thousand years before angle notation as we use it existed.

Corollary: A true statement that follows immediately, by substitution or direct calculation, from a statement already proven — requiring no independent proof of its own.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Around the year 150, the astronomer Claudius Ptolemy needed a table of chords — the length of the straight segment connecting two points on a circle, for every angle between them — accurate enough to predict the positions of the planets. To build that table, he proved a theorem about any four points sitting on one circle: if you label them \(A\), \(B\), \(C\), \(D\) in order around the circle, then the product of the two diagonals equals the sum of the products of the two pairs of opposite sides, \(AC\cdot BD = AB\cdot CD + AD\cdot BC\). It looks like a fact about geometry with nothing to do with angles at all. It is not. Set up the circle with diameter exactly \(1\), and choose \(A\), \(C\) so that segment \(AC\) is itself a diameter. Then place \(B\) so that angle \(BAC=\alpha\) and \(D\) so that angle \(CAD=\beta\). Because \(AC\) is a diameter, the angles at \(B\) and at \(D\) are both right angles (a fact known since Thales), which makes \(AB=\cos\alpha\), \(BC=\sin\alpha\), \(AD=\cos\beta\), and \(CD=\sin\beta\) — four ordinary right-triangle ratios. Ptolemy's theorem, with \(AC=1\), now reads \(BD = \sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta\). And because \(BD\) is a chord subtending the inscribed angle \(\angle BAD = \alpha+\beta\) in a circle of diameter \(1\), that same chord also equals \(\sin(\alpha+\beta)\). Two descriptions of the same segment, set equal, and the sum formula for sine falls out of a theorem about quadrilaterals — centuries before anyone wrote \(\sin\) or \(\cos\) at all. Ptolemy's chord table was, in the language of his century, a sum-and-difference formula in disguise.

Today's proof takes the more modern route, with coordinates instead of chords — but it keeps exactly the discipline 7-13 insisted on. Nothing below is allowed to assume the formula it is proving. Each new result is either proved directly from the unit circle, or derived as a strict corollary of a result already proved.

Proving cos(α − β) from the Distance Formula

Place two points on the unit circle: \(P_1=(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)\) at angle \(\alpha\), and \(P_2=(\cos\beta,\sin\beta)\) at angle \(\beta\). Their squared distance apart, by the distance formula, is:

\(d^2 = (\cos\alpha-\cos\beta)^2+(\sin\alpha-\sin\beta)^2 = 2-2(\cos\alpha\cos\beta+\sin\alpha\sin\beta)\)

(the middle terms expand using \(\cos^2\alpha+\sin^2\alpha=1\) and \(\cos^2\beta+\sin^2\beta=1\)). Now rotate the whole picture rigidly by \(-\beta\) — a rotation changes no distance between two points, so \(d^2\) must come out the same number either way. After the rotation, \(P_2\) lands on \((1,0)\) and \(P_1\) lands on \((\cos(\alpha-\beta),\sin(\alpha-\beta))\), so the same distance, computed the second way, is:

\(d^2 = (\cos(\alpha-\beta)-1)^2+\sin^2(\alpha-\beta) = 2-2\cos(\alpha-\beta)\)

Both expressions equal the same physical distance, so they equal each other: \(2-2\cos(\alpha-\beta)=2-2(\cos\alpha\cos\beta+\sin\alpha\sin\beta)\), which simplifies directly to \(\boxed{\cos(\alpha-\beta)=\cos\alpha\cos\beta+\sin\alpha\sin\beta}\). Nothing here assumed the formula — only the distance formula and the Pythagorean identity, both already established.

Example 1 — the rest of the family, as corollaries

Derive \(\cos(\alpha+\beta)\), then \(\sin(\alpha+\beta)\), from the boxed formula above — without a second distance-formula argument.

Solution:

Cosine of a sum. Write \(\alpha+\beta\) as \(\alpha-(-\beta)\), and apply the boxed formula with the second angle replaced by \(-\beta\). Since \(\cos(-\beta)=\cos\beta\) and \(\sin(-\beta)=-\sin\beta\):

\(\cos(\alpha+\beta)=\cos(\alpha-(-\beta)) = \cos\alpha\cos(-\beta)+\sin\alpha\sin(-\beta) = \cos\alpha\cos\beta-\sin\alpha\sin\beta\)

A cofunction corollary, in passing. Set \(\alpha=\frac{\pi}{2}\) in the boxed formula: \(\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\beta\right)=\cos\frac{\pi}{2}\cos\beta+\sin\frac{\pi}{2}\sin\beta = (0)\cos\beta+(1)\sin\beta=\sin\beta\). This single substitution — not a separately assumed fact — gives \(\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta\right)=\sin\theta\) for every \(\theta\). Substituting \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}-\gamma\) into that result gives \(\cos\gamma=\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\gamma\right)\), i.e., \(\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta\right)=\cos\theta\) as well. (Lesson 7-15 returns to build the complete cofunction family from these two lines.)

Sine of a sum. Using \(\sin x=\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-x\right)\) with \(x=\alpha+\beta\):

\(\sin(\alpha+\beta) = \cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\alpha-\beta\right) = \cos\!\left(\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\alpha\right)-\beta\right)\)

Apply the boxed formula with angles \(\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\alpha\right)\) and \(\beta\):

\(=\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\alpha\right)\cos\beta+\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\alpha\right)\sin\beta = \sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta\)

Answer: \(\cos(\alpha+\beta)=\cos\alpha\cos\beta-\sin\alpha\sin\beta\) and \(\sin(\alpha+\beta)=\sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta\). Every step traces back to the one distance-formula proof — nothing borrowed, nothing assumed. \(\sin(\alpha-\beta)\) follows the same way \(\cos(\alpha+\beta)\) did, replacing \(\beta\) with \(-\beta\): \(\sin(\alpha-\beta)=\sin\alpha\cos\beta-\cos\alpha\sin\beta\).

📜 The Four Formulas, Gathered

\(\cos(\alpha\pm\beta)=\cos\alpha\cos\beta\mp\sin\alpha\sin\beta\)

\(\sin(\alpha\pm\beta)=\sin\alpha\cos\beta\pm\cos\alpha\sin\beta\)

\(\tan(\alpha\pm\beta)=\dfrac{\tan\alpha\pm\tan\beta}{1\mp\tan\alpha\tan\beta}\) — obtained by dividing the sine formula by the cosine formula, then dividing every term by \(\cos\alpha\cos\beta\).

Example 2 — an exact value no unit circle seed provides

Find the exact value of \(\cos 15^\circ\).

Solution:

Write \(15^\circ = 45^\circ-30^\circ\), two angles the unit circle already knows exactly:

\(\cos15^\circ=\cos(45^\circ-30^\circ)=\cos45^\circ\cos30^\circ+\sin45^\circ\sin30^\circ\)

\(=\left(\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)\!\left(\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)+\left(\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)\!\left(\dfrac12\right)=\dfrac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}\)

Verify: \(\dfrac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}\approx\dfrac{2.4495+1.4142}{4}\approx0.9659\), which matches a calculator's \(\cos15^\circ\approx0.9659\) exactly.

Answer: \(\cos15^\circ=\dfrac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}\).

Example 3

Find the exact value of \(\tan 105^\circ\).

Solution:

Write \(105^\circ=60^\circ+45^\circ\):

\(\tan105^\circ = \dfrac{\tan60^\circ+\tan45^\circ}{1-\tan60^\circ\tan45^\circ} = \dfrac{\sqrt3+1}{1-\sqrt3}\)

Rationalize by multiplying by \(\dfrac{1+\sqrt3}{1+\sqrt3}\):

\(=\dfrac{(\sqrt3+1)(1+\sqrt3)}{(1-\sqrt3)(1+\sqrt3)} = \dfrac{4+2\sqrt3}{-2} = -(2+\sqrt3)\)

Verify: \(-(2+\sqrt3)\approx-(2+1.7321)\approx-3.7321\), matching a calculator's \(\tan105^\circ\approx-3.7321\).

Answer: \(\tan105^\circ = -(2+\sqrt3)\). The negative sign is not a mistake to erase — \(105^\circ\) is a second-quadrant angle, where tangent is negative, exactly as the exact value shows.

▶  Try it yourself: Find the exact value of \(\sin75^\circ\), writing \(75^\circ=45^\circ+30^\circ\), and verify it numerically.
\(\sin75^\circ=\sin45^\circ\cos30^\circ+\cos45^\circ\sin30^\circ=\dfrac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}\approx0.9659\), matching a calculator's \(\sin75^\circ\approx0.9659\). (Notice this is the same value as \(\cos15^\circ\) in Example 2 — a preview of the cofunction relationship 7-15 formalizes.)
▶  Try it yourself: Use the sum formula to verify \(\sin(x+\pi)=-\sin x\).
\(\sin(x+\pi)=\sin x\cos\pi+\cos x\sin\pi = \sin x(-1)+\cos x(0) = -\sin x\). This is exactly why shifting a sine graph by \(\pi\) (half a period) flips it upside down — the algebra and the graph transformation from Week 22 are the same fact.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Treating the sum formula as if it distributed, writing \(\cos(\alpha+\beta)=\cos\alpha+\cos\beta\). Check it at \(\alpha=60^\circ\), \(\beta=30^\circ\): the true value is \(\cos90^\circ=0\), but \(\cos60^\circ+\cos30^\circ\approx0.5+0.8660=1.3660\) — nowhere close. Trigonometric functions are not linear; a sum inside the function does not become a sum of the function's outputs. The correct formula's extra cross-terms, \(\mp\sin\alpha\sin\beta\) or \(\pm\cos\alpha\sin\beta\), are not decorations — they carry the entire content of the identity.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Ptolemy's chord table, built partly on the quadrilateral argument above, remained the best trigonometric reference in the Mediterranean and Islamic worlds for well over a thousand years — longer than the distance between his lifetime and ours divided in two. A single correct theorem, honestly proved once, can outlive empires. That, too, is a form of self-government: getting the argument right the first time, so no one downstream has to take it on faith.

Conclusion

Four formulas, one proof, and a chain of corollaries that never once assumed what it was trying to show: that is the entire content of today's lesson, and it is why this is the week's Focus lesson — every later identity in this unit either applies these formulas or specializes them. Lesson 7-15 puts them to work simplifying and evaluating, and formally completes the cofunction relationship that fell out almost by accident above. Lesson 7-16 does something quietly remarkable with the very same four formulas: it sets \(\beta=\alpha\) and lets a whole new family — the double-angle formulas — fall out for free.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. In the distance-formula proof, what step guarantees that the two computed distances must be equal, and why is that step doing all the real work of the proof?

2. What makes the cofunction relationship derived in Example 1 a corollary rather than an assumption — and why does that distinction matter after Lesson 7-13?

3. Why does \(105^\circ=60^\circ+45^\circ\) give the same tangent value regardless of which two special angles you choose to decompose it into (try \(105^\circ=135^\circ-30^\circ\) if you want to check)?

4. Ptolemy proved a theorem about quadrilaterals nearly two thousand years before anyone wrote a sum formula in modern notation. What has to be true about mathematics for the same fact to survive translation across that much time and notation?
