The Law of Sines
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to derive the Law of Sines from the area-of-a-triangle relationship.

  • I will be able to solve an oblique triangle given AAS or ASA data, and know why each of these cases produces exactly one triangle.

  • I will be able to apply the Law of Sines to real surveying and navigation problems, checking every solved triangle for closure before trusting it.

📚 VOCABULARY

Oblique triangle: A triangle with no right angle. Every triangle you have solved since Unit 6 has leaned on a \(90^\circ\) angle somewhere; an oblique triangle offers none, so \(\sin\), \(\cos\), and \(\tan\) of a single acute angle can no longer carry the whole solution.

Law of Sines: For any triangle with angles \(A, B, C\) and sides \(a, b, c\) opposite them, \(\dfrac{\sin A}{a} = \dfrac{\sin B}{b} = \dfrac{\sin C}{c}\) — every angle's sine stands in the same ratio to the length of the side across from it.

AAS / ASA: Two angle-and-side data patterns — two angles and a non-included side (AAS), or two angles and the included side (ASA) — that between them fix a triangle's third angle by the angle-sum property and its remaining two sides by the Law of Sines, with exactly one triangle as the result.

Circumdiameter: The constant \(2R\) that each ratio \(\dfrac{\sin A}{a}\) actually equals, where \(R\) is the radius of the circle that passes through all three vertices of the triangle — a fact this lesson's honors work puts to use, not merely states.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Sometime in the second century AD, the astronomer Claudius Ptolemy sat in Alexandria compiling a table of chords — for every arc of a circle, the straight-line length of the chord it cuts off — so that he could predict the positions of the sun, moon, and planets against the fixed stars. Along the way he proved a theorem about quadrilaterals inscribed in a circle: for such a figure, the product of the two diagonals equals the sum of the products of the two pairs of opposite sides. It looks, at first glance, like a curiosity about four-sided figures. But buried inside Ptolemy's chord relationships is the very same proportion this lesson is about to name: the length of a chord and the sine of the angle it subtends are locked together, so that a triangle's sides and the sines of its opposite angles rise and fall in lockstep. Ptolemy never wrote the Law of Sines in the notation you are about to learn, but the astronomers, surveyors, and navigators who inherited his chord tables were, in every practical sense, already using it. You are joining a conversation almost two thousand years old.

Every triangle you solved in Unit 6 had a right angle to lean on: once you knew one acute angle and one side, SOH-CAH-TOA supplied everything else. An oblique triangle — no right angle anywhere — refuses that shortcut. You need a relationship that holds across the whole triangle, not just across one of its right-angle pieces. The Law of Sines is that relationship, and it falls directly out of a formula you already know: the area of a triangle.

Deriving the Law of Sines from Area

Take any triangle with sides \(a, b, c\) opposite angles \(A, B, C\). Drop an altitude from vertex \(C\) to side \(c\) (or imagine one from any vertex — the argument is the same from any corner). That altitude has length \(a\sin B\) (read it off the right triangle the altitude creates with side \(a\) and angle \(B\)), or equally \(b\sin A\) (read from the right triangle on the other side of the altitude). Since “area \(= \frac{1}{2}\)(base)(height)” doesn't care which altitude you drop, all three choices of base-and-altitude must give the same area:

\(\text{Area} = \tfrac{1}{2}ab\sin C = \tfrac{1}{2}bc\sin A = \tfrac{1}{2}ca\sin B\)

Divide every term of that chain by \(\tfrac{1}{2}abc\):

\(\dfrac{\sin C}{c} = \dfrac{\sin A}{a} = \dfrac{\sin B}{b}\)

That is the Law of Sines. Nothing about the derivation assumed a right angle — it holds for every triangle, obtuse ones included, because “area” and “altitude” are honest for any shape at all.

The Law of Sines needs a full angle and its opposite side as one matched pair before it can do anything — it is a chain of equal ratios, and a ratio needs both its parts. That is exactly what AAS and ASA data supply: two angles pin down the third by the angle-sum property (\(A+B+C=180^\circ\)), and once all three angles are known, any one known side unlocks both of the others through the Law of Sines ratio. Each of these two data patterns produces exactly one triangle — a fact worth noticing now, because tomorrow's lesson shows what happens when the given side is not opposite a known angle, and the guarantee disappears.

Example 1

Solve the triangle with \(A = 40^\circ\), \(B = 60^\circ\), and \(a = 10\).

Solution:

First, the angle sum: \(C = 180^\circ - 40^\circ - 60^\circ = 80^\circ\). Now every angle is known, and side \(a\) is matched with its opposite angle \(A\), so the Law of Sines ratio \(\dfrac{\sin A}{a}\) is fully known and can be set equal to each unknown ratio in turn:

\(\dfrac{\sin 60^\circ}{b} = \dfrac{\sin 40^\circ}{10} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; b = \dfrac{10\sin 60^\circ}{\sin 40^\circ} \approx \dfrac{10(0.8660)}{0.6428} \approx 13.47\)

\(\dfrac{\sin 80^\circ}{c} = \dfrac{\sin 40^\circ}{10} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; c = \dfrac{10\sin 80^\circ}{\sin 40^\circ} \approx \dfrac{10(0.9848)}{0.6428} \approx 15.32\)

Verify: angles sum to \(40^\circ+60^\circ+80^\circ=180^\circ\); the shortest side (\(a=10\), opposite the smallest angle \(40^\circ\)) is indeed the smallest side, and the longest side (\(c\approx15.32\), opposite the largest angle \(80^\circ\)) is indeed the longest — the triangle closes and its side–angle order agrees with itself.

Answer: \(C = 80^\circ\), \(b \approx 13.47\), \(c \approx 15.32\).

Example 2 — A River Crossing

Two surveyors stand \(250\) m apart at points \(A\) and \(B\) on a straight riverbank. Both sight a lone tree at point \(C\) on the far bank. The angle at \(A\) between the bank and the line of sight to the tree is \(63^\circ\); the angle at \(B\) between the bank and its line of sight is \(52^\circ\). Find the distance from each surveyor to the tree.

Solution:

This is ASA: the two given angles sit at \(A\) and \(B\), with the known side \(c = AB = 250\) m included between them. First, \(C = 180^\circ - 63^\circ - 52^\circ = 65^\circ\). The Law of Sines then gives both unknown distances from the one known ratio \(\dfrac{\sin C}{c}\):

\(a = BC = \dfrac{c\sin A}{\sin C} = \dfrac{250\sin 63^\circ}{\sin 65^\circ} \approx \dfrac{250(0.8910)}{0.9063} \approx 245.8 \text{ m}\)

\(b = AC = \dfrac{c\sin B}{\sin C} = \dfrac{250\sin 52^\circ}{\sin 65^\circ} \approx \dfrac{250(0.7880)}{0.9063} \approx 217.4 \text{ m}\)

Verify: the perpendicular distance from the tree to the riverbank should come out the same whether you compute it from \(A\)'s side or from \(B\)'s side. From \(A\): \(b\sin A \approx 217.4(0.8910)\approx193.7\) m. From \(B\): \(a\sin B\approx245.8(0.7880)\approx193.7\) m. They agree — the two sightlines describe one consistent tree, not two.

Answer: \(BC \approx 245.8\) m, \(AC \approx 217.4\) m (and the river itself is about \(193.7\) m across).

📡 Self-Government: One Path, Checked Twice

Notice what Example 2 just did: it found the same distance across the river two independent ways — once through \(A\)'s sightline, once through \(B\)'s — and required them to agree before calling the answer trustworthy. That habit is this unit's virtue in its plainest form. Self-government is not only about periodic motion repeating in ordered discipline; it is the disposition to hold your own work to a standard whether or not anyone is checking it — to verify a triangle's closure, to distrust an answer that agrees with itself by luck rather than by structure, and to do that checking as a matter of habit, not because a rubric demands it.

Example 3 — Radar Triangulation

Two coastal radar stations \(A\) and \(B\) sit \(15\) km apart along a straight, east–west coastline, with \(A\) to the west. Station \(A\) reports a ship on bearing N\(54^\circ\)E; station \(B\) reports the same ship on bearing N\(68^\circ\)W. Find the ship's distance from each station.

Solution:

Convert each bearing into an interior angle of the triangle formed by \(A\), \(B\), and the ship \(C\). Since the coastline runs east–west, the ray from \(A\) toward \(B\) points due east, and “due north” is perpendicular to it. A bearing of N\(54^\circ\)E is \(54^\circ\) measured from north toward east, so the angle it makes with the eastward coastline (i.e., with ray \(AB\)) is the complement: \(A = 90^\circ - 54^\circ = 36^\circ\). At \(B\), the ray toward \(A\) points due west, and a bearing of N\(68^\circ\)W is \(68^\circ\) from north toward west, so its angle with ray \(BA\) is \(B = 90^\circ - 68^\circ = 22^\circ\). This is ASA data with the included side \(c = AB = 15\) km.

\(C = 180^\circ - 36^\circ - 22^\circ = 122^\circ\)

\(a = BC = \dfrac{c\sin A}{\sin C} = \dfrac{15\sin 36^\circ}{\sin 122^\circ} \approx \dfrac{15(0.5878)}{0.8480} \approx 10.4 \text{ km}\)

\(b = AC = \dfrac{c\sin B}{\sin C} = \dfrac{15\sin 22^\circ}{\sin 122^\circ} \approx \dfrac{15(0.3746)}{0.8480} \approx 6.6 \text{ km}\)

Verify: triangle inequality holds (\(6.6+10.4>15\)), and the angle sum is exactly \(180^\circ\). The obtuse angle \(122^\circ\) sits opposite the longest side \(c=15\), as it must.

Answer: The ship is about \(10.4\) km from station \(B\) and about \(6.6\) km from station \(A\). Notice the conversion step — bearing to interior angle — is exactly the disciplined habit Lesson 6-11 insisted on with compass headings; oblique triangles do not excuse you from it.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Matching a side to the wrong angle in the Law of Sines ratio. The ratio \(\dfrac{\sin A}{a}\) pairs an angle with the side directly across from it — not the side next to it, and not whichever side is given first in the problem. Before writing a single ratio, redraw the triangle and label every side with the letter of the angle it faces; a mismatched pair produces a number that looks plausible but solves a triangle that does not exist.
▶  Try it yourself: Solve the triangle with \(B = 48^\circ\), \(C = 77^\circ\), and \(b = 26\).
\(A = 180^\circ-48^\circ-77^\circ = 55^\circ\). \(a = \dfrac{26\sin55^\circ}{\sin48^\circ}\approx 28.66\). \(c = \dfrac{26\sin77^\circ}{\sin48^\circ}\approx 34.09\).
▶  Try it yourself: Why does knowing two angles of a triangle automatically hand you the third, with no further work?
Because the three interior angles of any triangle always sum to \(180^\circ\) — a fixed fact of Euclidean geometry, not a Law-of-Sines result. Once \(A\) and \(B\) are known, \(C=180^\circ-A-B\) is forced; the Law of Sines only becomes necessary afterward, to recover the side lengths.
💻 ON YOUR CALCULATOR
Confirm Example 3 in Desmos (desmos.com/calculator): type 15sin(36°)/sin(122°) directly into an expression line — Desmos accepts the degree symbol from its keyboard, or you can type 36 deg and it auto-formats. You do not need to convert to radians by hand for a one-off ratio like this, but always glance at the result: an answer wildly larger than either given side, or negative, signals a mismatched angle–side pair before you have even reached for a protractor.
▶  Try it yourself: For the triangle in Example 1 (\(A=40^\circ, B=60^\circ, C=80^\circ, a=10\)), compute \(\dfrac{a}{\sin A}\), \(\dfrac{b}{\sin B}\), and \(\dfrac{c}{\sin C}\). What do you notice?
All three equal approximately \(15.56\). This is the circumdiameter \(2R\) — the diameter of the unique circle passing through all three vertices — and it is the same for every angle–side pair in a given triangle, a fact Ptolemy's chord tables were, in effect, already exploiting.

Conclusion

The Law of Sines settles AAS and ASA triangles cleanly, and it does so because two angles plus one side leave nothing undetermined: the angle sum fixes the third angle, and the ratio fixes the rest. But notice what made that certainty possible — the known side was always paired with a known angle directly across from it. Tomorrow's lesson asks what happens when that is no longer true: two sides and an angle that is not included between them. The same Law of Sines still applies, but the certainty of “exactly one triangle” quietly disappears, and a disciplined case-by-case check — the self-governed habit Example 2 already modeled — becomes the whole of the mathematics.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does the Law of Sines — unlike SOH-CAH-TOA — place no requirement on any angle of the triangle being \(90^\circ\)?

2. In the derivation from area, why is it legitimate to set \(\tfrac{1}{2}ab\sin C\) equal to \(\tfrac{1}{2}bc\sin A\)?

3. Why do AAS and ASA data each guarantee exactly one triangle, when the angle-sum property is doing part of the work before the Law of Sines is even needed?

4. What did checking the river's width two independent ways in Example 2 actually prove, and why does that habit belong to this unit's virtue of self-government?
