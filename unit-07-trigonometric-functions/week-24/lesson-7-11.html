The Law of Cosines and Triangle Area
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to derive the Law of Cosines two independent ways — by dropping an altitude, and by placing the triangle on coordinate axes and applying the distance formula.

  • I will be able to solve any SAS or SSS triangle using the Law of Cosines, with no ambiguous case to check.

  • I will be able to compute a triangle's area two ways — from \(\tfrac{1}{2}ab\sin C\) and from Heron's formula — and use the agreement between them as a check on my own work.

📚 VOCABULARY

Law of Cosines: For a triangle with sides \(a,b,c\) and angle \(C\) opposite side \(c\), \(c^2 = a^2+b^2-2ab\cos C\) — a generalization of the Pythagorean theorem that supplies the correction term \(-2ab\cos C\) whenever \(C\) is not \(90^\circ\).

SAS / SSS: Two triangle data patterns — two sides and the included angle (SAS), or all three sides (SSS) — that the Law of Cosines solves directly, with no case analysis required, because a triangle's angles are entirely determined once its three sides are fixed.

Semiperimeter (\(s\)): Half the perimeter of a triangle, \(s = \dfrac{a+b+c}{2}\) — the single quantity Heron's formula builds everything else from.

Heron's formula: \(\text{Area} = \sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}\), giving a triangle's area from its three side lengths alone, with no angle needed at all — named for Heron of Alexandria, the first-century engineer and mathematician who proved it.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Two lessons ago, the Law of Sines cracked open AAS and ASA triangles by relating angles to their opposite sides. Yesterday, SSA data cracked that same law's certainty wide open, forcing a disciplined four-case check before a single triangle could be trusted. Today's lesson removes the case-checking altogether, because it starts from a different kind of data entirely: SAS (two sides and the angle between them) and SSS (all three sides). Neither pattern lets a triangle wobble between two shapes — two sides and their included angle rigidly determine one triangle, full stop, and so do three side lengths. What is needed is a tool built for exactly this rigidity: the Law of Cosines. It will pay for itself twice today — once as the way through SAS and SSS, and again as the fastest route to a triangle's area when its only known height would otherwise have to be reconstructed from scratch.

Derivation 1 — Dropping an Altitude

Take triangle \(ABC\) with the angle \(C\) between sides \(a=BC\) and \(b=AC\), and drop an altitude from vertex \(B\) down to line \(AC\), meeting it at point \(D\), with height \(h = BD\). In right triangle \(BDC\): \(h = a\sin C\) and \(DC = a\cos C\). Since \(AD = AC - DC = b - a\cos C\), right triangle \(BDA\) gives, by the Pythagorean theorem:

\(c^2 = h^2 + AD^2 = (a\sin C)^2 + (b-a\cos C)^2\)

\(= a^2\sin^2C + b^2 - 2ab\cos C + a^2\cos^2C = a^2(\sin^2C+\cos^2C) + b^2 - 2ab\cos C\)

Since \(\sin^2C+\cos^2C=1\) (Lesson 7-12 makes this identity's own proof explicit), the result collapses to \(c^2 = a^2+b^2-2ab\cos C\) — the Law of Cosines. Notice what happens when \(C=90^\circ\): \(\cos C = 0\), the correction term vanishes, and the formula becomes the Pythagorean theorem exactly. The Law of Cosines does not replace Pythagoras — it is Pythagoras's honest generalization to every angle, not only right ones.

Derivation 2 — The Distance Formula in Disguise

Place the same triangle on coordinate axes with vertex \(C\) at the origin and vertex \(A\) at \((b,0)\) along the positive \(x\)-axis (side \(CA=b\) lies flat on the axis by construction). Vertex \(B\) is a distance \(a\) from \(C\) at angle \(C\) above the axis, so \(B = (a\cos C,\, a\sin C)\). Side \(c = AB\) is now just the distance between two coordinate points:

\(c^2 = (b - a\cos C)^2 + (0 - a\sin C)^2\)

Expand this and it is the identical algebra as Derivation 1 — \(c^2 = a^2+b^2-2ab\cos C\) falls out again. This is not a coincidence: dropping the altitude from \(B\) to the \(x\)-axis in this picture reconstructs exactly the right triangles \(BDC\) and \(BDA\) from Derivation 1. Two derivations, one built from geometry and one from coordinates, are two views of a single fact — a small, satisfying confirmation that the result does not depend on which tool proved it.

Example 1 — SAS

Solve the triangle with \(a=8\), \(b=10\), and included angle \(C=55^\circ\).

Solution:

\(c^2 = a^2+b^2-2ab\cos C = 8^2+10^2-2(8)(10)\cos55^\circ = 164 - 160(0.5736) \approx 72.22\)

\(c \approx \sqrt{72.22} \approx 8.50\)

With all three sides now known, find angle \(A\) by the Law of Cosines again — not the Law of Sines, which would risk the ambiguous case:

\(\cos A = \dfrac{b^2+c^2-a^2}{2bc} = \dfrac{100+72.22-64}{2(10)(8.50)} \approx \dfrac{108.22}{169.98} \approx 0.6367 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; A \approx 50.5^\circ\)

\(B = 180^\circ - 55^\circ - 50.5^\circ \approx 74.5^\circ\)

Verify: \(\dfrac{\sin A}{a} \approx \dfrac{\sin50.5^\circ}{8}\approx0.0964\) and \(\dfrac{\sin C}{c}\approx\dfrac{\sin55^\circ}{8.50}\approx0.0964\) — the Law of Sines ratios agree, confirming the solve.

Answer: \(c\approx8.50\), \(A\approx50.5^\circ\), \(B\approx74.5^\circ\).

Example 2 — SSS

Solve the triangle with \(a=7\), \(b=9\), \(c=12\).

Solution:

With SSS, there is no angle to start from — find the largest angle first, since it is the one a solver most needs to get right (an obtuse angle here would go undetected by a careless solver using only the Law of Sines). It sits opposite the longest side, \(c=12\):

\(\cos C = \dfrac{a^2+b^2-c^2}{2ab} = \dfrac{49+81-144}{2(7)(9)} = \dfrac{-14}{126} \approx -0.1111 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; C \approx 96.4^\circ\)

The negative cosine already tells you \(C\) is obtuse before you even take the inverse cosine — a built-in check the Law of Cosines gives for free, since \(\cos\) is negative exactly when an angle is obtuse.

\(\cos A = \dfrac{b^2+c^2-a^2}{2bc} = \dfrac{81+144-49}{2(9)(12)} = \dfrac{176}{216} \approx 0.8148 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; A \approx 35.4^\circ\)

\(B = 180^\circ-96.4^\circ-35.4^\circ\approx48.2^\circ\)

Verify: \(\dfrac{\sin A}{a}\approx\dfrac{\sin35.4^\circ}{7}\approx0.0828\), \(\dfrac{\sin B}{b}\approx\dfrac{\sin48.2^\circ}{9}\approx0.0828\), \(\dfrac{\sin C}{c}\approx\dfrac{\sin96.4^\circ}{12}\approx0.0828\) — all three ratios agree.

Answer: \(C\approx96.4^\circ\), \(A\approx35.4^\circ\), \(B\approx48.2^\circ\).

📏 Two Ways to Measure the Same Ground

A triangle's area from \(\tfrac{1}{2}ab\sin C\) needs an angle; Heron's formula needs no angle at all, only the three sides. That the two methods, starting from completely different information, always land on the same number is not a coincidence to shrug past — it is a fact worth proving to yourself once by cross-checking a real triangle both ways, exactly as Examples 1 and 2 will do next. A method that agrees with an independent method by structure, not by luck, is the entire discipline this unit keeps insisting on.

Example 3 — A Triangular Plot of Land

A surveyor measures two sides of a triangular plot as \(150\) m and \(200\) m, with an included angle of \(72^\circ\) between them. Find the length of the third side (the fence needed to close the plot) and the plot's area, confirming the area two independent ways.

Solution:

Third side, by the Law of Cosines:

\(c^2 = 150^2+200^2-2(150)(200)\cos72^\circ = 62500 - 60000(0.3090) \approx 43960 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; c\approx209.7 \text{ m}\)

Area, from \(\tfrac{1}{2}ab\sin C\):

\(\text{Area} = \tfrac{1}{2}(150)(200)\sin72^\circ \approx 15000(0.9511) \approx 14{,}266 \text{ m}^2\)

Area, from Heron's formula (using all three sides, \(a=150,b=200,c\approx209.7\)):

\(s = \dfrac{150+200+209.7}{2} \approx 279.8\)

\(\text{Area} = \sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)} = \sqrt{279.8(129.8)(79.8)(70.1)} \approx 14{,}266 \text{ m}^2\)

Answer: Fence length \(\approx209.7\) m; area \(\approx14{,}266\) m\(^2\), confirmed by two independent formulas that share no common step until the final square root.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
After finding the largest angle in an SSS triangle by the Law of Cosines, switching to the Law of Sines to find the remaining angles — and picking the wrong companion angle by accident. It is not wrong in principle (the largest angle is already safely identified, so no ambiguity remains for the smaller two), but it invites an unnecessary risk. Safer and just as fast: use the Law of Cosines again for the second angle, exactly as Examples 1 and 2 did, and get the third by subtraction from \(180^\circ\). One tool, used consistently, is more trustworthy than switching tools mid-problem.
▶  Try it yourself: Find the area of the triangle in Example 2 (\(a=7,b=9,c=12\)) two ways, and confirm they agree.
Heron's: \(s=\dfrac{7+9+12}{2}=14\); Area \(=\sqrt{14(7)(5)(2)}=\sqrt{980}\approx31.30\). Using \(\tfrac12 ab\sin C\) with \(C\approx96.4^\circ\): Area \(\approx\tfrac12(7)(9)\sin96.4^\circ\approx31.5(0.9938)\approx31.30\). They agree.
▶  Try it yourself: Why does the Law of Cosines never need an ambiguous-case check, even though it also relies on an inverse trig function?
Because \(\cos^{-1}\) returns exactly one angle between \(0^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\) for any input between \(-1\) and \(1\) — and every angle of a triangle already lies in that exact range. Unlike \(\sin^{-1}\), which only covers \(-90^\circ\) to \(90^\circ\) and therefore always has a missing obtuse companion to consider, \(\cos^{-1}\) already speaks the full language a triangle's angles need.
💻 ON YOUR CALCULATOR
Check Example 3 in Desmos (desmos.com/calculator): define c=sqrt(150^2+200^2-2*150*200*cos(72°)) on one line, then s=(150+200+c)/2, then A=sqrt(s(s-150)(s-200)(s-c)) on a third — Desmos will show \(A\approx14265.8\), matching the hand computation. Building the calculation in labeled steps like this, rather than one long expression, is itself good mathematical presentation — a habit worth carrying from paper onto the screen.

Conclusion

Two derivations converged on one formula, and two area formulas — one needing an angle, one needing none — converged on one number, twice. That double convergence is the real content of today's lesson: the Law of Cosines does not merely solve SAS and SSS triangles, it does so with a built-in honesty check available at every step, from the sign of \(\cos C\) revealing an obtuse angle before you finish the arithmetic, to Heron's formula confirming an area found by an entirely different route. Lesson 7-12 turns to a different kind of checked truth — not a triangle's closure, but an equation's, as the Pythagorean identity that quietly powered today's altitude-drop derivation finally gets proven in its own right.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does the Law of Cosines reduce to the Pythagorean theorem exactly when \(C=90^\circ\), and what does that tell you about which theorem is the more general one?

2. What did placing the triangle on coordinate axes actually add, if the algebra in Derivation 2 turned out identical to Derivation 1's?

3. Why is finding an SSS triangle's largest angle first a wiser first move than finding any angle at random?

4. What did confirming Example 3's area two independent ways actually prove, and how is that the same discipline Lesson 7-9 practiced when it checked the river's width two ways?
