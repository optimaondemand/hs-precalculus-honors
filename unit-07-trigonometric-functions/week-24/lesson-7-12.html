The Pythagorean Identities: Proof and Practice
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to prove \(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1\) directly from the unit-circle definition of sine and cosine.

  • I will be able to derive the two companion identities, \(\tan^2\theta+1=\sec^2\theta\) and \(1+\cot^2\theta=\csc^2\theta\), by dividing the original identity through by \(\cos^2\theta\) and \(\sin^2\theta\).

  • I will be able to use all three Pythagorean identities to find unknown trigonometric values and to simplify or verify trigonometric expressions.

📚 VOCABULARY

Pythagorean identity: The equation \(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1\), true for every real number \(\theta\) — not an equation to solve for particular values of \(\theta\), but a statement true for all of them at once, because it restates the unit circle's own definition, \(x^2+y^2=1\).

Companion identity: One of the two identities produced by dividing the original Pythagorean identity through by \(\cos^2\theta\) (giving \(\tan^2\theta+1=\sec^2\theta\)) or by \(\sin^2\theta\) (giving \(1+\cot^2\theta=\csc^2\theta\)) — same fact, restated in the language of a different pair of functions.

Reciprocal / quotient identities: The definitions carried forward from Lesson 6-7 — \(\tan\theta=\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}\), \(\cot\theta=\dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\), \(\sec\theta=\dfrac{1}{\cos\theta}\), \(\csc\theta=\dfrac{1}{\sin\theta}\) — that make today's division-based derivations possible.

Verify an identity: To confirm that an equation holds for every value of \(\theta\) in its domain, typically by transforming one side using known identities until it matches the other — the discipline Lesson 7-13 turns into its own full lesson.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Lesson 7-11's altitude-drop proof of the Law of Cosines used a single line without pausing on it: “since \(\sin^2C+\cos^2C=1\)”, and moved on. That line deserves to be proven in its own right, not borrowed on faith — and it turns out to need almost nothing beyond a definition you have known since Unit 6. Today's lesson proves it properly, then shows that one identity secretly contains two more, each waiting to be uncovered by nothing more adventurous than division.

Proving \(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1\)

Recall the unit-circle definition from Lesson 6-5: for any angle \(\theta\) in standard position, its terminal side meets the unit circle \(x^2+y^2=1\) at the point \((\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\). That point lies on the unit circle by definition — there is no additional assumption to make. Substitute \(x=\cos\theta\) and \(y=\sin\theta\) directly into the circle's own equation:

\(x^2+y^2=1 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta = 1\)

That is the entire proof. It holds for every real number \(\theta\), with no quadrant exception and no domain restriction, because every angle's terminal side meets the unit circle at exactly one point, and that point's coordinates are the definitions of cosine and sine — not approximations of them.

Deriving the Two Companion Identities

Divide every term by \(\cos^2\theta\) (valid wherever \(\cos\theta\ne0\)):

\(\dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{\cos^2\theta} + \dfrac{\cos^2\theta}{\cos^2\theta} = \dfrac{1}{\cos^2\theta} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \tan^2\theta + 1 = \sec^2\theta\)

Divide every term by \(\sin^2\theta\) instead (valid wherever \(\sin\theta\ne0\)):

\(\dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{\sin^2\theta} + \dfrac{\cos^2\theta}{\sin^2\theta} = \dfrac{1}{\sin^2\theta} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; 1 + \cot^2\theta = \csc^2\theta\)

Both companions are the same identity wearing different clothes — nothing new was assumed, only a division applied to something already true. And each companion inherits its parent's domain restriction: \(\tan^2\theta+1=\sec^2\theta\) cannot hold at \(\theta=\dfrac{\pi}{2}\), where \(\cos\theta=0\) makes both \(\tan\theta\) and \(\sec\theta\) undefined; likewise \(1+\cot^2\theta=\csc^2\theta\) cannot hold wherever \(\sin\theta=0\).

Example 1

Given \(\sin\theta = \dfrac{3}{5}\) with \(\theta\) in Quadrant II, find \(\cos\theta\), \(\tan\theta\), \(\sec\theta\), \(\csc\theta\), and \(\cot\theta\).

Solution:

From the Pythagorean identity: \(\cos^2\theta = 1-\sin^2\theta = 1-\dfrac{9}{25}=\dfrac{16}{25}\), so \(\cos\theta=\pm\dfrac{4}{5}\). Quadrant II means cosine is negative, so \(\cos\theta=-\dfrac{4}{5}\).

\(\tan\theta = \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} = \dfrac{3/5}{-4/5} = -\dfrac{3}{4} \qquad \sec\theta = \dfrac{1}{\cos\theta} = -\dfrac{5}{4} \qquad \csc\theta=\dfrac{1}{\sin\theta}=\dfrac{5}{3} \qquad \cot\theta=\dfrac{1}{\tan\theta}=-\dfrac{4}{3}\)

Verify: \(\tan^2\theta+1 = \dfrac{9}{16}+1=\dfrac{25}{16}\), and \(\sec^2\theta=\left(-\dfrac54\right)^2=\dfrac{25}{16}\) — they match, confirming the companion identity holds for these values too.

Answer: \(\cos\theta=-\dfrac45\), \(\tan\theta=-\dfrac34\), \(\sec\theta=-\dfrac54\), \(\csc\theta=\dfrac53\), \(\cot\theta=-\dfrac43\).

Example 2

Given \(\cos\theta = -\dfrac{12}{13}\) with \(\theta\) in Quadrant III, find the other five values.

Solution:

\(\sin^2\theta = 1-\cos^2\theta = 1-\dfrac{144}{169}=\dfrac{25}{169}\), so \(\sin\theta=\pm\dfrac{5}{13}\). Quadrant III means sine is negative: \(\sin\theta=-\dfrac{5}{13}\).

\(\tan\theta=\dfrac{-5/13}{-12/13}=\dfrac{5}{12} \qquad \sec\theta=\dfrac{1}{-12/13}=-\dfrac{13}{12} \qquad \csc\theta=\dfrac{1}{-5/13}=-\dfrac{13}{5} \qquad \cot\theta=\dfrac{12}{5}\)

Verify: \(\tan^2\theta+1=\dfrac{25}{144}+1=\dfrac{169}{144}\), and \(\sec^2\theta=\left(\dfrac{13}{12}\right)^2=\dfrac{169}{144}\) — matches.

Answer: \(\sin\theta=-\dfrac{5}{13}\), \(\tan\theta=\dfrac{5}{12}\), \(\sec\theta=-\dfrac{13}{12}\), \(\csc\theta=-\dfrac{13}{5}\), \(\cot\theta=\dfrac{12}{5}\). Notice both examples used a familiar integer triple (\(3\text{-}4\text{-}5\), then \(5\text{-}12\text{-}13\)) underneath the fractions — the Pythagorean identity is the same relationship the Pythagorean theorem gives a right triangle, only rescaled onto the unit circle.

Example 3 — Simplifying with the Identity

Simplify \(\sin\theta\csc\theta - \cos^2\theta\) to a single trigonometric expression.

Solution:

Since \(\csc\theta=\dfrac{1}{\sin\theta}\), the product \(\sin\theta\csc\theta=\sin\theta\cdot\dfrac{1}{\sin\theta}=1\). The expression becomes \(1-\cos^2\theta\), and the Pythagorean identity, rearranged, states exactly that \(1-\cos^2\theta=\sin^2\theta\).

Answer: \(\sin\theta\csc\theta-\cos^2\theta = \sin^2\theta\). Check numerically at \(\theta=37^\circ\): \(\sin37^\circ\csc37^\circ-\cos^237^\circ\approx1-0.6378=0.3622\), and \(\sin^237^\circ\approx0.3622\) — matches.

Example 4 — Combining Both Companions

Show that \(\sec^2\theta+\csc^2\theta=\sec^2\theta\csc^2\theta\) for every \(\theta\) where both sides are defined.

Solution:

Rewrite both terms on the left over a common denominator using the reciprocal definitions:

\(\sec^2\theta+\csc^2\theta = \dfrac{1}{\cos^2\theta}+\dfrac{1}{\sin^2\theta} = \dfrac{\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta}{\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta}\)

The numerator is exactly the original Pythagorean identity, equal to \(1\):

\(= \dfrac{1}{\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta} = \dfrac{1}{\cos^2\theta}\cdot\dfrac{1}{\sin^2\theta} = \sec^2\theta\csc^2\theta\)

Answer: Confirmed — and confirmed numerically at \(\theta=50^\circ\) as well: \(\sec^250^\circ+\csc^250^\circ\approx4.1244\), and \(\sec^250^\circ\csc^250^\circ\approx4.1244\). Notice this identity did not need either companion individually; it needed only the original identity, hiding inside a common denominator — a preview of exactly the one-side-at-a-time discipline Lesson 7-13 turns into its subject.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Writing \(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1\) as \(\sin\theta+\cos\theta=1\) by (incorrectly) taking a square root term-by-term. The Pythagorean identity squares each function before adding — \((\sin\theta)^2\), not \(\sin(\theta^2)\), and definitely not something you can un-square by splitting a sum. Test it: at \(\theta=45^\circ\), \(\sin45^\circ+\cos45^\circ=\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}+\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}=\sqrt2\approx1.41\), not \(1\) — the "simplified" version is simply false.
▶  Try it yourself: Given \(\sin\theta=-\dfrac{5}{13}\) with \(\theta\) in Quadrant IV, find \(\cos\theta\) and \(\tan\theta\).
\(\cos^2\theta=1-\dfrac{25}{169}=\dfrac{144}{169}\); Quadrant IV has cosine positive, so \(\cos\theta=\dfrac{12}{13}\). \(\tan\theta=\dfrac{-5/13}{12/13}=-\dfrac{5}{12}\).
▶  Try it yourself: Why must \(1+\cot^2\theta=\csc^2\theta\) fail at \(\theta=0\)?
Because this companion was produced by dividing through by \(\sin^2\theta\), a step only valid where \(\sin\theta\ne0\). At \(\theta=0\), \(\sin\theta=0\), so both \(\cot\theta\) and \(\csc\theta\) are undefined — the identity's domain excludes exactly the points its own derivation divided by zero to reach.

🔒 Self-Government: An Identity That Governs Itself

A trigonometric identity is not an equation to be solved for special values of \(\theta\) — it is a claim that holds everywhere it is defined, without exception, without a case where the mathematician gets to decide it applies. That is a small, exact model of this unit's virtue: self-government as a rule that binds uniformly, not selectively — the same discipline that made you check both SSA triangles in Lesson 7-10 rather than the convenient one, and the same discipline that will make Lesson 7-13's identity verifications refuse to assume the very thing they are trying to prove.

Conclusion

Every identity proven today — the original and its two companions — traces back to a single fact about the unit circle, \(x^2+y^2=1\), restated three ways. That is the first tool in what the next lessons build into a full identity toolbox: Lesson 7-13 turns “verify an identity” into a disciplined, one-side-at-a-time practice; Lesson 7-14 proves the sum and difference formulas, which let you find exact values like \(\cos15^\circ\) that no special-angle triangle alone could reach; and Lesson 7-16 builds the double-angle formulas directly out of those sum formulas. Every tool in that toolbox will lean on today's identities the way today's Law of Cosines leaned on them without pausing to say so — so hold onto \(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1\) and its two companions; you will reach for all three again and again from here to the end of the unit.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does \(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1\) require no case-by-case check across quadrants, when so many of this unit's other results have?

2. What single step turns the original Pythagorean identity into each of its two companions, and why does that step also explain each companion's domain restriction?

3. In Example 4, why was it enough to use the original identity rather than either companion individually?
