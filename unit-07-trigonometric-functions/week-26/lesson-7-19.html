🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to solve trigonometric equations that require an identity substitution before they can be solved at all.

  • I will be able to build a periodic real-world model from given data and solve it end to end — from raw description, to equation, to an honest, checked answer.

  • I will be able to recognize a disguised double-angle expression inside a model and reduce it before attempting to solve.

📚 VOCABULARY

Identity-assisted equation: A trigonometric equation that is not already in a directly solvable form — it contains a mixture of functions, or a disguised multiple angle, that must be reduced by an identity before the ordinary solving techniques of Lesson 7-8 apply.

General solution: The complete, infinite family of angles satisfying an equation, expressed with an arbitrary integer \(k\) (e.g., \(\theta = \frac{\pi}{6}+2\pi k\)) — as opposed to the finitely many solutions that happen to fall inside one stated interval.

Model closure: The final step of an applied problem, where a numeric answer is translated back into the original units and context, and checked for whether it is physically reasonable — a solved equation is not a finished answer until it has been closed this way.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In 1807, Joseph Fourier made a claim that struck many of his contemporaries as absurd: that any periodic phenomenon, however jagged or irregular it looks, can be built up out of nothing but sines and cosines, added together in the right proportions. The mathematicians who reviewed his work for the French Academy were not convinced — the claim seemed too strong, the proof too incomplete — and it took decades to vindicate him fully. But Fourier's instinct was sound, and it is the instinct this whole unit has been quietly training: a wave, a tide, a temperature, a rider's height on a wheel — whatever the phenomenon, if it repeats, sine and cosine are the honest language for describing it. Today, every tool this unit built — identities, graphs, equations — meets that instinct at once, in problems where solving "when does this equal that" requires reducing the model with an identity first.

Why Some Equations Need an Identity First

Lesson 7-8 solved equations already written in one function — \(\sin\theta = \frac12\), or a quadratic in \(\sin\theta\) alone. Today's equations often are not written that way on arrival: they mix \(\sin\theta\) with \(\cos2\theta\), or hide a double angle inside a sum of squares, or disguise a single cosine wave as a difference of two squared terms. Before any solving technique applies, the expression has to be rewritten in one function of one angle — and choosing which identity does that job is exactly the toolbox work of the last two lessons, now put to use.

Example 1 — An Equation That Needs an Identity

Solve \(\cos 2\theta = \sin\theta\) for \(\theta\in[0,2\pi)\).

Solution:

The equation mixes \(\cos2\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\) — not solvable directly. Substitute the double-angle identity \(\cos2\theta = 1-2\sin^2\theta\), turning everything into one function:

\(1-2\sin^2\theta = \sin\theta \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; 2\sin^2\theta+\sin\theta-1=0\)

This factors: \((2\sin\theta-1)(\sin\theta+1)=0\), so \(\sin\theta=\dfrac12\) or \(\sin\theta=-1\).

\(\sin\theta=\dfrac12 \Rightarrow \theta=\dfrac{\pi}{6},\dfrac{5\pi}{6} \qquad \sin\theta=-1 \Rightarrow \theta=\dfrac{3\pi}{2}\)

Verify: at \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{6}\): \(\cos\frac{\pi}{3}=0.5\) and \(\sin\frac{\pi}{6}=0.5\) — matches. At \(\theta=\frac{3\pi}{2}\): \(\cos3\pi=\cos\pi=-1\) and \(\sin\frac{3\pi}{2}=-1\) — matches.

Answer: \(\theta=\dfrac{\pi}{6},\ \dfrac{5\pi}{6},\ \dfrac{3\pi}{2}\).

Example 2 — Modeling End to End

A city's average daily temperature \(T\) (\(^\circ\)F), \(d\) days after January 1, reaches a high of \(84^\circ\) around day \(200\) and a low of \(28^\circ\) around day \(15\), swinging smoothly between them over the year (\(365\)-day period). Build the model, then find every \(d\) in \([0,365)\) at which \(T=70^\circ\).

Solution:

Build the model. Amplitude \(A=\dfrac{84-28}{2}=28\); midline \(D=\dfrac{84+28}{2}=56\); since the maximum falls at \(d=200\) (not \(d=0\)), a cosine shifted to peak there is the natural choice, with \(B=\dfrac{2\pi}{365}\):

\(T(d) = 28\cos\!\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{365}(d-200)\right) + 56\)

Solve. Set \(T(d)=70\):

\(28\cos\!\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{365}(d-200)\right) = 14 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \cos\!\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{365}(d-200)\right) = \dfrac12\)

\(\dfrac{2\pi}{365}(d-200) = \pm\dfrac{\pi}{3} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; d-200 = \pm\dfrac{365}{6} \approx \pm60.83\)

Answer: \(d\approx139.2\) or \(d\approx260.8\) — that is, around day \(139\) (mid-May) and day \(261\) (mid-September). Both are physically reasonable: the model crosses \(70^\circ\) once on its way up to the July peak, and once again on its way down — exactly the two crossings a real temperature curve should have, and exactly what "closing the model" means: the algebra handed back two numbers, and reading them against the calendar is what makes them an answer.

Example 3 — A Disguised Model

A buoy's height above mean sea level, in meters, \(t\) hours after noon, is modeled by \(H(t) = 3\sin^2\!\left(\dfrac{\pi t}{8}\right) - 3\cos^2\!\left(\dfrac{\pi t}{8}\right)\). Find every \(t\) in \([0,8)\) at which \(H(t)=1.5\).

Solution:

Reduce first. \(\sin^2\theta-\cos^2\theta = -(\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta) = -\cos2\theta\), with \(\theta=\dfrac{\pi t}{8}\):

\(H(t) = -3\cos\!\left(\dfrac{\pi t}{4}\right)\)

The raw formula looked like it needed a graphing calculator; reduced, it is a single cosine wave with amplitude \(3\) and period \(\dfrac{2\pi}{\pi/4}=8\) hours — consistent with the buoy completing one full rise-and-fall cycle every \(8\) hours.

Solve. \(-3\cos\left(\frac{\pi t}{4}\right)=1.5 \Rightarrow \cos\left(\frac{\pi t}{4}\right) = -\dfrac12 \Rightarrow \dfrac{\pi t}{4} = \dfrac{2\pi}{3}\text{ or }\dfrac{4\pi}{3}\ \text{(in }[0,2\pi))\)

\(t = \dfrac83 \approx 2.67 \text{ hr} \qquad t = \dfrac{16}{3} \approx 5.33 \text{ hr}\)

Answer: \(t\approx2.67\) hr and \(t\approx5.33\) hr after noon. Neither number was reachable without reducing the model first — the disguise was the entire difficulty.

💡 CHECK YOURSELF WITH DESMOS
In Desmos (desmos.com/calculator), graph the original, undisguised form of Example 3 — y = 3sin(πx/8)^2 - 3cos(πx/8)^2 — alongside the reduced form y = -3cos(πx/4). The two curves sit exactly on top of each other, confirming the reduction before you ever solve anything. Then add y = 1.5 as a third graph and read the intersection points directly off the screen — they should land within a hundredth of an hour of \(t=\frac83\) and \(t=\frac{16}{3}\).
▶  Try it yourself: Solve \(\sin 2\theta = \cos\theta\) for \(\theta\in[0,2\pi)\).
\(2\sin\theta\cos\theta=\cos\theta \Rightarrow \cos\theta(2\sin\theta-1)=0\). So \(\cos\theta=0 \Rightarrow \theta=\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{2}\), or \(\sin\theta=\frac12 \Rightarrow \theta=\frac{\pi}{6},\frac{5\pi}{6}\). Four solutions total: \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{6},\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{5\pi}{6},\frac{3\pi}{2}\).
▶  Try it yourself: A rider's height on a wheel is \(f(t) = 1-\cos\left(\frac{\pi t}{6}\right)+\sin^2\left(\frac{\pi t}{12}\right)\), where \(\frac{\pi t}{12}\) is half of \(\frac{\pi t}{6}\). Use a reduction identity to rewrite \(f(t)\) with a single argument, before attempting to solve anything.
With \(\theta=\frac{\pi t}{12}\), \(\sin^2\theta = \frac{1-\cos2\theta}{2} = \frac{1-\cos(\pi t/6)}{2}\). So \(f(t) = 1-\cos\left(\frac{\pi t}{6}\right) + \frac{1-\cos(\pi t/6)}{2} = \frac32 - \frac32\cos\left(\frac{\pi t}{6}\right)\) — a single cosine wave, midline \(1.5\), amplitude \(1.5\), ready to solve.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Dividing both sides of a trigonometric equation by a trig expression that could be zero — and silently losing the solutions that make it zero. Solving \(\sin2\theta=\sin\theta\) by dividing both sides by \(\sin\theta\) gives \(2\cos\theta=1\), or \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{3},\frac{5\pi}{3}\) — but this throws away every solution where \(\sin\theta=0\) in the first place. The disciplined path never divides by a variable expression: move everything to one side and factor instead. \(2\sin\theta\cos\theta-\sin\theta=0 \Rightarrow \sin\theta(2\cos\theta-1)=0\), giving \(\sin\theta=0\) (\(\theta=0,\pi\)) as well as \(\cos\theta=\frac12\) (\(\theta=\frac{\pi}{3},\frac{5\pi}{3}\)) — four solutions, not two. Dividing is never wrong when you first rule out the divisor being zero; skipping that check is where solutions quietly disappear.

Conclusion

Look back across the whole arc this unit walked. It began with a single coordinate riding around a circle, traced out one angle at a time until it became a wave — ordered, repeating, entirely predictable from one period to the next. It built a toolbox of identities proving that the wave's disguises are all the same wave underneath, governed by rules that never bend for convenience. And today it closed the loop: those same identities, applied with the same discipline, let you solve for exactly when a tide crests, a temperature crosses a threshold, a buoy returns to a given height. Self-government was never a slogan sitting beside this content — it was this content: a periodic function's whole claim to being useful is that it keeps its promises, cycle after cycle, and every identity you proved and every equation you solved this unit held itself to that same standard, cycle after cycle, step after step.

Thursday's test gathers this entire arc — graphs, inverses, models, the Laws of Sines and Cosines, and the identities — into one sitting. And beyond it, the circle you have spent a whole unit unrolling into a wave is about to become something else again: next unit, that same circle stops being a function's hidden source and becomes a coordinate system in its own right, with points named not by \((x,y)\) but by a distance and an angle. The wave came from the circle. Now watch the circle come back.
