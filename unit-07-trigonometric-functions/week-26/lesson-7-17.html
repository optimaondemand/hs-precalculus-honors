Half-Angle and Reduction Formulas
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to derive the half-angle formulas for sine, cosine, and tangent from the double-angle and Pythagorean identities.

  • I will be able to choose the correct sign for a half-angle value by locating the quadrant of the half-angle itself — never the quadrant of the original angle.

  • I will be able to apply the power-reducing (reduction) formulas to rewrite a squared trigonometric expression as a first-power expression in a multiple angle.

📚 VOCABULARY

Half-angle formula: A formula expressing \(\sin\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\), \(\cos\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\), or \(\tan\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\) exactly in terms of \(\cos\alpha\) — built by solving a double-angle identity backward, so that halving an angle costs nothing but a square root.

Reduction (power-reducing) formula: The same algebra as the half-angle formula, read in the opposite direction: an identity that rewrites \(\sin^2\theta\) or \(\cos^2\theta\) — a squared, first-degree-in-appearance-only expression — as a first-power expression in \(\cos 2\theta\).

Sign selection by quadrant: The discipline of resolving the \(\pm\) in the half-angle sine and cosine formulas by locating where \(\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\) itself terminates — not where \(\alpha\) terminates — since bisecting an angle can move its terminal ray into an entirely different quadrant.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Long before any calculator, the astronomer Ptolemy compiled a table of chords in the Almagest — the ancestor of a modern trig table — and he did not compute each entry from scratch. He built the table from a handful of chords he could construct geometrically, and then bisected his way to the rest: knowing the chord of an angle, he had a method for finding the chord of half that angle, and could climb down from large, easy angles to small, precise ones, one halving at a time. You are about to learn the modern descendant of that exact bisection trick — and you already own every ingredient it needs. Last lesson's double-angle formulas, read backward, are the whole recipe.

Deriving the Half-Angle Formulas

Start from the double-angle identity \(\cos 2\theta = 1 - 2\sin^2\theta\), and solve it for \(\sin^2\theta\):

\(\sin^2\theta = \dfrac{1-\cos 2\theta}{2}\)

Now let \(\theta = \dfrac{\alpha}{2}\), so that \(2\theta = \alpha\):

\(\sin^2\!\left(\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\right) = \dfrac{1-\cos\alpha}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sin\!\left(\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\right) = \pm\sqrt{\dfrac{1-\cos\alpha}{2}}\)

The same move, starting from \(\cos 2\theta = 2\cos^2\theta - 1\), gives the cosine half-angle formula:

\(\cos\!\left(\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\right) = \pm\sqrt{\dfrac{1+\cos\alpha}{2}}\)

Every entry on the right depends only on \(\alpha\) itself — a single known value, cosine of the whole angle, unlocks the half. The \(\pm\) is not carelessness; it is the one honest admission that a square root has two roots, and the next section is entirely about resolving it correctly.

The Tangent Half-Angle: No Sign to Choose

Dividing the two boxed formulas gives \(\tan\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right) = \pm\sqrt{\dfrac{1-\cos\alpha}{1+\cos\alpha}}\) — but multiply the numerator and denominator of \(\dfrac{\sin(\alpha/2)}{\cos(\alpha/2)}\) by \(2\cos(\alpha/2)\), and the square roots cancel entirely, leaving two equivalent forms with no ambiguity at all:

\(\tan\!\left(\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\right) = \dfrac{\sin\alpha}{1+\cos\alpha} = \dfrac{1-\cos\alpha}{\sin\alpha}\)

Because \(\sin\alpha\) already carries whatever sign it needs, these two forms hand you the correct sign of \(\tan(\alpha/2)\) automatically — the one half-angle value you never have to reason about by quadrant.

Example 1

Find the exact value of \(\cos 15^\circ\) using a half-angle formula.

Solution:

Since \(15^\circ = \dfrac{30^\circ}{2}\) and \(0^\circ < 15^\circ < 90^\circ\) (Quadrant I, cosine positive), take the \(+\) root:

\(\cos 15^\circ = \sqrt{\dfrac{1+\cos 30^\circ}{2}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{1+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}}{2}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2+\sqrt3}{4}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt3}}{2}\)

This radical simplifies to the more familiar form \(\dfrac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}\) (the two are algebraically equal — square both and you get the same \(\frac{2+\sqrt3}{4}\)).

Answer: \(\cos 15^\circ = \dfrac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4} \approx 0.9659\). Lesson 7-15's sum-formula toolbox reaches \(\cos(45^\circ-30^\circ)\) and lands on this exact same number — two different tools, one honest answer, which is itself a check worth running whenever two methods are available.

Example 2

Given \(\cos\alpha = -\dfrac{7}{25}\) with \(180^\circ < \alpha < 270^\circ\) (Quadrant III), find the exact values of \(\sin\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\), \(\cos\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\), and \(\tan\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\).

Solution:

Locate \(\alpha/2\) first — before computing anything. Halve every part of the inequality: \(90^\circ < \dfrac{\alpha}{2} < 135^\circ\). That places \(\alpha/2\) in Quadrant II, where sine is positive and cosine is negative. This is the step a rushed solver skips, and it is the whole game.

\(\sin\!\left(\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\right) = +\sqrt{\dfrac{1-\left(-\frac{7}{25}\right)}{2}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{32/25}{2}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{16}{25}} = \dfrac{4}{5}\)

\(\cos\!\left(\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\right) = -\sqrt{\dfrac{1+\left(-\frac{7}{25}\right)}{2}} = -\sqrt{\dfrac{18/25}{2}} = -\sqrt{\dfrac{9}{25}} = -\dfrac{3}{5}\)

\(\tan\!\left(\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\right) = \dfrac{\sin(\alpha/2)}{\cos(\alpha/2)} = \dfrac{4/5}{-3/5} = -\dfrac{4}{3}\)

Verify: in Quadrant III, \(\sin\alpha = -\dfrac{24}{25}\) (from \(\sin^2\alpha = 1-\frac{49}{625}=\frac{576}{625}\), negative in QIII). The sign-free formula gives \(\tan(\alpha/2) = \dfrac{\sin\alpha}{1+\cos\alpha} = \dfrac{-24/25}{18/25} = -\dfrac{24}{18} = -\dfrac{4}{3}\) — matches, and it never needed a quadrant decision at all.

Answer: \(\sin(\alpha/2)=\dfrac{4}{5}\), \(\cos(\alpha/2)=-\dfrac{3}{5}\), \(\tan(\alpha/2)=-\dfrac{4}{3}\).

Reduction Formulas: The Same Identity, Read Forward

The reduction (power-reducing) formulas are not new facts — they are the double-angle identities you already proved, solved for the squared function instead of substituted for the half-angle:

\(\sin^2\theta = \dfrac{1-\cos 2\theta}{2} \qquad \cos^2\theta = \dfrac{1+\cos 2\theta}{2} \qquad \tan^2\theta = \dfrac{1-\cos 2\theta}{1+\cos 2\theta}\)

Where the half-angle formula trades a known whole angle for an unknown half (paying with a square root and a quadrant decision), the reduction formula trades a squared function of \(\theta\) for a first-power function of \(2\theta\) — no square root, no sign to choose, because nothing is being un-squared.

Example 3

Use a reduction formula to simplify \(4\sin^2 x - 2\) to a single first-power expression.

Solution:

\(4\sin^2x - 2 = 4\left(\dfrac{1-\cos 2x}{2}\right) - 2 = 2(1-\cos 2x) - 2 = 2 - 2\cos 2x - 2 = -2\cos 2x\)

Answer: \(4\sin^2x - 2 = -2\cos 2x\). Verify at \(x=30^\circ\): left side \(=4(0.5)^2-2 = 1-2=-1\); right side \(=-2\cos 60^\circ = -2(0.5)=-1\). Matches.

Example 4

Find the exact value of \(\sin^2 75^\circ\) using a reduction formula.

Solution:

\(\sin^2 75^\circ = \dfrac{1-\cos 150^\circ}{2} = \dfrac{1-\left(-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)}{2} = \dfrac{1+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}}{2} = \dfrac{2+\sqrt3}{4}\)

Answer: \(\sin^2 75^\circ = \dfrac{2+\sqrt3}{4} \approx 0.9330\) — and indeed \((0.9659)^2 \approx 0.9330\), matching \(\sin 75^\circ \approx 0.9659\) directly.

💡 CHECK YOURSELF WITH DESMOS
In Desmos (desmos.com/calculator), graph y = sin(x)^2 and y = (1-cos(2x))/2 on the same axes. The two curves sit exactly on top of each other everywhere — a visual proof of the reduction formula running the whole real line at once, not just at one angle. Then graph y = cos(x/2) next to y = sqrt((1+cos(x))/2): watch the two agree only where \(\cos(x/2)\ge 0\), and visibly disagree where it goes negative — the graph itself is showing you, without a word, exactly where the \(+\) root stops being honest.
▶  Try it yourself: Find the exact value of \(\sin 22.5^\circ\) using a half-angle formula.
\(22.5^\circ = \frac{45^\circ}{2}\), Quadrant I (sine positive): \(\sin 22.5^\circ = \sqrt{\dfrac{1-\cos 45^\circ}{2}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{1-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}}{2}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2-\sqrt2}{4}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{2-\sqrt2}}{2} \approx 0.3827\).
▶  Try it yourself: Given \(\sin\alpha = -\frac{3}{5}\) with \(270^\circ < \alpha < 360^\circ\) (Quadrant IV), find \(\cos\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\).
First, \(\cos\alpha = +\frac{4}{5}\) (QIV, cosine positive). Halving the inequality: \(135^\circ < \frac{\alpha}{2} < 180^\circ\), Quadrant II — cosine of the half-angle is negative. \(\cos(\alpha/2) = -\sqrt{\dfrac{1+4/5}{2}} = -\sqrt{\dfrac{9}{10}} = -\dfrac{3\sqrt{10}}{10} \approx -0.9487\).
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Choosing the \(\pm\) sign in a half-angle formula from the quadrant of \(\alpha\) instead of the quadrant of \(\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\). In Example 2, \(\alpha\) sits in Quadrant III (where cosine is negative) — but \(\alpha/2\) sits in Quadrant II, where cosine is also negative, so the mistake happens to look harmless there. It is not harmless in general: halving an angle's inequality can land it in a completely different sign pattern than the original angle carried, and the only way to know is to do the halving, every time, before writing a single square root.

Conclusion

You have now derived every family this course will hand you: the Pythagorean identities, the sum and difference formulas, the double-angle formulas, and today, half-angle and reduction formulas built from the same double-angle algebra read in two directions. Self-government is exactly this: not skipping the quadrant check because a formula "usually" works, but running the same disciplined halving — of the angle, of the inequality, of the reasoning — every single time, whether or not it feels necessary. Next lesson, all four families go into one toolbox together, and the real skill begins: not deriving an identity, but recognizing which one a hard expression is quietly asking for.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does the tangent half-angle formula \(\tan(\alpha/2) = \dfrac{\sin\alpha}{1+\cos\alpha}\) carry no \(\pm\), while the sine and cosine half-angle formulas both do?

2. Why must the sign of a half-angle value be chosen from the quadrant of \(\alpha/2\), never from the quadrant of \(\alpha\) itself — and can you describe a case where the two quadrants would give different signs?

3. In what sense are the half-angle formulas and the reduction formulas "the same identity, read in two directions"? What does each direction cost you (a square root and a sign decision, versus nothing)?

4. The reduction formula lowers \(\sin^2\theta\) to a first power of \(\cos 2\theta\). Could the same technique, applied twice, lower \(\sin^4\theta\) to first powers of \(\cos 2\theta\) and \(\cos 4\theta\)? What would the first step look like?
