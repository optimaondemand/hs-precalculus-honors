The Identity Toolbox (Honors Capstone)
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to select strategically among the Pythagorean, sum/difference, double-angle, and half-angle/reduction identities to simplify a hard trigonometric expression.

  • I will be able to write a clean identity proof — working from one side toward the other, never assuming the equation I am trying to establish.

  • I will be able to judge a claimed simplification — my own, a classmate's, or an AI's — for whether it actually used a valid identity, and whether it chose the efficient path.

📚 VOCABULARY

Identity toolbox: The complete set of tools this unit has built — the Pythagorean identities, the sum and difference formulas, the double-angle formulas, and the half-angle/reduction formulas — considered together as a set from which a solver must choose, rather than a list to apply in a fixed order.

Disciplined proof: An identity proof that begins from one side of the claimed equation and transforms it, step by valid step, until it matches the other side — never manipulating both sides at once, and never writing down the equation being proved as if it were already established.

Strategic simplification: The skill of recognizing, before committing to a method, which identity turns a hard expression simple — as opposed to which identity happens to be the first one that comes to mind.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Euclid's Elements is built on a rule so simple it is easy to miss how strict it is: a proof may use only what has already been established — the axioms, and the theorems proved before it — and it may never, at any step, quietly assume the very claim it is trying to reach. Historians of mathematics have long pointed to this discipline, not the individual theorems, as Euclid's deepest legacy: a proof is not a proof because its conclusion is true, but because every step follows necessarily from what came before. Today's lesson asks you to hold your own trigonometric arguments to that same standard. You now own four families of identities. The honors work is no longer deriving them — it is choosing, with self-government, which one a hard expression is quietly asking for, and refusing to write down anything you have not actually earned.

The Toolbox, Named

Four families, four different jobs:

Pythagorean (\(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1\) and its two companions) — trades one squared function for another; the tool of first resort whenever an expression mixes \(\sin^2\) and \(\cos^2\).

Sum/difference (\(\sin(A\pm B)\), \(\cos(A\pm B)\)) — splits an angle that is a sum into pieces you already know, or — read backward — recognizes a sum-or-difference pattern hiding inside an expression that never mentions a sum at all.

Double-angle — connects \(\theta\) to \(2\theta\); useful whenever an expression contains both an angle and its double.

Half-angle / reduction — connects \(\theta\) to \(2\theta\) in the other direction; useful whenever an expression is squared and a first-power form would simplify things, or an angle is literally half of one you know.

Example 1 — Choosing the Efficient Path

Simplify \(\dfrac{\sin 3\theta}{\sin\theta} - \dfrac{\cos 3\theta}{\cos\theta}\).

Solution:

A first instinct reaches for the triple-angle expressions \(\sin 3\theta\) and \(\cos 3\theta\) — each of which would first have to be derived from \(\sin(2\theta+\theta)\) using the sum formula and the double-angle formula together, expanded, and only then substituted in. That path exists, and it works, but it is three formulas deep before the expression is even combined. Look again before committing to it.

Combine the two fractions over the common denominator \(\sin\theta\cos\theta\):

\(\dfrac{\sin 3\theta\cos\theta - \cos 3\theta\sin\theta}{\sin\theta\cos\theta}\)

The numerator is not a random combination — it is exactly the pattern \(\sin A\cos B - \cos A\sin B = \sin(A-B)\), with \(A=3\theta\) and \(B=\theta\), read backward. No triple-angle expansion is needed at all:

\(\sin 3\theta\cos\theta - \cos 3\theta\sin\theta = \sin(3\theta-\theta) = \sin 2\theta\)

So the whole expression collapses to \(\dfrac{\sin 2\theta}{\sin\theta\cos\theta}\). One more identity — the double-angle formula \(\sin 2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta\) — finishes it:

\(\dfrac{2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{\sin\theta\cos\theta} = 2\)

Verify: at \(\theta=20^\circ\), \(\dfrac{\sin60^\circ}{\sin20^\circ}-\dfrac{\cos60^\circ}{\cos20^\circ} \approx \dfrac{0.86603}{0.34202}-\dfrac{0.5}{0.93969} \approx 2.53184 - 0.53209 \approx 2.00\).

Answer: the expression simplifies to the constant \(2\), for every \(\theta\) where it is defined — and the entire simplification cost two identities, not three formulas' worth of triple-angle expansion. That choice, made before the first line was written, is the skill this lesson is actually teaching.

Example 2 — A Disciplined Proof

Prove the identity \(\dfrac{\cos 2x}{1+\sin 2x} = \dfrac{\cos x-\sin x}{\cos x+\sin x}\).

Solution:

Work from the right side only — the left side is not touched until the two sides meet. Multiply the right side by \(\dfrac{\cos x-\sin x}{\cos x-\sin x}\) (a legal move: this is \(1\)):

\(\dfrac{\cos x-\sin x}{\cos x+\sin x}\cdot\dfrac{\cos x-\sin x}{\cos x-\sin x} = \dfrac{\cos^2x - 2\sin x\cos x+\sin^2x}{\cos^2x-\sin^2x}\)

The numerator's outer terms are exactly \(\sin^2x+\cos^2x\), which the Pythagorean identity replaces with \(1\); the denominator is exactly the double-angle form \(\cos^2x-\sin^2x=\cos2x\); and the middle numerator term is \(2\sin x\cos x=\sin2x\):

\(= \dfrac{1-\sin 2x}{\cos 2x}\)

This is not yet the left side — but multiply again, this time by \(\dfrac{1+\sin2x}{1+\sin2x}\) (again legal, again equal to \(1\)):

\(\dfrac{1-\sin2x}{\cos2x}\cdot\dfrac{1+\sin2x}{1+\sin2x} = \dfrac{1-\sin^22x}{\cos2x(1+\sin2x)} = \dfrac{\cos^22x}{\cos2x(1+\sin2x)} = \dfrac{\cos2x}{1+\sin2x}\)

Verify: at \(x=20^\circ\), left side \(=\dfrac{\cos40^\circ}{1+\sin40^\circ}\approx\dfrac{0.76604}{1.64279}\approx0.46631\); right side \(=\dfrac{\cos20^\circ-\sin20^\circ}{\cos20^\circ+\sin20^\circ}\approx\dfrac{0.59767}{1.28171}\approx0.46630\). Matches.

Answer: the identity holds. Notice what made this a proof and not a guess: every line followed from the one before it by a named, legal move — multiplying by a form of \(1\), substituting the Pythagorean identity, substituting the double-angle identity — and the left side was never touched until the chain of equalities reached it on its own.

Example 3 — A Third Combination

Simplify \(\cot x + \tan x\).

Solution:

\(\cot x+\tan x = \dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}+\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x} = \dfrac{\cos^2x+\sin^2x}{\sin x\cos x} = \dfrac{1}{\sin x\cos x}\)

The numerator collapsed by the Pythagorean identity. Now the double-angle identity \(\sin2x=2\sin x\cos x\) rewrites the denominator: \(\sin x\cos x = \dfrac{\sin2x}{2}\), so:

\(\dfrac{1}{\sin x\cos x} = \dfrac{2}{\sin2x} = 2\csc2x\)

Answer: \(\cot x+\tan x = 2\csc2x\). Verify at \(x=30^\circ\): left side \(=\cot30^\circ+\tan30^\circ = \sqrt3+\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\approx1.7321+0.5774\approx2.3094\); right side \(=2\csc60^\circ = \dfrac{2}{\sin60^\circ}\approx\dfrac{2}{0.86603}\approx2.3094\). Matches — one Pythagorean substitution, one double-angle substitution, and a two-function sum became a single reciprocal function.

⚠️ AI CHECKPOINT: THE SIGN THAT FOLLOWED THE WRONG QUADRANT

A student asked an AI: “Given \(\sin\alpha = \frac{4}{5}\) with \(\alpha\) in Quadrant II, find the exact value of \(\cos\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\).” It answered:

“Since \(\alpha\) is in Quadrant II, \(\cos\alpha = -\frac{3}{5}\). Then \(\cos\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right) = \pm\sqrt{\frac{1+\cos\alpha}{2}} = \pm\sqrt{\frac{1}{5}} = \pm\frac{\sqrt5}{5}\). Since \(\cos\alpha\) is negative, \(\cos\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\) must also be negative: \(\cos\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right) = -\frac{\sqrt5}{5} \approx -0.4472\).”

The first line is correct — \(\cos\alpha=-\frac35\) is exactly right for Quadrant II, and the half-angle radical \(\pm\frac{\sqrt5}{5}\) is computed correctly too. The error is the sentence that resolves the sign: the AI reused the sign of \(\cos\alpha\) instead of finding the quadrant of \(\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\) — precisely the shortcut 7-17's common-mistake box warned against, and precisely as tempting as it looks here.

Locate \(\alpha/2\) properly. Quadrant II means \(90^\circ<\alpha<180^\circ\); halving gives \(45^\circ<\dfrac{\alpha}{2}<90^\circ\) — Quadrant I, not Quadrant II, and cosine is positive in Quadrant I.

The corrected value is \(\cos\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right) = +\dfrac{\sqrt5}{5}\approx0.4472\). Sanity check: with \(\sin\alpha=0.8\) in QII, \(\alpha\approx126.87^\circ\), so \(\alpha/2\approx63.44^\circ\) — a first-quadrant angle, whose cosine cannot be negative. An AI's algebra can be flawless right up to the one sentence that matters; the discipline this whole unit has built — find the quadrant of the half-angle itself, every time — is exactly what catches it.

▶  Try it yourself: Simplify \(\dfrac{1-\cos 2x}{\sin 2x}\), naming the identities you used.
Double-angle: \(1-\cos2x = 2\sin^2x\) and \(\sin2x=2\sin x\cos x\), so the ratio is \(\dfrac{2\sin^2x}{2\sin x\cos x} = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x} = \tan x\).
▶  Try it yourself: Prove \(\sin^4x - \cos^4x = 1 - 2\cos^2x\), stating which identity does the work.
Factor the left side as a difference of squares: \(\sin^4x-\cos^4x = (\sin^2x-\cos^2x)(\sin^2x+\cos^2x)\). The Pythagorean identity collapses the second factor to \(1\), leaving \(\sin^2x-\cos^2x\). Substitute \(\sin^2x = 1-\cos^2x\) (Pythagorean again): \(1-\cos^2x-\cos^2x = 1-2\cos^2x\), matching the right side exactly.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Writing \(\sin(A-B) = \sin A - \sin B\), or \(\cos(A+B)=\cos A+\cos B\) — treating sine and cosine as if they distributed over addition the way multiplication distributes over a sum. Neither function does this: \(\sin(A-B)=\sin A\cos B-\cos A\sin B\) and \(\cos(A+B)=\cos A\cos B-\sin A\sin B\) are the actual rules, and skipping straight to the "obvious-looking" shortcut produces an expression that is wrong at nearly every angle. Test it once, at \(A=90^\circ,B=90^\circ\): \(\sin(A-B)=\sin0^\circ=0\), but \(\sin A-\sin B = 1-1=0\) too — a coincidence that hides the error at this one pair. Try \(A=90^\circ,B=0^\circ\) instead: \(\sin(A-B)=\sin90^\circ=1\), while \(\sin A-\sin B=1-0=1\) — still hides it. Try \(A=60^\circ,B=30^\circ\): \(\sin(30^\circ)=0.5\), but \(\sin60^\circ-\sin30^\circ\approx0.866-0.5=0.366\) — the shortcut is exposed the moment the angles are not special partners.

Conclusion

Every example today reused the same two-part discipline: recognize which identity an expression is quietly built from, and then transform one side, step by named step, until the two sides meet honestly — never assuming, along the way, the very claim under proof. That is self-government applied to algebra: governing your own reasoning by a standard that does not bend, whether the expression is easy or the AI's answer looks confident. Tomorrow, that same toolbox goes to work solving equations — where an identity substitution is not decoration but the only way in, and where the unit's whole arc, from a single point traveling around a circle to a fully governed argument, closes.
