Modeling Tides, Daylight, and Wheels
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to build a sinusoidal model directly from recorded data — a short table of measurements, not a tidy handed-to-me maximum and minimum.

  • I will be able to interpret every parameter of a sinusoidal model — amplitude, midline, period, and horizontal shift — in the language of the real situation it describes.

  • I will be able to recognize the domain of honesty of a periodic model — the range of inputs over which the model can be trusted, and the point past which extending it becomes a claim the data never supported.

📚 VOCABULARY

Sinusoidal model: A function of the form \(y = A\cos\big(B(x-C)\big) + D\) (or the sine version) fit to real, recorded data rather than to a clean, invented maximum and minimum — every parameter answers a specific question about the phenomenon it describes.

Domain of honesty: The range of inputs over which a model's predictions can actually be trusted, because the underlying mechanism that produced the data continues to behave the way the model assumes. Beyond that range, the model may still compute an answer — but the answer is no longer earned by evidence.

Idealized cycle: A simplified, perfectly regular version of a real periodic process, used to teach or estimate. Real tides, unlike daylight hours, are shaped by several overlapping cycles at once and are only approximately sinusoidal — "idealized" is the word that keeps that approximation honest.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Lesson 7-6 gave you the method: identify the amplitude, the midline, the period, and the shift, and a periodic phenomenon becomes a function you can compute with. But 7-6's numbers arrived clean — a maximum and a minimum, handed to you, already labeled. Real measurements are rarely so polite. A tide gauge does not announce "this is the maximum"; it just keeps recording a number every few minutes, and it is your job to notice the pattern inside the noise. Today's lesson is the same method, applied to data that looks like what a scientist, a sailor, or a farmer's almanac actually receives — and it asks a second question 7-6 never had to ask: once you have a model, how far do you trust it?

Consider first the most reliable periodic motion most people ever depend on without thinking about it: the length of daylight across a year. At a mid-latitude city — call it roughly \(40^\circ\) north, a common latitude across the middle of the United States and much of Europe — daylight reaches its longest around day \(172\) of the year (the June solstice), at about \(15.0\) hours, and its shortest around day \(355\) (the December solstice), at about \(9.3\) hours. Notice this is exactly the shape of 7-6's Ferris wheel, dressed in different units: a maximum, a minimum, and a period, this time measured in days instead of minutes.

Reading Parameters Off a Table, Not Off a Handout

When a maximum and minimum are simply given to you, building the model is arithmetic. When they must be found inside a table or a graph of real measurements, the work happens first: scan the data for its highest and lowest recorded values and the inputs where they occur, then apply the same four questions as always — how far above and below the midline does the data swing (amplitude), where does it center (midline), how long until the pattern repeats (period, which gives \(B\)), and where does the peak actually fall (the shift, \(C\))? The formulas do not change. What changes is that you must find your own maximum and minimum first, and be honest about how confidently the data actually supports the values you read off it.

For the daylight data above: amplitude \(A = \dfrac{15.0 - 9.3}{2} = 2.85\); midline \(D = \dfrac{15.0 + 9.3}{2} = 12.15\); period \(365\) days, so \(B = \dfrac{2\pi}{365}\); and since the maximum falls at day \(172\), the shift is \(C = 172\). Because the maximum sits at the peak of a cosine curve, the model is:

\(H(t) = 2.85\cos\!\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{365}(t-172)\right) + 12.15\)

where \(t\) is the day of the year. Check it the way any model must be checked, against its own construction: \(H(172) = 2.85\cos(0) + 12.15 = 2.85(1)+12.15 = 15.0\) — matches. And a cosine model's minimum must fall exactly half a period away from its maximum: \(172 - \tfrac{365}{2} = 172 - 182.5 = -10.5\), which is coterminal with \(-10.5 + 365 = 354.5\), essentially day \(355\) — matches the recorded minimum almost exactly. A model that cannot verify its own symmetry has no business being trusted with a prediction; this one can, and does.

📊 In Excel: Fitting a Model to a Data Table 12 min

Build a small daylight-hours table — day of year in column A, recorded (or, here, model-generated) daylight hours in column B:

Day \(1\): \(\approx 9.4\) h · Day \(90\): \(\approx 12.6\) h · Day \(172\): \(15.0\) h · Day \(265\): \(\approx 12.1\) h · Day \(355\): \(9.3\) h.

In column C, build the model formula and copy it down every row: =2.85*COS(RADIANS(360*(A2-172)/365))+12.15. Notice the trick inside it — Excel's COS function expects radians, so the fraction of a full \(360^\circ\) turn, 360*(A2-172)/365, is wrapped in RADIANS() before it ever reaches COS. That one formula is doing the same job as \(\dfrac{2\pi}{365}(t-172)\) — just written the way a spreadsheet expects it.

Column C should reproduce column B almost exactly at every row — that agreement is the fit. A model that only matches at the two points you built it from and drifts everywhere else has not actually captured the pattern; a model that matches a handful of independent data points you did not use to build it has earned some trust. Now turn to a second periodic phenomenon with a very different personality: the tide. Real ocean tides are driven by the overlapping pulls of the moon and the sun at once, so an actual tide curve is only approximately sinusoidal — close enough to model this way for a first pass, not close enough to trust to the decimal the way daylight hours can be trusted. For today's work, treat the tide as an idealized \(12\)-hour cycle: a harbor's high tide of \(9\) ft occurs at midnight (\(t=0\) hours), and its low tide of \(1\) ft occurs \(6\) hours later, at \(6\!:\!00\) AM (\(t=6\)). Amplitude \(A = \dfrac{9-1}{2} = 4\); midline \(D = \dfrac{9+1}{2} = 5\); period \(12\) hours, so \(B = \dfrac{2\pi}{12} = \dfrac{\pi}{6}\); and since the maximum falls at \(t=0\), the shift is \(C=0\):

\(h(t) = 4\cos\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{6}t\right) + 5\)

Check: \(h(0) = 4\cos(0)+5 = 4(1)+5 = 9\) — matches the high tide. \(h(6) = 4\cos(\pi)+5 = 4(-1)+5 = 1\) — matches the low tide.

🔒 SELF-GOVERNMENT: A SCHEDULE YOU CAN BUILD A LIFE AROUND
A sundial keeps no calendar and a tide keeps no clock, yet both return to the same value on the same disciplined schedule every single cycle, without exception, without negotiation. That is self-government in its purest physical form — motion that submits to an order it did not choose and, in submitting, becomes something a navigator can chart a course by and a farmer can plant a season around. The whole reason a tide table or an almanac is worth publishing a year in advance is that the cycle it describes governs itself with a discipline no human institution could improve on.

Example 1

Using the daylight model \(H(t) = 2.85\cos\!\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{365}(t-172)\right) + 12.15\), find the number of daylight hours on day \(45\) (mid-February).

Solution:

\(H(45) = 2.85\cos\!\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{365}(45-172)\right) + 12.15 = 2.85\cos\!\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{365}(-127)\right) + 12.15\)

\(\dfrac{2\pi}{365}(-127) \approx -2.186 \text{ rad} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \cos(-2.186) \approx -0.5828\)

\(H(45) \approx 2.85(-0.5828) + 12.15 \approx -1.661 + 12.15 \approx 10.49\)

Answer: About \(10.5\) hours of daylight — reasonable for mid-February, still well below the \(12.15\)-hour midline and climbing toward the spring equinox.

Example 2

Using the idealized tide model \(h(t) = 4\cos\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{6}t\right) + 5\), find the tide height at \(9\!:\!00\) AM (\(t=9\)).

Solution:

\(h(9) = 4\cos\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{6}(9)\right) + 5 = 4\cos\!\left(\dfrac{3\pi}{2}\right) + 5 = 4(0) + 5 = 5\)

Answer: \(5\) ft — exactly the midline, which makes sense: \(9\!:\!00\) AM is \(3\) hours after the low tide at \(6\!:\!00\) AM, a quarter of the way through the next half-cycle back up to high tide, and a quarter-cycle point on a cosine curve always lands exactly on the midline.

Example 3

Using the same tide model, find every time \(t\) in one \(12\)-hour cycle, \(0 \le t < 12\), when the tide height equals \(7\) ft.

Solution:

\(4\cos\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{6}t\right) + 5 = 7 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \cos\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{6}t\right) = 0.5\)

Cosine equals \(0.5\) at \(\dfrac{\pi}{3}\) and at \(-\dfrac{\pi}{3}\) (equivalently \(2\pi - \dfrac{\pi}{3} = \dfrac{5\pi}{3}\)) in each cycle, so:

\(\dfrac{\pi}{6}t = \dfrac{\pi}{3} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; t = 2 \qquad\qquad \dfrac{\pi}{6}t = \dfrac{5\pi}{3} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; t = 10\)

Verify: \(h(2) = 4\cos\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{3}\right)+5 = 4(0.5)+5 = 7\) — matches. \(h(10) = 4\cos\!\left(\tfrac{5\pi}{3}\right)+5 = 4(0.5)+5=7\) — matches (since \(\cos\tfrac{5\pi}{3} = \cos\!\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{3}\right) = 0.5\)).

Answer: \(t = 2\) and \(t = 10\) — that is, \(2\!:\!00\) AM and \(10\!:\!00\) AM. Notice what you just did without a name for it: you took a sinusoidal equation, not just an evaluation, and solved it for every input that satisfies it. The next lesson gives that exact move its own name and its own general method.

Example 4 — Return to the Ferris Wheel

Recall Lesson 7-6's Ferris wheel: diameter \(40\) m, boarding height \(2\) m above the ground, one revolution every \(6\) minutes, giving the model \(h(t) = -20\cos\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{3}t\right) + 22\) (amplitude \(20\), midline \(22\), period \(6\), maximum at half the period since the ride starts at its lowest point). Find the rider's height at \(t = 1.5\) minutes.

Solution:

\(h(1.5) = -20\cos\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{3}(1.5)\right) + 22 = -20\cos\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right) + 22 = -20(0) + 22 = 22\)

Answer: \(22\) m — exactly the midline, once again because a quarter-cycle point on a cosine curve always sits on the midline. Three completely different physical situations — daylight, tide, Ferris wheel — and the same handful of algebraic facts about cosine govern every one of them.

The Domain of Honesty

Every model this course has built — a rational function's asymptote, an exponential's growth rate, and now a sinusoid's cycle — has come with a domain of honesty: the range of inputs over which the model's assumptions actually still hold. The daylight model \(H(t)\) is honest for interpreting one ordinary, non-leap year at that specific \(40^\circ\)-north latitude. It becomes dishonest the moment it is stretched past what it can actually support: applied without adjustment to a leap year (which shifts every date by a day), applied to a city at a very different latitude (the amplitude itself depends on latitude), or worse, mistaken for a weather forecast — daylight length is governed by orbital geometry and is essentially perfectly regular, but weather is governed by the atmosphere and is not remotely as regular. A model's honesty is not a permanent property; it is a claim about a specific range of situations, and part of using a model well is being able to say exactly where that claim stops.

▶  Try it yourself: Using \(H(t) = 2.85\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{365}(t-172)\right)+12.15\), find the daylight hours on day 300 (late October).
\(H(300) = 2.85\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{365}(128)\right)+12.15\). \(\frac{2\pi}{365}(128) \approx 2.203\) rad, \(\cos(2.203)\approx -0.5966\), so \(H(300) \approx 2.85(-0.5966)+12.15 \approx -1.700+12.15 \approx 10.45\) hours — reasonable for late October, below the midline and still falling toward the December minimum.
▶  Try it yourself: Using the idealized tide model \(h(t) = 4\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{6}t\right)+5\), find the tide height at \(t = 3\) (3:00 AM) and state, without recomputing, why this value could have been predicted from the pattern in Example 3.
\(h(3) = 4\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)+5 = 4(0)+5 = 5\) ft. This is the midline again, for the same reason as Example 2: \(t=3\) is a quarter-cycle after the maximum at \(t=0\), and every quarter-cycle point on a cosine curve lands on the midline — no new computation needed once that pattern is recognized.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Assuming that anything periodic-looking is automatically sinusoidal. Daylight hours genuinely are extremely close to a pure cosine curve, because the geometry that produces them — Earth's smooth, regular orbit and axial tilt — is itself smooth and regular. Real ocean tides are not: they are the sum of several overlapping cycles (the moon's pull, the sun's pull, and the shape of the particular coastline) layered on top of each other, so an actual tide curve only resembles a single cosine wave — closely enough to be useful, not closely enough to trust past the word "idealized." Before fitting a sinusoidal model to any data, ask what mechanism produced it, and whether that mechanism has any reason to behave as cleanly as a single cosine curve does.

Conclusion

Lesson 7-6 taught you to recognize which function shape a periodic phenomenon calls for and to build it from a clean maximum and minimum. Today you did the harder, more honest version of that same work — reading a model's parameters out of a data table, checking a model against its own symmetry, and naming exactly where a model's honesty runs out. Along the way, in Example 3, you solved a genuine trigonometric equation — \(4\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{6}t\right)+5=7\) — without yet having a general method for it. Lesson 7-8 supplies that method: solving trigonometric equations, linear and quadratic in form, exactly and in general, on any interval you are given.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why must you locate the maximum and minimum inside a data table yourself before you can build a model, when 7-6's numbers were simply handed to you — and what could go wrong if you misidentify either one?

2. What does it mean for a model to have a "domain of honesty," and what specifically makes the daylight model dishonest if stretched to a different latitude or used to forecast weather?

3. Why are daylight hours much closer to genuinely sinusoidal than real ocean tides are, even though both are commonly modeled with a cosine curve?

4. In what sense is the reliability of a tide cycle or a daylight cycle an example of this unit's virtue of self-government?
