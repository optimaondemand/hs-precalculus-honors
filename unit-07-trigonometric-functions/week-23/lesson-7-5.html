🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to explain why sine, cosine, and tangent must have their domains restricted before an inverse function can be defined, using the horizontal line test.

  • I will be able to define \(\arcsin\), \(\arccos\), and \(\arctan\) by their restricted domains and their ranges, and evaluate each exactly for familiar special-angle values.

  • I will be able to evaluate composite expressions like \(\cos(\arcsin x)\) using a reference right triangle, and evaluate any inverse trig expression using technology.

📚 VOCABULARY

Restricted domain: A deliberately narrowed input set on which a function that is not one-to-one over its full domain becomes one-to-one — and therefore invertible. The restriction is not a loophole; it is the discipline that makes an inverse trustworthy.

Principal value: The single output an inverse trigonometric function is required to return — the one value, inside the restricted range, that an infinite family of coterminal and reference-angle solutions all collapse down to.

Arcsine, arccosine, arctangent (written \(\arcsin x\), \(\arccos x\), \(\arctan x\), or equivalently \(\sin^{-1}x\), \(\cos^{-1}x\), \(\tan^{-1}x\)): the inverse functions of sine, cosine, and tangent restricted to their principal domains. Warning: \(\sin^{-1}x\) is notation for “the angle whose sine is \(x\)” — it is not \(\dfrac{1}{\sin x}\), which is instead written \(\csc x\). This is such a common point of confusion that many textbooks, and Desmos itself, prefer writing out arcsin to keep the two ideas visibly separate.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Several months ago, in Unit 4's lesson on restricting the domain, you learned a fact that felt almost like a loophole: a function that is not one-to-one — and therefore has no inverse over its full domain — can sometimes be made invertible by narrowing the domain down to a piece on which it passes the horizontal line test. That lesson closed with a promise: the payoff was coming, and it would arrive in trigonometry. This is that payoff, worked in full.

Watch the promise break down first, honestly. Graph \(y = \sin x\) over all real numbers and draw a horizontal line at \(y = 0\). It crosses the graph not once but infinitely often — at \(x = 0\), at \(x = \pi\), at \(x = 2\pi\), at \(x = -\pi\), and on forever, since \(\sin 0 = \sin \pi = 0\). Two different inputs, one shared output, repeated without end — sine fails the horizontal line test everywhere, and that failure is not a defect. It is the direct fingerprint of this unit's virtue: sine is periodic, an ordered, disciplined, endlessly repeating motion, and a function that repeats its outputs forever cannot, as a whole, be run backward. Ask “which angle has sine \(\frac{1}{2}\)?” of the entire sine function, and the honest answer is “infinitely many — which one do you mean?”

Unit 4's fix applies exactly here. Restrict sine's domain to the interval \(\left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]\) and something changes: on that interval, and only that interval, sine climbs the whole way from \(-1\) up to \(1\) without ever repeating a value — strictly increasing, one input for every output, passing the horizontal line test with no exceptions. That restricted, well-behaved piece of sine is invertible, and its inverse is what this lesson calls arcsine, written \(\arcsin x\) or \(\sin^{-1}x\). Because the restricted sine has domain \(\left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]\) and range \([-1,1]\), the inverse simply swaps those: \(\arcsin x\) has domain \([-1,1]\) and range \(\left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]\). Every time you evaluate \(\arcsin\) of anything, the function is bound by a kind of self-government — it has submitted to that restricted range so that it can be trusted to hand back exactly one angle, never a family of infinitely many.

Cosine and tangent submit to their own restrictions, chosen for the identical reason — monotonic, one-to-one, no repeats. Restrict cosine to \([0, \pi]\): on that interval cosine falls steadily from \(1\) down to \(-1\), strictly decreasing, one-to-one. Its inverse, arccosine (\(\arccos x\)), has domain \([-1,1]\) and range \([0, \pi]\). Restrict tangent to the open interval \(\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)\) — the branch of the tangent graph that runs between its two vertical asymptotes: on that interval tangent climbs strictly from \(-\infty\) to \(\infty\), covering every real number exactly once. Its inverse, arctangent (\(\arctan x\)), has domain all real numbers and range \(\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)\).

Notice the pattern common to all three: each inverse's range is exactly the restricted domain the original function submitted to. That restricted range is what a “principal value” means — the one answer the inverse is permitted to return.

Example 1

Find the exact value of \(\arcsin\dfrac{1}{2}\), \(\arcsin\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right)\), and \(\arccos\dfrac{1}{2}\).

Solution:

Each question asks: what angle, inside the required range, has this sine or cosine value?

\(\arcsin\dfrac{1}{2}\): I need an angle in \(\left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]\) whose sine is \(\frac{1}{2}\). From the special angles, \(\sin\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{1}{2}\), and \(\frac{\pi}{6}\) is inside the required range. So \(\arcsin\dfrac{1}{2} = \dfrac{\pi}{6}\).

\(\arcsin\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right)\): sine is negative, so the angle must be in the lower half of the restricted range, \(\left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, 0\right)\). Since \(\sin\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\) and sine is an odd function, \(\sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\), and \(-\frac{\pi}{4}\) is inside \(\left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right]\). So \(\arcsin\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right) = -\dfrac{\pi}{4}\).

\(\arccos\dfrac{1}{2}\): I need an angle in \([0, \pi]\) whose cosine is \(\frac{1}{2}\). Since \(\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{1}{2}\) and \(\frac{\pi}{3}\) is inside \([0,\pi]\), \(\arccos\dfrac{1}{2} = \dfrac{\pi}{3}\).

Answer: \(\arcsin\dfrac{1}{2} = \dfrac{\pi}{6}\), \(\arcsin\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right) = -\dfrac{\pi}{4}\), \(\arccos\dfrac{1}{2} = \dfrac{\pi}{3}\).

Example 2

Find the exact value of \(\arccos\left(-\dfrac{1}{2}\right)\).

Solution:

Cosine is negative, so the angle must live in the second half of arccosine's range, \(\left(\frac{\pi}{2}, \pi\right]\). Using the reference angle \(\frac{\pi}{3}\) (since \(\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{1}{2}\)), the Quadrant II angle with the same reference angle is \(\pi - \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{2\pi}{3}\). Check: \(\cos\frac{2\pi}{3} = \cos\left(\pi - \frac{\pi}{3}\right) = -\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = -\frac{1}{2}\) — matches, and \(\frac{2\pi}{3}\) is inside \([0,\pi]\), so it is the valid principal value.

Answer: \(\arccos\left(-\dfrac{1}{2}\right) = \dfrac{2\pi}{3}\). Notice this is the only acceptable answer — the angle \(\frac{4\pi}{3}\) also has cosine \(-\frac{1}{2}\), but it lies outside \([0,\pi]\), so arccosine is not permitted to return it.

Example 3

Find the exact value of \(\cos\left(\arcsin\dfrac{3}{5}\right)\).

Solution:

Let \(\theta = \arcsin\dfrac{3}{5}\). Since \(\frac{3}{5} > 0\) and arcsine's range is \(\left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right]\), \(\theta\) must lie in \(\left(0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\) — Quadrant I, where every ratio is positive. Build a reference right triangle for \(\theta\): since \(\sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}} = \frac{3}{5}\), let opposite \(= 3\) and hypotenuse \(= 5\). By the Pythagorean theorem, adjacent \(= \sqrt{5^2 - 3^2} = \sqrt{25-9} = \sqrt{16} = 4\). Then \(\cos\theta = \dfrac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}} = \dfrac{4}{5}\), positive as expected in Quadrant I.

Answer: \(\cos\left(\arcsin\dfrac{3}{5}\right) = \dfrac{4}{5}\). The whole computation never needed a decimal or a calculator — the restricted range told us exactly which quadrant the triangle belonged to, and the Pythagorean theorem supplied the missing side.

Example 4

Find the exact value of \(\sin\left(\arccos\left(-\dfrac{1}{3}\right)\right)\) and \(\tan\left(\arccos\dfrac{5}{13}\right)\).

Solution:

Let \(\theta = \arccos\left(-\frac{1}{3}\right)\). Arccosine's range is \([0,\pi]\), and since cosine is negative here, \(\theta\) must be in \(\left(\frac{\pi}{2}, \pi\right]\) — Quadrant II, where sine is positive but cosine and the horizontal leg are negative. With \(\cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}} = -\frac{1}{3}\), take hypotenuse \(= 3\) and the signed adjacent leg \(= -1\). The opposite leg's magnitude is \(\sqrt{3^2 - (-1)^2} = \sqrt{9-1} = \sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2}\), and it is positive since sine is positive in Quadrant II. So \(\sin\theta = \dfrac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}\).

Now let \(\varphi = \arccos\dfrac{5}{13}\). Cosine here is positive, so \(\varphi \in \left[0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\) — Quadrant I. With adjacent \(=5\), hypotenuse \(=13\), the opposite leg is \(\sqrt{13^2-5^2} = \sqrt{169-25} = \sqrt{144} = 12\). So \(\tan\varphi = \dfrac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}} = \dfrac{12}{5}\).

Answer: \(\sin\left(\arccos\left(-\dfrac{1}{3}\right)\right) = \dfrac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}\); \(\tan\left(\arccos\dfrac{5}{13}\right) = \dfrac{12}{5}\).

Example 5 — the pitfall that catches almost everyone once

Evaluate \(\arcsin\left(\sin\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\right)\).

Solution:

The tempting answer is \(\frac{2\pi}{3}\) — “arcsine undoes sine, so it should just hand the angle back.” It does not, and the restricted range is exactly why. First evaluate the inside: \(\sin\frac{2\pi}{3} = \sin\left(\pi - \frac{\pi}{3}\right) = \sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\). Now apply arcsine to that result: \(\arcsin\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\) asks for the angle in \(\left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right]\) whose sine is \(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\), and that angle is \(\frac{\pi}{3}\) — not \(\frac{2\pi}{3}\), because \(\frac{2\pi}{3}\) is outside arcsine's permitted range and is therefore never an option.

Answer: \(\arcsin\left(\sin\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\right) = \dfrac{\pi}{3}\), not \(\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\). The identity \(\arcsin(\sin x) = x\) is only guaranteed when \(x\) already lives inside \(\left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right]\); step outside that interval, and arcsine's self-government takes over and returns the one angle it is permitted to return.

✅ Cancellation Rules — and Where They Break

Going “trig, then inverse trig” always cancels cleanly, because the domain restriction never gets in the way of the first step: \(\sin(\arcsin x) = x\) for every \(x\) in \([-1,1]\); \(\cos(\arccos x) = x\) for every \(x\) in \([-1,1]\); \(\tan(\arctan x) = x\) for every real \(x\).

Going “inverse trig, then trig” cancels only on one condition — the original angle must already sit inside the restricted range: \(\arcsin(\sin x) = x\) only when \(x \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right]\); \(\arccos(\cos x) = x\) only when \(x \in [0,\pi]\); \(\arctan(\tan x) = x\) only when \(x \in \left(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\). Outside those windows, the inverse function still returns a valid angle with the same trig value — just not the original one, exactly as Example 5 showed.

⚠️ AI CHECKPOINT: THE OUT-OF-RANGE ANSWER

A student asked an AI tool to evaluate \(\arccos\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)\). It answered:

“\(\arccos\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) = -\frac{2\pi}{3}\), since \(\cos\left(-\frac{2\pi}{3}\right) = -\frac{1}{2}\).”

Check the trig value first: \(\cos\left(-\frac{2\pi}{3}\right) = \cos\frac{2\pi}{3} = -\frac{1}{2}\) (cosine is even) — that part is true. But arccosine is not permitted to return any angle with the right cosine; it is bound by self-government to its range \([0,\pi]\), and \(-\frac{2\pi}{3}\) is negative, so it lies outside that interval entirely.

The correct principal value, as Example 2 derived, is \(\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\) — the angle with the same cosine that actually lives inside \([0,\pi]\). Before accepting any inverse-trig answer from a tool or from your own recall, ask the single question that catches this error every time: is the returned angle actually inside the function's required range? If it is not, the arithmetic may be flawless and the answer is still wrong.

▶  Try it yourself: Find the exact value of \(\arctan(-1)\) and \(\arctan\sqrt{3}\).
\(\arctan(-1) = -\dfrac{\pi}{4}\), since \(\tan\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = -1\) and \(-\frac{\pi}{4}\) is inside \(\left(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\). \(\arctan\sqrt{3} = \dfrac{\pi}{3}\), since \(\tan\frac{\pi}{3} = \sqrt{3}\) and \(\frac{\pi}{3}\) is inside the same interval.
▶  Try it yourself: Find the exact value of \(\sin\left(\arccos\dfrac{5}{13}\right)\), using a reference right triangle.
Let \(\theta = \arccos\frac{5}{13}\), which lies in \(\left[0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\) since cosine is positive. With adjacent \(=5\) and hypotenuse \(=13\), the opposite leg is \(\sqrt{13^2-5^2}=\sqrt{144}=12\). So \(\sin\theta = \dfrac{12}{13}\).
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Treating \(\arcsin(\sin x)\) or \(\arccos(\cos x)\) as automatically equal to \(x\), regardless of where \(x\) falls. The cancellation only holds when \(x\) is already inside the inverse function's range — exactly the lesson of Example 5. When \(x\) falls outside that window, the inverse function still returns a genuine angle with the matching trig value; it is simply not the angle you started with. Always ask which quadrant the answer is required to come from before trusting a cancellation.

Conclusion

Unit 4 promised that restricting a domain could turn an un-invertible function into one you could trust, and today that promise paid off in full: three restricted domains, three inverse functions, each one submitting to its range so that “the angle whose sine is \(x\)” never has to mean “infinitely many angles.” That discipline — a function governing itself so it can be relied upon — is this unit's virtue made concrete in the most literal way trigonometry offers. The next lesson turns this tool outward: once you can choose a sine, cosine, or tangent model to fit a periodic phenomenon, inverse trigonometric functions become exactly the instrument you need to solve that model for a specific time or angle — the payoff behind today's payoff.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does sine fail the horizontal line test over its full domain, and how does restricting its domain to \(\left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right]\) fix that failure?

2. Why is the range of \(\arccos x\) exactly \([0,\pi]\), and how does that range decide which quadrant \(\arccos\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)\) must come from?

3. In your own words, why is \(\arcsin(\sin x) = x\) not always true, even though \(\sin(\arcsin x) = x\) always is?

4. How does the restricted range of an inverse trig function embody this unit's virtue of self-government?
