Solving Trigonometric Equations
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to solve linear trigonometric equations exactly, both on a stated interval and in general form.

  • I will be able to solve quadratic-type trigonometric equations by substitution and factoring.

  • I will be able to use an identity to reduce an equation involving more than one trig expression into a solvable factored form.

  • I will be able to state a complete general solution using an arbitrary integer \(k\), and explain why sine/cosine equations need \(+2k\pi\) while tangent equations need only \(+k\pi\).

📚 VOCABULARY

Trigonometric equation: An equation in which the unknown angle appears inside a trigonometric function — \(\sin\theta = 1/2\), for instance, rather than \(x=1/2\). Because sine, cosine, and tangent are periodic, such an equation is rarely satisfied by a single number.

Solution set on an interval: The complete, finite list of angles within a stated interval (most often \([0,2\pi)\), one full trip around the circle) that satisfy the equation.

General solution: The complete, infinite family of every angle — on the whole real line, past and future revolutions alike — that satisfies the equation, written using an arbitrary integer \(k\): \(+2k\pi\) for sine and cosine (period \(2\pi\)), \(+k\pi\) for tangent and cotangent (period \(\pi\)).

Quadratic-type trigonometric equation: An equation that becomes an ordinary quadratic once a trig function is treated as a single variable — substitute \(x=\cos\theta\) (or \(\sin\theta\), or \(\tan\theta\)) and the familiar factoring or quadratic-formula toolkit applies.

Extraneous solution: A value produced by an algebraic step — squaring, or substituting an identity — that does not actually satisfy the original equation, and must be discarded by checking back against that original equation, not the intermediate one.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Long before any calendar existed to tell a farmer when to plant or a sailor when a tide would turn, the recurring positions of the sun, moon, and stars had to be predicted from pattern alone — and a prediction built on periodic motion is only as good as its accounting of every repetition, not merely the next one. An astronomer who solved "when will this eclipse configuration recur?" and stopped at the first answer would have handed a farmer or a sailor a single date and silently discarded every other true one buried in the same equation. That is exactly the discipline this lesson asks of you: a trigonometric equation, because sine, cosine, and tangent repeat forever, essentially never has one answer. It has a whole family of answers, and naming the entire family — not just the first one your calculator hands you — is this unit's self-government at its most literal: a repeating, disciplined pattern of solutions, and a mathematician disciplined enough to report all of it.

On an Interval, or in General?

Every trig equation can be answered two ways, and a problem should always tell you which is wanted:

On an interval (almost always \([0,2\pi)\), one full trip around the circle): list the finite set of angles in that interval that work.

In general: describe every angle, on the whole real line, that works — past revolutions and future ones alike — using an arbitrary integer \(k\). Because sine and cosine repeat every \(2\pi\), their general solutions add \(+2k\pi\) to each interval answer. Tangent (and cotangent) repeat every \(\pi\) — a fact you can verify directly: \(\tan(\theta+\pi)=\tan\theta\) for every \(\theta\) where tangent is defined — so a tangent equation's general solution needs only \(+k\pi\), and that single family already contains both quadrant answers a \([0,2\pi)\) search would have found separately.

Skipping this distinction — reporting only the interval answer when "general" was asked, or forgetting the second family for a sine/cosine equation — is the single most common way a correct piece of algebra turns into an incomplete answer.

Example 1

Solve \(2\sin\theta - 1 = 0\) on \([0,2\pi)\), then state the general solution.

Solution:

Isolate the trig expression first, exactly as you would isolate \(x\):

\(2\sin\theta - 1 = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \sin\theta = \dfrac{1}{2}\)

Sine is positive in Quadrants I and II, and the reference angle for \(\tfrac12\) is \(\pi/6\):

\(\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{6} \quad \text{or} \quad \theta = \pi - \dfrac{\pi}{6} = \dfrac{5\pi}{6}\)

Answer: On \([0,2\pi)\): \(\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{6}, \dfrac{5\pi}{6}\). Because sine repeats every \(2\pi\), the general solution is \(\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{6} + 2k\pi\) or \(\theta = \dfrac{5\pi}{6} + 2k\pi\), for any integer \(k\) — two separate, infinite families, one seeded from each interval answer.

Example 2

Solve \(2\cos^2\theta - \cos\theta - 1 = 0\) on \([0,2\pi)\).

Solution:

This is quadratic in \(\cos\theta\). Substitute \(x = \cos\theta\):

\(2x^2 - x - 1 = 0\)

Factor — check by expanding before trusting it: \((2x+1)(x-1) = 2x^2 - 2x + x - 1 = 2x^2 - x - 1\), a match:

\((2x+1)(x-1) = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x = -\dfrac{1}{2} \;\; \text{or} \;\; x = 1\)

Translate back to \(\theta\), one factor at a time. \(\cos\theta = 1\) has exactly one solution on \([0,2\pi)\): \(\theta = 0\). \(\cos\theta = -\tfrac12\) has reference angle \(\pi/3\) and is negative in Quadrants II and III: \(\theta = \tfrac{2\pi}{3}, \tfrac{4\pi}{3}\).

Answer: The complete solution set on \([0,2\pi)\) is \(\left\{0, \dfrac{2\pi}{3}, \dfrac{4\pi}{3}\right\}\) — three solutions from a single quadratic, because the substitution \(x=\cos\theta\) hid a second layer of solutions (the quadrant pair) inside what looked like one root.

Example 3

Solve \(\sin(2\theta) = \cos\theta\) on \([0,2\pi)\).

Solution:

Two different angle arguments (\(2\theta\) and \(\theta\)) cannot be compared directly — rewrite the double angle first using \(\sin(2\theta) = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta\):

\(2\sin\theta\cos\theta = \cos\theta \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; 2\sin\theta\cos\theta - \cos\theta = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \cos\theta\,(2\sin\theta - 1) = 0\)

A product is zero only if a factor is zero — never divide both sides by \(\cos\theta\) here, or you silently throw away every solution where \(\cos\theta = 0\).

\(\cos\theta = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \theta = \dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{3\pi}{2} \qquad\qquad \sin\theta = \dfrac{1}{2} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \theta = \dfrac{\pi}{6}, \dfrac{5\pi}{6}\)

Verify — in the ORIGINAL equation, not the factored one:

\(\theta=\tfrac{\pi}{6}: \sin\tfrac{\pi}{3}=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},\; \cos\tfrac{\pi}{6}=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\; \checkmark \qquad \theta=\tfrac{\pi}{2}: \sin\pi=0,\; \cos\tfrac{\pi}{2}=0\; \checkmark\)

\(\theta=\tfrac{5\pi}{6}: \sin\tfrac{5\pi}{3}=-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},\; \cos\tfrac{5\pi}{6}=-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\; \checkmark \qquad \theta=\tfrac{3\pi}{2}: \sin(3\pi)=0,\; \cos\tfrac{3\pi}{2}=0\; \checkmark\)

Answer: The complete solution set on \([0,2\pi)\) is \(\left\{\dfrac{\pi}{6}, \dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{5\pi}{6}, \dfrac{3\pi}{2}\right\}\). Notice that checking against the original equation, rather than stopping at the factored line \(\cos\theta(2\sin\theta-1)=0\), is what actually confirms the identity substitution was applied correctly — the factored form only proves consistency with itself.

Example 4

Solve \(\tan\theta = -1\) in general form.

Solution:

Reference angle \(\pi/4\); tangent is negative in Quadrants II and IV:

\(\theta = \pi - \dfrac{\pi}{4} = \dfrac{3\pi}{4} \qquad \text{or} \qquad \theta = 2\pi - \dfrac{\pi}{4} = \dfrac{7\pi}{4} \quad \text{(on } [0,2\pi))\)

But tangent's period is \(\pi\), not \(2\pi\) — so these two answers are not independent families; they are the same family, one \(\pi\) apart. Check directly: \(\dfrac{3\pi}{4} + \pi = \dfrac{7\pi}{4}\), exactly the QIV answer, recovered for free.

Answer: The general solution is the single family \(\theta = \dfrac{3\pi}{4} + k\pi\), for any integer \(k\) — writing \(+2k\pi\) here (as sine or cosine would require) would silently drop half of the true solutions, since it would skip right over \(\tfrac{7\pi}{4}\), \(\tfrac{11\pi}{4}\), and every other odd multiple of \(\pi\) added to \(\tfrac{3\pi}{4}\).

✅ The Verification Habit

Anytime an equation was changed along the way — an identity substituted, both sides squared, or a factor introduced — check every candidate solution against the original equation, not the rewritten one. The rewritten form only proves you followed your own algebra correctly; the original equation is the actual claim you set out to solve, and it is the only place an extraneous solution (or a solution silently dropped) will show itself. Example 3 above is the model: four candidates, four independent checks, against \(\sin(2\theta)=\cos\theta\) itself.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Two versions of the same failure of discipline: (1) finding one quadrant answer and stopping — reporting \(\theta=\tfrac{2\pi}{3}\) alone for \(\cos\theta=-\tfrac12\) and missing its Quadrant III partner \(\tfrac{4\pi}{3}\); and (2) writing \(+2k\pi\) on a tangent equation's general solution, which looks careful but actually reports each true solution twice under two different names while implying a period the function does not have. Both mistakes come from treating "a" solution as "the" solution — exactly the discipline this lesson is built to correct.
💡 ON YOUR CALCULATOR
Desmos (desmos.com/calculator) turns any equation into a zero-finding problem: move everything to one side and graph the result. For Example 3, graph y = sin(2x) - cos(x) in radian mode over \(0 \le x < 2\pi\) and look for the \(x\)-intercepts — you should see exactly four, at \(\tfrac{\pi}{6}\approx0.524\), \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\approx1.571\), \(\tfrac{5\pi}{6}\approx2.618\), and \(\tfrac{3\pi}{2}\approx4.712\), matching the algebra exactly. This does not replace solving by hand — a graph cannot hand you an exact value like \(\pi/6\) — but it is a fast, reliable check that you have found every solution and none you shouldn't have.

\(\cos\theta = -\tfrac12\); reference angle \(\pi/3\), cosine negative in QII and QIII: \(\theta = \dfrac{2\pi}{3}, \dfrac{4\pi}{3}\).
▶  Try it yourself: Solve \(2\sin^2\theta + \sin\theta - 1 = 0\) on \([0,2\pi)\).
Let \(x=\sin\theta\): \(2x^2+x-1=0 \Rightarrow (2x-1)(x+1)=0 \Rightarrow x=\tfrac12\) or \(x=-1\). \(\sin\theta=\tfrac12 \Rightarrow \theta=\tfrac{\pi}{6},\tfrac{5\pi}{6}\); \(\sin\theta=-1 \Rightarrow \theta=\tfrac{3\pi}{2}\). Solution set: \(\left\{\tfrac{\pi}{6}, \tfrac{5\pi}{6}, \tfrac{3\pi}{2}\right\}\).

Conclusion

Every move in this lesson — isolating a trig expression before anything else, substituting to reveal a hidden quadratic, using an identity to bring mismatched angles into agreement, and above all refusing to stop at the first solution — is about to be needed again, immediately. Next week, the Law of Sines and the Law of Cosines let you solve triangles that have no right angle at all, and solving them for an unknown angle produces exactly this same kind of equation: one that, given only a ratio or a squared length, is often satisfied by two genuinely different triangles rather than one. That situation even has its own name — the ambiguous case — and it is nothing more than this lesson's lesson wearing a triangle's clothing: don't stop at the first solution; find out how many there really are.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does a trigonometric equation generally have infinitely many solutions, and what does it mean to give a "complete" answer to one?

2. When is \(+2k\pi\) the correct way to generalize a solution, and when must \(+k\pi\) be used instead? Why does the difference trace back to each function's period?

3. Why must a candidate solution be checked against the original equation rather than an intermediate factored or identity-substituted form?

4. In what sense does naming a complete, disciplined solution set — rather than the first answer a calculator returns — count as an act of self-government?
