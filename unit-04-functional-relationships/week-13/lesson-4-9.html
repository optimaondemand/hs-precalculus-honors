Finding and Graphing Inverses
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to find the inverse of a one-to-one function algebraically by the swap-and-solve method, and verify my answer by composition.

  • I will be able to graph a function and its inverse together as reflections across the line \(y = x\), with domains and ranges exchanged.

  • I will be able to read inverse values from a table or graph without any formula at all.

📚 VOCABULARY

Inverse function \(f^{-1}\): The function that undoes \(f\) — for every pairing \(f(a) = b\), the inverse pairs \(f^{-1}(b) = a\). It exists exactly when \(f\) is one-to-one.

Swap-and-solve: The algebraic method for finding \(f^{-1}\): write \(y = f(x)\), exchange the letters \(x\) and \(y\) (exchanging the roles of input and output), then solve for the new \(y\).

Identity line: The line \(y = x\), where input and output are equal. It is the mirror of today's lesson: the graphs of \(f\) and \(f^{-1}\) are reflections of each other across it.

One-to-one (review from 4-8): A function in which no output is ever produced by two different inputs — the property, certified by the horizontal line test, that makes undoing possible at all.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Yesterday you learned what an inverse is: two functions that undo each other, certified by the composition test \(f^{-1}(f(x)) = x\) and \(f(f^{-1}(x)) = x\). But yesterday the candidate inverses were handed to you. Today you learn to manufacture them — and to see them. When Leibniz began writing about functions in the 1690s, his whole idea was that a function is a relationship read in a direction: from input to output. An inverse is the same relationship read the other way, with the courtesy returned — every output escorted back to the input it came from. The algebra of that reversal is one move, used three ways: on formulas, on graphs, and on tables.

The move is called swap-and-solve, and it works for a reason worth stating before the mechanics. The equation \(y = f(x)\) says “\(y\) is the output of \(x\).” Exchanging the letters — writing \(x = f(y)\) — asks the reverse question: “which input \(y\) produced the output \(x\)?” Solving for \(y\) answers it. The formula that emerges is \(f^{-1}\), because it expresses each input as a function of its output.

Example 1

Find the inverse of \(f(x) = 2x + 3\), and verify by both compositions.

Solution:

Write \(y = 2x + 3\), swap the letters, and solve:

\(x = 2y + 3 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x - 3 = 2y \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; y = \dfrac{x - 3}{2}\)

So \(f^{-1}(x) = \dfrac{x-3}{2}\). Notice what the inverse actually does: \(f\) says multiply by 2, then add 3; the inverse says subtract 3, then divide by 2 — each step undone, in reverse order, like taking off shoes before socks. Verify:

\(f\!\left(f^{-1}(x)\right) = 2\cdot\dfrac{x-3}{2} + 3 = (x - 3) + 3 = x\) ✓

\(f^{-1}\!\left(f(x)\right) = \dfrac{(2x+3)-3}{2} = \dfrac{2x}{2} = x\) ✓

Answer: \(f^{-1}(x) = \dfrac{x-3}{2}\), certified in both directions. The composition check is not a formality — it is the definition being confirmed, and it is how every inverse in this course earns its name.

Why the Mirror Works

Every fact about \(f^{-1}\) flows from one observation: if \((a, b)\) is a point of \(f\) — meaning \(f(a) = b\) — then \((b, a)\) is a point of \(f^{-1}\). The inverse's graph is the original's graph with every coordinate pair swapped.

  • Swapping the coordinates of a point reflects it across the line \(y = x\): the point \((5, 2)\) and the point \((2, 5)\) are mirror images in that line. So the whole graph of \(f^{-1}\) is the whole graph of \(f\), reflected across the identity line.
  • Since inputs and outputs trade places, the domain of \(f^{-1}\) is the range of \(f\), and the range of \(f^{-1}\) is the domain of \(f\). Whatever territory the original's outputs occupied, the inverse's inputs occupy.
  • Points on the line \(y = x\) itself — where input equals output — do not move under the reflection. Wherever the graph of \(f\) crosses the identity line, \(f^{-1}\) crosses at the very same point.

One idea, three dividends: the algebra (swap the letters), the geometry (reflect across \(y=x\)), and the bookkeeping (domain and range exchange) are all the same coordinate swap.

Example 2

Find the inverse of \(f(x) = \dfrac{3x - 2}{x + 1}\), and describe how the asymptotes of \(f\) and \(f^{-1}\) are related.

Solution:

Swap and solve — the new \(y\) appears in two places, so gather its terms on one side and factor it out:

\(x = \dfrac{3y - 2}{y + 1}\)

\(x(y + 1) = 3y - 2\)

\(xy + x = 3y - 2\)

\(xy - 3y = -x - 2\)

\(y(x - 3) = -(x + 2) \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; y = \dfrac{x + 2}{3 - x}\)

Spot-check with a point before trusting it: \(f(0) = -2\), and \(f^{-1}(-2) = \dfrac{0}{5} = 0\) ✓. The full composition check (worth doing once tonight) gives \(\dfrac{5x}{5} = x\) ✓.

Answer: \(f^{-1}(x) = \dfrac{x + 2}{3 - x}\). Now look at the asymptotes with Unit 2 eyes. The original has vertical asymptote \(x = -1\) and horizontal asymptote \(y = 3\); the inverse has vertical asymptote \(x = 3\) and horizontal asymptote \(y = -1\). The asymptotes traded places — and they had to, because the vertical asymptote marks a hole in the domain, the horizontal marks a hole in the range, and domain and range are exactly what an inverse exchanges.

Example 3

Find the inverse of \(f(x) = \sqrt{x + 4}\), graph \(f\) and \(f^{-1}\) on the same axes, and state both domains and ranges.

Solution:

First read the original: domain \([-4, \infty)\) (the radicand's guard), range \([0, \infty)\) (the principal root's outputs). Now swap and solve — and since solving requires squaring, Unit 3's discipline comes with it:

\(x = \sqrt{y + 4}\)

Guard: the left side equals a principal square root, so \(x \geq 0\) — posted before squaring. Then:

\(x^2 = y + 4 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; y = x^2 - 4, \qquad x \geq 0\)

The guard is not decoration. Without it, \(y = x^2 - 4\) is a full parabola — a curve that fails the vertical line test's mirror image and includes a phantom branch that was never part of the reversal. Squaring created that extra branch, exactly as it created ghost solutions in 3-10; the posted guard \(x \geq 0\) cuts it away and keeps only the true inverse.

For the graph, swap the anchor points of \(f\): the points \((-4, 0)\), \((-3, 1)\), \((0, 2)\), \((5, 3)\) on \(f\) become \((0, -4)\), \((1, -3)\), \((2, 0)\), \((3, 5)\) on \(f^{-1}\). Draw both curves and the dashed line \(y = x\): each curve is the other's reflection.

Answer: \(f^{-1}(x) = x^2 - 4\) with domain \([0, \infty)\) and range \([-4, \infty)\) — precisely the range and domain of \(f\), exchanged. Verify the guard did its work: \(f^{-1}(f(x)) = \left(\sqrt{x+4}\right)^2 - 4 = x\) ✓, and \(f(f^{-1}(x)) = \sqrt{x^2 - 4 + 4} = \sqrt{x^2} = |x| = x\) because \(x \geq 0\) ✓. That absolute value quietly becoming \(x\) is the guard earning its keep.

The Inverse Dictionary

Everything an inverse does, in five matched pairs — memorize the left column and the right column comes free:

  • \(f\) sends \(a \mapsto b\)  ↔  \(f^{-1}\) sends \(b \mapsto a\)
  • point \((a, b)\) on \(f\)  ↔  point \((b, a)\) on \(f^{-1}\)
  • domain of \(f\)  ↔  range of \(f^{-1}\) (and vice versa)
  • vertical asymptote of \(f\) at \(x = c\)  ↔  horizontal asymptote of \(f^{-1}\) at \(y = c\)
  • graph of \(f\)  ↔  graph of \(f^{-1}\), reflected across \(y = x\)

Example 4

The table below defines a one-to-one function \(f\). Find \(f^{-1}(3)\), \(f^{-1}(-4)\), and \(\left(f^{-1} \circ f\right)(1)\).

\(\begin{array}{c|ccccc} x & -2 & 0 & 1 & 3 & 5 \\ \hline f(x) & 7 & 3 & 0 & -1 & -4 \end{array}\)

Solution:

No formula, no algebra — an inverse value is a question read backward through the table. \(f^{-1}(3)\) asks: which input produced the output 3? Scan the bottom row: \(f(0) = 3\), so \(f^{-1}(3) = 0\). Likewise \(f(5) = -4\), so \(f^{-1}(-4) = 5\). For the composition: \(f(1) = 0\), and \(f^{-1}(0)\) asks which input produced 0 — that is \(1\) itself, so \(\left(f^{-1} \circ f\right)(1) = 1\).

Answer: \(f^{-1}(3) = 0\), \(\;f^{-1}(-4) = 5\), \(\;\left(f^{-1} \circ f\right)(1) = 1\). The last one is the whole theory in miniature: an inverse composed with its function returns every input home, whether the function lives in a formula, a graph, or five columns of a table. (And note why the table's one-to-one-ness mattered: if two inputs had shared the output 3, the question “which input produced 3?” would have no single answer — and \(f^{-1}\) would not be a function.)

💻 In Desmos: The Mirror, Live 8 min

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), rebuild Example 3 — and learn Desmos's best inverse trick:

  1. Graph f(x) = sqrt(x + 4), and add the identity line y = x (click its color swatch and make it dashed — it is scaffolding, not a curve under study).
  2. Now type x = sqrt(y + 4). Desmos graphs any equation in \(x\) and \(y\), so this plots every point whose coordinates satisfy the swapped equation — which is precisely the graph of \(f^{-1}\), no solving required. Watch it appear as the mirror image of \(f\).
  3. Check your algebra against the machine: graph y = x^2 - 4. The parabola matches Desmos's inverse curve on the right half — and grows an extra left branch the inverse does not have. That surplus branch is what the guard \(x \geq 0\) exists to remove. Fix it: y = x^2 - 4 {x >= 0} — now the match is exact.
  4. Turn the grid: click any point on \(f\), read its coordinates, and find the swapped pair on the inverse curve. Every pair, every time. The calculator confirms; your algebra convinces.

▶  Try it yourself: Find the inverse of \(f(x) = 4 - 2x\) and verify it by both compositions. Work it on paper before you peek.
Swap: \(x = 4 - 2y\), so \(2y = 4 - x\) and \(y = \dfrac{4 - x}{2} = 2 - \dfrac{x}{2}\). Verify: \(f\!\left(2 - \tfrac{x}{2}\right) = 4 - 2\left(2 - \tfrac{x}{2}\right) = 4 - 4 + x = x\) ✓; and \(f^{-1}(4 - 2x) = 2 - \dfrac{4 - 2x}{2} = 2 - 2 + x = x\) ✓. So \(f^{-1}(x) = 2 - \dfrac{x}{2}\).
▶  Try it yourself: Find the inverse of \(f(x) = \sqrt{x - 1} + 2\), with the guard posted and both domains stated.
\(f\) has domain \([1, \infty)\) and range \([2, \infty)\). Swap: \(x = \sqrt{y - 1} + 2\), so \(x - 2 = \sqrt{y - 1}\) — guard: \(x \geq 2\). Square: \((x-2)^2 = y - 1\), so \(f^{-1}(x) = (x - 2)^2 + 1\) with domain \([2, \infty)\) and range \([1, \infty)\). Check: \(f\!\left(f^{-1}(x)\right) = \sqrt{(x-2)^2 + 1 - 1} + 2 = |x - 2| + 2 = x\) since \(x \geq 2\) ✓.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Reading \(f^{-1}(x)\) as \(\dfrac{1}{f(x)}\). The superscript \(-1\) on a function means “inverse under composition,” not “reciprocal” — the two are almost never equal. Test it on Example 1: \(\dfrac{1}{f(1)} = \dfrac{1}{5}\), but \(f^{-1}(1) = \dfrac{1-3}{2} = -1\). Not close. When a reciprocal is genuinely meant, write \(\left(f(x)\right)^{-1}\) or \(\dfrac{1}{f(x)}\) — and keep \(f^{-1}(x)\) reserved for the undoing function. This distinction will matter enormously in the spring, when \(\sin^{-1} x\) means arcsine and emphatically not \(\frac{1}{\sin x}\).
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The notation \(f^{-1}\) is younger than you might guess. Leibniz gave mathematics the word “function” in the 1690s and Euler gave it the costume \(f(x)\) in the 1730s, but the inverse had no symbol of its own until 1813, when the English astronomer John Herschel proposed \(f^{-1}\) — borrowing the exponent notation on purpose, so that \(f^{-1}(f(x)) = x\) would echo \(a^{-1} \cdot a = 1\). Herschel himself flagged the danger you just met in the common-mistake box: the same symbol already meant reciprocal. Mathematics kept the notation, ambiguity and all — which is why, two centuries later, precision about which \(-1\) you mean is still part of the craft.

Conclusion

One coordinate swap paid three ways today: swap the letters and you have the algebra of \(f^{-1}\); swap the coordinates and you have its graph, mirrored across \(y = x\); swap domain and range and you have its bookkeeping, down to asymptotes trading places. And when the solving required a square, Unit 3's guard discipline came along — because squaring manufactures a phantom branch for inverses exactly as it manufactured ghost solutions for equations, and the posted guard is what keeps the reversal honest. But everything today rested on a courtesy the function had to extend first: being one-to-one. Tomorrow we face the functions that refuse — the parabolas and absolute values whose folded graphs make the reverse question ambiguous — and learn the elegant compromise that makes them invertible anyway. That compromise, small as it looks, is the reason half of next semester's trigonometry will exist.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does exchanging the letters \(x\) and \(y\) — a purely notational act — produce the inverse function? What question is the swapped equation actually asking?

2. A single fact — \((a, b)\) on \(f\) means \((b, a)\) on \(f^{-1}\) — explains the mirror graph, the domain-range exchange, and the asymptote trade. Trace each consequence back to the swap.

3. In Example 3, where exactly did the guard \(x \geq 0\) come from, and what false branch would the inverse have grown without it?

4. Why must \(\left(f^{-1}\circ f\right)(x) = x\) hold for every \(x\) in the domain — and why is checking only one direction of the composition not yet a proof?
