Domain and Range of Composite Functions
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to determine the domain of \(f \circ g\) by posting both guards: the inner function's own domain, and the requirement that every inner output land inside the outer function's domain.

  • I will be able to detect the hidden restrictions that a simplified composite formula conceals, and state composite domains honestly.

  • I will be able to find the range of a composite function by following its true domain through the chain.

📚 VOCABULARY

Domain of a composition: The set of all inputs \(x\) such that \(x\) is in the domain of \(g\) and \(g(x)\) is in the domain of \(f\). Both conditions, always.

Hidden restriction: A domain condition inherited from the inner function that leaves no visible trace in the simplified composite formula — the restriction is real, but the algebra has erased the evidence.

Guard: Our standing course word for a domain condition posted before the algebra begins — the same guards you posted for rational functions in Unit 2 and radical equations in Unit 3, now standing watch over a chain.

Pedigree: An informal but useful word for a composite function's ancestry — which \(f\) and which \(g\) it was born from. A composite's domain is determined by its pedigree, not by its simplified formula.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

For a century and a half after Leibniz named the function, mathematicians quietly assumed that a function simply was its formula. It took until 1837 for Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet to state the modern view plainly: a function is any rule of correspondence assigning one output to each input — and the domain is part of the function's very identity, not an afterthought scribbled beside the formula. Two rules with the same formula but different domains are different functions. Today's lesson is Dirichlet's insight applied to yesterday's constructions: when functions are chained, the composite's domain is inherited from both parents — and, in the most instructive cases, the simplified formula will look you in the eye and misreport it. This is a domain-of-honesty lesson from start to finish: the formula smiles and says “all real numbers”; the pedigree keeps the true record.

Think again about the courtesy protocol of \(f(g(x))\): \(g\) speaks first, and \(f\) responds to what \(g\) hands it. Two things can go wrong in that exchange. The input \(x\) may be one that \(g\) itself cannot accept — or \(g\) may accept it and then hand \(f\) a value that \(f\)'s domain refuses. A legal input for the composition must clear both checkpoints.

The Two Guards of \(f \circ g\)

\(x\) belongs to the domain of \(f \circ g\) exactly when:

  • Guard 1 (the inner gate): \(x\) is in the domain of \(g\) — the inner function can receive it; and
  • Guard 2 (the outer gate): \(g(x)\) is in the domain of \(f\) — the outer function can receive what the inner one hands over.

Post both guards before you simplify anything. Simplification is legal algebra, but it can erase Guard 1's fingerprints from the formula — and a guard whose evidence has been erased is still on duty.

Example 1

Let \(f(x) = \sqrt{x}\) and \(g(x) = x - 4\). Find the domain and range of \(f \circ g\).

Solution:

Guard 1: \(g\) is a polynomial — it accepts every real number. No restriction. Guard 2: \(f\) demands a nonnegative input, so we need \(g(x) \geq 0\):

\(x - 4 \geq 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x \geq 4\)

The composite is \((f \circ g)(x) = \sqrt{x - 4}\), and here the formula is honest — the restriction is still visible under the radical.

Answer: Domain \([4, \infty)\); range \([0, \infty)\), since the square root delivers every nonnegative value as \(x\) runs through the domain. When only Guard 2 restricts, the composite formula usually still shows the scar — the interesting trouble starts when Guard 1 is the restrictive one.

Example 2

Let \(f(x) = x^2\) and \(g(x) = \sqrt{x - 1}\). Find \((f \circ g)(x)\), its domain, and its range.

Solution:

Guard 1: \(g\)'s radicand demands \(x - 1 \geq 0\), so \(x \geq 1\). Guard 2: \(f\) accepts every real number — no further condition. Now, and only now, simplify:

\((f \circ g)(x) = \left(\sqrt{x-1}\,\right)^2 = x - 1\)

Read that simplified formula naïvely and it claims to be a line defined for all real numbers. But the pedigree says otherwise: no input below 1 ever survived the inner gate. The composite equals \(x - 1\) only on \([1, \infty)\) — a ray, not a line. And the range follows from the honest domain: as \(x\) runs through \([1, \infty)\), the output \(x - 1\) runs through \([0, \infty)\).

Answer: \((f \circ g)(x) = x - 1\) with domain \([1, \infty)\) and range \([0, \infty)\). The squaring erased the radical's fingerprints from the formula, but it could not erase the guard. A composite function's domain is decided at its birth, not at its simplification. This is the hidden restriction — and it is exactly the phenomenon your calculator can make visible, below.

Example 3

Let \(f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x - 1}\) and \(g(x) = \dfrac{2}{x}\). Find \((f \circ g)(x)\) and its domain.

Solution:

Guard 1: \(g\) forbids \(x = 0\). Guard 2: \(f\) forbids the input 1, so we need \(g(x) \neq 1\):

\(\dfrac{2}{x} = 1 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x = 2, \qquad \text{so the guard is } x \neq 2\)

Simplify:

\((f \circ g)(x) = \dfrac{1}{\frac{2}{x} - 1} = \dfrac{1}{\frac{2 - x}{x}} = \dfrac{x}{2 - x}\)

Check at a trusted point, \(x = 4\): the relay gives \(g(4) = \frac{1}{2}\), then \(f\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{\frac{1}{2} - 1} = -2\); the formula gives \(\frac{4}{2 - 4} = -2\) ✓.

Answer: \((f \circ g)(x) = \dfrac{x}{2 - x}\) with domain \(\{x : x \neq 0 \text{ and } x \neq 2\}\). Interrogate the simplified formula and it confesses to \(x \neq 2\) — but it has completely forgotten \(x \neq 0\), the inner gate where \(g\) itself fails. One guard visible, one guard hidden: the formula tells the truth and nothing but the truth, yet not the whole truth. Only the pedigree tells the whole truth.

Example 4

Let \(f(x) = \sqrt{x - 2}\) and \(g(x) = x^2\). Find the domain of \(f \circ g\).

Solution:

Guard 1: \(g\) accepts everything. Guard 2: \(f\) demands input \(\geq 2\), so we need \(x^2 \geq 2\). Solve it honestly — this is a quadratic inequality, and its solution set has two branches:

\(x^2 \geq 2 \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; |x| \geq \sqrt{2} \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; x \leq -\sqrt{2} \;\text{ or }\; x \geq \sqrt{2}\)

Answer: \((f \circ g)(x) = \sqrt{x^2 - 2}\), with domain \((-\infty, -\sqrt{2}\,] \cup [\sqrt{2}, \infty)\). Notice what Guard 2 really asks: not “where is \(x\) legal,” but “where does \(g\) send \(x\) somewhere legal.” The guard stands at \(f\)'s gate but does its arithmetic back at \(g\) — a condition on the inner function's outputs, translated into a condition on \(x\).

💻 On Your Calculator: The Pedigree in Pixels 8 min

Desmos composes functions natively, and — unlike a careless simplifier — it never forgets a pedigree. At desmos.com/calculator (or below), put Example 2 on trial:

  1. Define the parents on their own lines: type g(x) = sqrt(x - 1) and f(x) = x^2. Desmos accepts function definitions exactly as written.
  2. On a third line, graph the composition itself: y = f(g(x)). Look closely: a ray, launching at \((1, 0)\) and rising to the right — nothing to the left of \(x = 1\).
  3. Now graph the naïve simplification on a fourth line: y = x - 1. A full line, sailing carelessly through negative territory the true composite never visits. The horizontal gap between the two graphs is the hidden restriction, drawn in pixels.
  4. Repeat the experiment on Example 3: define that lesson's \(f\) and \(g\), graph y = f(g(x)), and click the curve at \(x = 0\) — Desmos reports the point as undefined, though the simplified formula \(\frac{x}{2-x}\) would happily have computed \(0\) there.

Desmos evaluates \(f(g(x))\) the honest way — through the chain, gate by gate — so its graph obeys the guards automatically. The calculator confirms; your algebra convinces.

▶  Try it yourself: For \(f(x) = \sqrt{x}\) and \(g(x) = 9 - x^2\), find the domain and range of \(f \circ g\). Post both guards on paper before you peek.
Guard 1: \(g\) accepts everything. Guard 2: need \(9 - x^2 \geq 0\), i.e. \(x^2 \leq 9\), i.e. \(-3 \leq x \leq 3\). So \((f \circ g)(x) = \sqrt{9 - x^2}\) with domain \([-3, 3]\). Range: the radicand \(9 - x^2\) runs from \(0\) (at \(x = \pm 3\)) up to \(9\) (at \(x = 0\)), so the root runs through \([0, 3]\). (You are looking at the upper half of a circle of radius 3 — a shape with a starring role waiting in Unit 5.)
▶  Try it yourself: For \(f(x) = \frac{1}{x}\) and \(g(x) = x + 5\), find the domain of \(f \circ g\) and of \(g \circ f\) — and notice they differ.
\((f \circ g)(x) = \frac{1}{x + 5}\): Guard 1 is nothing, Guard 2 needs \(x + 5 \neq 0\), so the domain is \(x \neq -5\). \((g \circ f)(x) = \frac{1}{x} + 5\): Guard 1 needs \(x \neq 0\), Guard 2 is nothing, so the domain is \(x \neq 0\). Same two parents, opposite order, different domains — one more way \(f \circ g\) and \(g \circ f\) are genuinely different functions.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Finding the domain of \(f \circ g\) by interrogating only the simplified formula. In Example 2 that method answers “all real numbers” and is wrong on an entire half-line; in Example 3 it catches \(x \neq 2\) but waves \(x = 0\) straight through a gate where \(g\) itself fails. The simplified formula is a witness with amnesia: honest about what it remembers, silent about what the algebra erased. Post both guards from the pedigree first; simplify second; and let the guards, not the survivor formula, write the domain.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The word domain comes from the Latin dominium — lordship, ownership, the estate over which a lord holds authority. The etymology is exact: a function genuinely rules its domain, assigning to every resident input exactly one output, and holds no authority one inch beyond its borders. When Dirichlet made the domain part of a function's identity in 1837, he was, in effect, ruling that a lord and his lands cannot be spoken of separately — which is why a formula that has forgotten its lands has forgotten part of what it is.

Conclusion

Yesterday you learned how functions chain; today you learned what the chain owes its ancestors. The domain of \(f \circ g\) is guarded twice — once at the inner gate, once at the outer — and the deepest lesson was a domain-of-honesty lesson: legal algebra can simplify a formula right past its own restrictions, so the true domain is read from the pedigree, never from the survivor. You watched Desmos honor the guards automatically, and you practiced the discipline of posting them before simplifying, in exactly the spirit of Unit 2's rational guards and Unit 3's radical guards. Tomorrow the chains leave the page and enter the checkout line, the pond, and the factory floor: real processes — a discount followed by a tax, a spreading ripple followed by an area — are compositions wearing work clothes, and you will build, evaluate, and solve them in context, units and all.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why must the domain of \(f \circ g\) satisfy two separate conditions, and what does each guard protect against?

2. How can a correctly simplified formula misreport its own function's domain — and which record tells the whole truth instead?

3. Guard 2 stands at the outer function's gate, yet its inequality is solved in terms of \(x\). What translation is happening there, and why does it sometimes split the domain into two branches?

4. Desmos graphed \(f(g(x))\) with the hidden restriction intact, no guards posted by you at all. What was the calculator doing that the simplified formula was not — and why does your written algebra still carry the burden of proof?
