Composition of Functions
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to evaluate compositions of functions at particular inputs, working from formulas and from tables.

  • I will be able to build and simplify the formulas for \((f \circ g)(x)\) and \((g \circ f)(x)\), and demonstrate that the order of composition matters.

  • I will be able to decompose a given function into an inner and an outer function, and explain why a decomposition is never unique.

📚 VOCABULARY

Composition: The operation that feeds one function's output into another function as its input, written \((f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))\) — from the Latin componere, “to place together.”

Composite function: The single new function that a composition produces. \(f \circ g\) is one function with its own formula, its own table, its own graph.

Inner function: The function applied first — \(g\) in \(f(g(x))\). It sits innermost in the notation and speaks first in the calculation.

Outer function: The function applied last — \(f\) in \(f(g(x))\). It receives whatever the inner function hands it, and has the final word.

Decomposition: Writing a complicated function as a composition of simpler ones — running the assembly line backward to see the stages.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

The word at the heart of this unit was coined by a man who wanted to mechanize thought itself. In a 1673 manuscript, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz — philosopher, diplomat, co-inventor of the calculus — wrote the Latin word functio to describe a quantity that performs a duty: a quantity whose value depends on, and answers to, another. Through his correspondence with Johann Bernoulli in the 1690s the word hardened into a mathematical term, and the modern idea took shape: a function is a rule of correspondence, a machine that receives an input and returns exactly one output. Yesterday you did arithmetic with these machines — adding, subtracting, multiplying, and dividing their outputs side by side. Today the machines stop working side by side and begin working in sequence: the output of one becomes the input of the next. This is composition, and it is the deepest way two functions can cooperate.

The notation is \((f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))\), read “\(f\) of \(g\) of \(x\).” Look carefully at the order, because it is the single most important convention of the week: the function written closest to \(x\) acts first. In \(f(g(x))\), the input \(x\) reports to \(g\); \(g\) computes and hands its answer to \(f\); \(f\) has the last word. There is a courtesy in the protocol: \(f\) never interrupts — it waits to hear exactly what \(g\) has said, and responds to that and nothing else.

The Protocol of Composition

\((f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))\): work from the inside out. The rightmost function in the symbol \(f \circ g\) — the innermost in the parentheses — acts first. To evaluate, resist every temptation to hurry: compute \(g(x)\) completely, then hand that finished number to \(f\). Composition is not multiplication: \(f \circ g\) and \(f \cdot g\) are entirely different constructions, and in general \(f \circ g \neq g \circ f\) — who speaks first changes the sentence.

Example 1

Let \(f(x) = x^2 + 1\) and \(g(x) = 3x - 2\). Evaluate \((f \circ g)(2)\) and \((g \circ f)(2)\).

Solution:

For \((f \circ g)(2)\), the inner function \(g\) speaks first:

\(g(2) = 3(2) - 2 = 4, \qquad f(4) = 4^2 + 1 = 17\)

For \((g \circ f)(2)\), the roles reverse — now \(f\) is inner:

\(f(2) = 2^2 + 1 = 5, \qquad g(5) = 3(5) - 2 = 13\)

Answer: \((f \circ g)(2) = 17\) and \((g \circ f)(2) = 13\). Same two machines, same starting input — different answers. The order of composition is not a formality; it is the whole architecture.

Example 2

For the same \(f\) and \(g\), build and simplify the formulas for \((f \circ g)(x)\) and \((g \circ f)(x)\), and check both against Example 1.

Solution:

To build \((f \circ g)(x)\), hand the entire expression \(g(x) = 3x - 2\) to \(f\) as its input — everywhere \(f\)'s rule says “input,” write \((3x-2)\), parentheses and all:

\((f \circ g)(x) = f(3x - 2) = (3x-2)^2 + 1 = 9x^2 - 12x + 4 + 1 = 9x^2 - 12x + 5\)

To build \((g \circ f)(x)\), hand \(f(x) = x^2 + 1\) to \(g\):

\((g \circ f)(x) = g(x^2 + 1) = 3(x^2 + 1) - 2 = 3x^2 + 1\)

Check at \(x = 2\):   \(9(4) - 12(2) + 5 = 36 - 24 + 5 = 17\) ✓   and   \(3(4) + 1 = 13\) ✓ — both formulas reproduce Example 1 exactly.

Answer: \((f \circ g)(x) = 9x^2 - 12x + 5\) and \((g \circ f)(x) = 3x^2 + 1\). Two genuinely different functions — one composition squares a linear expression, the other merely scales a square. Building the formula and then checking it at a point you already trust is the discipline: the formula is the claim, the point-check is the evidence.

Example 3

Functions defined only by tables compose just as well. Given the tables below, evaluate \((f \circ g)(1)\), \((g \circ f)(2)\), and \((f \circ f)(3)\).

\(\begin{array}{c|cccc} x & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\ \hline g(x) & 3 & 1 & 4 & 2 \end{array} \qquad\qquad \begin{array}{c|cccc} x & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\ \hline f(x) & 2 & 4 & 1 & 3 \end{array}\)

Solution:

A table composition is a relay: read the baton out of one table and carry it into the other.

\((f \circ g)(1)\):   \(g(1) = 3\), then \(f(3) = 1\)

\((g \circ f)(2)\):   \(f(2) = 4\), then \(g(4) = 2\)

\((f \circ f)(3)\):   \(f(3) = 1\), then \(f(1) = 2\)

Answer: \((f \circ g)(1) = 1\), \((g \circ f)(2) = 2\), \((f \circ f)(3) = 2\). Notice the third one: a function may perfectly well be composed with itself — run the machine, then feed its output straight back into its own hopper. Notation you will meet later writes this \(f^2 = f \circ f\), an exponent that counts applications, not multiplications — hold that thought until Thursday.

Example 4

Decompose \(h(x) = \sqrt{5x - 7}\) — find functions \(f\) and \(g\), neither equal to \(h\) itself, with \(h = f \circ g\). Then give a second, different decomposition of \(H(x) = (x^2 - 3)^5\).

Solution:

Read \(h\) as a sequence of duties performed on \(x\): first the input is quintupled and reduced by 7, then the square root is taken. The stage performed first is the inner function:

\(g(x) = 5x - 7 \;\;\text{(inner)}, \qquad f(x) = \sqrt{x} \;\;\text{(outer)}, \qquad f(g(x)) = \sqrt{5x-7} = h(x)\) ✓

For \(H(x) = (x^2 - 3)^5\), the obvious split is inner \(g(x) = x^2 - 3\) with outer \(f(x) = x^5\). But the assembly line can be cut at a different joint: take inner \(g(x) = x^2\) and outer \(f(x) = (x - 3)^5\). Check: \(f(g(x)) = (x^2 - 3)^5 = H(x)\) ✓.

Answer: \(h = \sqrt{x} \circ (5x - 7)\); and \(H\) decomposes both as \(x^5 \circ (x^2 - 3)\) and as \((x-3)^5 \circ x^2\). Decomposition is never unique — a chain can be cut at any link. The skill of choosing where to cut looks like a parlor trick this week; in calculus it becomes the chain rule's opening move, the most-used single skill of that entire course.

Why Order Matters: Composition Is Not Commutative

Addition and multiplication of functions inherited their manners from arithmetic: \(f + g = g + f\) because numbers commute. Composition inherits nothing, because it is not arithmetic at all — it is sequencing, and sequences care about order the way sentences care about word order.

  • “Square, then add one” and “add one, then square” are different instructions: at \(x = 3\) the first gives \(10\), the second gives \(16\).
  • Occasionally two functions do commute — you will meet an honest example in Lesson 4-7, and on Thursday you will meet the most important commuting pairs in mathematics: a function and its inverse, which undo each other in either order.

So never assume \(f \circ g = g \circ f\); when the two agree, that agreement is a fact to be proven, and it always signals that something structurally special is going on between the two functions.

⚠️ AI CHECKPOINT: THE TUTOR, SUPERVISED

Composition drills are exactly what an AI tutor is good for — provided the mathematician stays in charge. Open your AI tool and run this two-part session:

1. Order it to drill you. Prompt: “Make two small tables of values for functions f and g on the inputs 1 through 4, like a precalculus textbook, then ask me five composition evaluations such as (f∘g)(2) — but do not show me the answers until I give mine.” Work each one with the relay discipline of Example 3, then compare.

2. Then audit it. Give the AI this lesson's Example 1 functions and ask it for \((g \circ f)(2)\). Check its answer against your own hand computation (\(13\)). Order-of-composition is precisely the kind of convention an AI can fumble while sounding fluent — if it computed \(17\), it silently answered the other composition. Either way, write one sentence in your notes recording what you checked and what you found. The tutor drills; the mathematician verifies.

▶  Try it yourself: For \(f(x) = x^2 - 4\) and \(g(x) = 2x + 1\), build both composite formulas, then check each at \(x = 1\). Work it on paper before you peek.
\((f \circ g)(x) = (2x+1)^2 - 4 = 4x^2 + 4x + 1 - 4 = 4x^2 + 4x - 3\).   \((g \circ f)(x) = 2(x^2 - 4) + 1 = 2x^2 - 7\).   Check at \(x = 1\): the relay gives \(g(1) = 3\), \(f(3) = 5\), and the formula gives \(4 + 4 - 3 = 5\) ✓; the other way, \(f(1) = -3\), \(g(-3) = -5\), and the formula gives \(2 - 7 = -5\) ✓. Both formulas certified at a trusted point.
▶  Try it yourself: Decompose \(k(x) = \sqrt[3]{x^2 + 1}\) two different ways.
First cut: inner \(g(x) = x^2 + 1\), outer \(f(x) = \sqrt[3]{x}\), since \(f(g(x)) = \sqrt[3]{x^2+1}\) ✓. Second cut: inner \(g(x) = x^2\), outer \(f(x) = \sqrt[3]{x + 1}\), since \(f(g(x)) = \sqrt[3]{x^2 + 1}\) ✓. Same function, two honest assembly lines — the chain was cut at different links.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Reading \((f \circ g)(x)\) left to right and applying \(f\) first — or worse, treating \(f \circ g\) as the product \(f(x) \cdot g(x)\). They are three different objects. With Example 1's functions at \(x = 2\): the composition \((f \circ g)(2) = 17\), the reversed composition \((g \circ f)(2) = 13\), and the product \(f(2) \cdot g(2) = 5 \cdot 4 = 20\). Three constructions, three answers. The small circle \(\circ\) is hollow on purpose — nothing is being multiplied through it. Read every composition from the inside out, and when in doubt, say it aloud: “\(g\) first, then \(f\).”
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Leibniz named the function, but the symbol you write every day came later: it was Leonhard Euler, in a paper written in 1734, who first wrapped a function's input in parentheses after a single letter — \(f(x)\). One letter for the rule, one slot for the input: the notation is itself a little machine diagram, and it made compositions like \(f(g(x))\) almost write themselves. Good notation, Leibniz insisted all his life, does part of your thinking for you.

Conclusion

Yesterday's algebra of functions combined outputs side by side; today's composition chained the machines in sequence, and everything followed from one convention held with discipline: the inner function speaks first, and the outer function responds to exactly what it hears. You evaluated compositions from formulas and from tables, built and certified composite formulas, watched \(f \circ g\) and \(g \circ f\) come apart at a single point, and learned to cut a chain into stages — the decomposition skill that calculus will one day spend a full course rewarding. But one question was quietly postponed all lesson: when the inner function hands its output onward, is the outer function always able to receive it? What happens when \(g\) offers \(f\) a number that \(f\)'s domain refuses? Tomorrow that courtesy question gets a precise answer — and the composite functions you built today will have to declare, honestly, where they are allowed to live.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. In \(f(g(x))\), why does the function written closest to the input act first — and what breaks if you read the symbol left to right instead?

2. Why is \(f \circ g\) generally a different function from \(g \circ f\), and what should you suspect about two functions whenever the two compositions turn out to agree?

3. A composition can be evaluated from formulas, from tables, or one value at a time. What single relay discipline runs underneath all three?

4. Why is the decomposition of a function never unique — and what makes choosing a good cut a skill rather than a formality?
