Modeling with Composite Functions
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to translate a two-stage real-world process into a composition, identifying the inner and outer functions and tracking the units through the chain.

  • I will be able to evaluate and solve composite models in context — including finding when a chained quantity reaches a target value.

  • I will be able to determine whether two processes commute, and defend the answer algebraically rather than by feel.

📚 VOCABULARY

Mathematical model: A function built to describe a real process, whose inputs and outputs carry units and whose domain is bounded by the realities of the situation, not just the algebra.

Process chain: Two or more real processes run in sequence, each acting on the previous one's result — the real-world costume of \(f \circ g\).

Commute: Two processes commute when running them in either order produces the same result: \(f \circ g = g \circ f\). Rare, and always worth explaining when it happens.

Unit analysis: Tracking the units of measure through a chain — seconds into feet into square feet — to confirm that each function is being handed the kind of quantity it was built to receive.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Leibniz did not only name the function — he built one out of brass. Between 1673 and 1694 he designed and constructed the stepped reckoner, the first machine that could multiply and divide, its crank turning gear trains in which the output of each stage became, physically, the input of the next. He demonstrated it to the Royal Society in London in 1673, and he spent the rest of his life convinced that reasoning itself might one day be carried out by such chains of mechanism. Every composition you write today is a stepped reckoner on paper: stage one receives the raw input, stage two receives stage one's result, and the composite function is the whole machine viewed from outside — crank in, answer out. Today we build these machines from real processes: a discount followed by a tax, a ripple followed by an area, an hour of production followed by a bill.

One reading skill governs the whole lesson. When a story says “this happens, then that,” the word then is nesting instructions: the first process named is the inner function, the later process the outer. Story order runs inside-out, exactly opposite to the left-to-right order of the symbols — which is why careless translation is the most common modeling error of the week.

Example 1

A store offers 20% off every item, and Florida charges 6% sales tax. Let \(d(x) = 0.80x\) apply the discount to a price of \(x\) dollars and \(t(x) = 1.06x\) apply the tax. (a) Find \((t \circ d)(x)\) and \((d \circ t)(x)\), and test both on a \$50 sweater. (b) Now add a \$10-off coupon, \(c(x) = x - 10\). Compare \((t \circ c)(x)\) with \((c \circ t)(x)\) on the same sweater. Which order is better for the shopper, and by how much?

Solution:

(a) Discount first, then tax:

\((t \circ d)(x) = t(0.80x) = 1.06(0.80x) = 0.848x\)

Tax first, then discount:

\((d \circ t)(x) = d(1.06x) = 0.80(1.06x) = 0.848x\)

Identical — these two processes commute. On the \$50 sweater, both orders give \(0.848 \times 50 = \$42.40\): check the long way, \(d(50) = 40\), \(t(40) = 42.40\); and \(t(50) = 53\), \(d(53) = 42.40\) ✓. Why? Both processes are pure scalings, and multiplication of numbers commutes: \(1.06 \times 0.80 = 0.80 \times 1.06\). Yesterday's warning said that when compositions agree, something structural is going on — here it is.

(b) The coupon is not a scaling — it is a shift — and the truce ends immediately. Coupon first, then tax:

\((t \circ c)(x) = 1.06(x - 10) = 1.06x - 10.60\)

Tax first, then coupon:

\((c \circ t)(x) = 1.06x - 10\)

On the sweater: coupon-first gives \(t(40) = \$42.40\); tax-first gives \(53 - 10 = \$43.00\).

Answer: The scalings commute; the scaling and the shift do not. Coupon-before-tax is better for the shopper by \(\$0.60\) — and the algebra says exactly why: the two formulas differ only in \(10.60\) versus \(10\), a difference of \(0.06 \times 10\), the tax on the coupon itself. When the coupon is applied first, the \$10 it removes is never taxed. The composite formulas did not just compute the answer; they explained it.

Example 2

A pebble dropped in a still pond sends out a circular ripple whose radius grows at 1.5 feet per second: \(r(t) = 1.5t\) feet after \(t\) seconds. The area enclosed by a circle of radius \(r\) is \(A(r) = \pi r^2\) square feet. (a) Build \(A(r(t))\) and interpret it. (b) Find the enclosed area at \(t = 4\) seconds. (c) When does the ripple enclose 1000 square feet?

Solution:

(a) The story order: time makes a radius, then the radius makes an area. So \(r\) is inner, \(A\) is outer:

\(A(r(t)) = \pi (1.5t)^2 = 2.25\pi t^2 \;\text{ square feet after } t \text{ seconds}\)

Watch the units move through the chain: seconds enter \(r\), feet come out; feet enter \(A\), square feet come out. The composite swallows seconds and produces square feet directly — the middle unit, feet, lives only inside the machine.

(b) At \(t = 4\): the relay gives \(r(4) = 6\) feet, then \(A(6) = 36\pi \approx 113.1\) square feet; the composite formula agrees, \(2.25\pi(16) = 36\pi\) ✓.

(c) Set the composite equal to the target and solve:

\(2.25\pi t^2 = 1000\)

\(t^2 = \dfrac{1000}{2.25\pi} \approx 141.47\)

\(t \approx 11.9 \text{ seconds}\)

(The negative root is rejected — the model's domain is \(t \geq 0\), a guard posted by reality rather than by algebra.)

Answer: \(A(r(t)) = 2.25\pi t^2\); about \(113.1 \text{ ft}^2\) at four seconds; and the ripple encloses \(1000 \text{ ft}^2\) at \(t \approx 11.9\) seconds. Solving a composite model runs the machine in reverse gear — a first taste of the undoing that tomorrow's lesson makes precise.

Example 3

A workshop produces \(q(t) = 90t\) machined parts in \(t\) hours, and the daily cost of producing \(q\) parts is \(C(q) = 500 + 4q\) dollars. Build \(C(q(t))\), interpret both of its constants in context, and find how long production can run before the day's cost reaches \$3,740.

Solution:

Hours make parts, then parts make cost — \(q\) inner, \(C\) outer:

\(C(q(t)) = 500 + 4(90t) = 500 + 360t \;\text{ dollars after } t \text{ hours}\)

The 500 is the fixed cost — rent, heat, the lights — paid before the first part is cut. The 360 is dollars per hour, manufactured by the chain itself: \(4\) dollars per part \(\times\, 90\) parts per hour. Composition multiplied the two rates, and the units certify it: \(\frac{\$}{\text{part}} \times \frac{\text{parts}}{\text{hour}} = \frac{\$}{\text{hour}}\).

Now solve \(500 + 360t = 3740\):

\(360t = 3240 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; t = 9 \text{ hours}\)

Answer: \(C(q(t)) = 500 + 360t\); the budget lasts a 9-hour run. That rate-times-rate arithmetic — the chain's overall rate is the product of its stages' rates — is no coincidence. It is the shadow of the chain rule, the theorem at the center of the calculus you are one year away from, and Leibniz's \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) notation was designed so the rule would look like fractions cancelling. You have just computed your first one, without the ceremony.

📊 In Excel: The Chain in Columns 12 min

A spreadsheet makes composition physical: each column is a function, and each column feeds the next. Open a blank Excel workbook and rebuild Example 2:

  1. Column A: time. Put t (sec) in A1, then 0, 1, 2, …, 14 down the column.
  2. Column B: the inner function. Put r = 1.5t (ft) in B1 and the formula =1.5*A2 in B2; fill down. Column B is \(r(t)\).
  3. Column C: the outer function — and here is the point: its formula never mentions A. Put A = πr² (ft²) in C1 and =PI()*B2^2 in C2; fill down. Column C reads only from column B: the outer function hears only what the inner one says. The worksheet is \(A(r(t))\).
  4. Check the row \(t = 4\): it should read \(r = 6\) and \(A \approx 113.10\) — your Example 2 arithmetic, confirmed.
  5. Now hunt the 1000-ft² moment: scan column C. Row \(t = 11\) gives \(\approx 855.3\) and row \(t = 12\) gives \(\approx 1017.9\) — so the answer is bracketed between them. Insert rows for \(t = 11.1, 11.2, \ldots\) (or change the step) and tighten the bracket to \(t \approx 11.9\). The spreadsheet brackets; your algebra from Example 2(c) nails it exactly.

⚠️ AI CHECKPOINT: GRADE ITS PREDICTION

Before an AI computes for you, make it commit — then hold it to the standard of your own verified arithmetic. Run this session:

1. Demand a prediction. Prompt: “A \$10 coupon and a 6% sales tax are both applied to a purchase. Without computing anything, predict: does the order matter? If so, which order is cheaper for the customer, and why?” Make it answer before any arithmetic appears.

2. Grade the prediction against your Example 1(b) work — the coupon-first order is cheaper by exactly the tax on the coupon, \$0.60 on any purchase where the coupon applies. Did the AI get the direction right? Did it get the reason right, or did it produce the right answer wrapped in vague reasoning? Both failures are worth catching; the second is sneakier.

3. Then put it to work. Ask it to invent two more everyday two-stage process chains (currency exchange and a flat fee; a percentage raise and a fixed bonus…). For each, you write the two functions, compose them in both orders, and decide whether they commute. The AI supplies raw material; the mathematician runs the mill.

▶  Try it yourself: A bank converts dollars to euros at \(e(d) = 0.90d\), and charges a flat 2-euro service fee, \(s(e) = e - 2\). A traveler exchanges \$150. Compute the result of fee-after-conversion, \((s \circ e)(d)\), and of converting after a 2-dollar fee, \((e \circ f)(d)\) with \(f(d) = d - 2\). Which arrangement does the bank prefer?
Fee after conversion: \((s \circ e)(d) = 0.90d - 2\), so \(0.90(150) - 2 = 135 - 2 = 133\) euros. Fee before conversion: \((e \circ f)(d) = 0.90(d - 2) = 0.90d - 1.80\), so \(0.90(148) = 133.20\) euros. The formulas differ by \(0.20\) — euro-fee costs the traveler \(2\) euros, dollar-fee costs only \(0.90 \times 2 = 1.80\) euros' worth. The bank prefers charging the fee in euros, after conversion — same structure as the coupon: a shift and a scaling refuse to commute, and the difference is the scaling applied to the shift.
▶  Try it yourself: A balloon's radius grows as \(r(t) = 4 + 0.25t\) centimeters after \(t\) seconds of inflation, and a circle's circumference is \(C(r) = 2\pi r\). Build \(C(r(t))\), and find the circumference of the balloon's equator at \(t = 8\).
\(C(r(t)) = 2\pi(4 + 0.25t) = 8\pi + 0.5\pi t\) centimeters. At \(t = 8\): the relay gives \(r(8) = 6\), then \(C(6) = 12\pi \approx 37.7\) cm; the formula gives \(8\pi + 4\pi = 12\pi\) ✓. Units through the chain: seconds \(\rightarrow\) centimeters \(\rightarrow\) centimeters — and the composite's rate, \(0.5\pi \approx 1.57\) cm of circumference per second, is again the product of the stage rates: \(2\pi \times 0.25\).
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Translating story order into symbol order. The story says “the discount is applied, then the tax” — and the careless hand writes \(d(t(x))\) because \(d\) was named first. Backward: the process performed first is the inner function, so discount-then-tax is \(t(d(x))\). The symbols read outside-in while the story reads first-to-last. Two defenses: say the chain aloud with arrows — \(x \xrightarrow{\;d\;} \text{sale price} \xrightarrow{\;t\;} \text{total}\) — before writing any parentheses; and run one unit check, since a function handed the wrong kind of quantity (taxing a radius, squaring a price) is usually caught by its units before it is caught by its arithmetic.

Conclusion

Composition came off the page today. A discount and a tax turned out to commute — and the algebra located the structural reason, two scalings — while a coupon broke the truce by exactly the tax on itself. A ripple chained seconds into feet into square feet; a workshop multiplied dollars-per-part by parts-per-hour and handed you the chain rule's calling card a year early; and the Excel columns made the whole idea physical — each column listening only to the column before it, the worksheet itself a stepped reckoner. Twice today you also ran a chain backward: from a target area to a time, from a budget to a shift length. Each time, undoing the machine took an ad hoc scramble of algebra. Tomorrow that scramble becomes a concept with a name: the inverse function, the machine built to run another machine's work exactly in reverse — and this week's Focus assignment will ask you to certify such machines with full ceremony.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. When a story says “this happens, then that,” how do you decide which process is the inner function — and why does the symbol order run opposite to the story order?

2. The discount and the tax commuted; the coupon and the tax did not. What structural difference between a scaling and a shift explains both facts at once?

3. What does unit analysis certify about a composition that checking the arithmetic alone cannot?

4. In the workshop model, the composite's rate was the product of the two stages' rates. Why does chaining processes multiply their rates — and what famous theorem is this the first shadow of?
