📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
This set drills the two-guard discipline from Lesson 4-6: composite domains found from the pedigree, hidden restrictions dug back out of simplified formulas, guards that split domains into branches, and ranges followed honestly through the chain. On every problem, post the guards before you simplify — the order of operations here is the whole method. This is a practice set: scored for honest completion, with worked solutions released after the due date for line-by-line self-correction.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
A simplified formula smiles and says “all real numbers.” Its pedigree says otherwise. Which do you believe — and why?
📝 PROBLEM SET

Show your work for every problem in neat vertical form — one step per line, equals signs aligned, final answers labeled. For every composition: Guard 1, Guard 2, then simplify — in that order, labeled. Domains in interval or set notation, exact values throughout.

1
Warm-up: For \(f(x) = \sqrt{x}\) and \(g(x) = 2x - 10\), write \((f \circ g)(x)\), post both guards, and state the domain of \(f \circ g\).
2
The formula with amnesia: For \(f(x) = x^2\) and \(g(x) = \sqrt{4 - x}\), build and simplify \((f \circ g)(x)\). State ① the domain the simplified formula appears to have, ② the true domain from the pedigree, and ③ the true range, followed through the chain from the true domain.
3
Both orders: For \(f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}\) and \(g(x) = x - 3\), find \((f \circ g)(x)\) with its domain and \((g \circ f)(x)\) with its domain. One sentence: why do the two domains differ, when the parents are the same two functions?
4
One guard visible, one hidden: For \(f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x - 2}\) and \(g(x) = \dfrac{5}{x}\), post both guards, then build and simplify \((f \circ g)(x)\). Identify which excluded value the simplified formula still shows and which it has forgotten — then certify your formula with a point-check at \(x = 1\), relay against formula.
5
A domain in two branches: For \(f(x) = \sqrt{x - 1}\) and \(g(x) = x^2\), find the domain of \(f \circ g\). (Guard 2 is a quadratic inequality — solve it honestly, and expect the domain to split.) Write \((f \circ g)(x)\) and its domain in interval notation.
6
Explain why: In three or four sentences, explain why the domain of a composite function cannot be read off its simplified formula. Your explanation should name the two guards, say which one the simplification tends to erase, and use the phrase domain of honesty as this course means it: the formula must carry the memory of where it came from.
7
Honors stretch: Let \(f(x) = \sqrt{3 - x}\). Find the domain of \(f \circ f\) — the function composed with itself. (Guard 1 restricts \(x\); Guard 2 demands that \(f(x)\) itself land back in \(f\)'s domain, which becomes an inequality in a radical. Solve it with Unit 3 discipline, and state the domain as a single closed interval. Check both endpoints by running the relay.)
💡 CHECK YOURSELF WITH DESMOS
After solving on paper, verify problems 2 and 5 in Desmos (desmos.com/calculator) exactly as the lesson taught: define f(x) and g(x) on their own lines, graph y = f(g(x)), and read the graph's left and right edges against your domain. For problem 2, also graph the naïve simplification and watch it sail past where the true composite stops — the gap between the two graphs is your answer to ②, drawn in pixels. The calculator confirms; your algebra convinces.
