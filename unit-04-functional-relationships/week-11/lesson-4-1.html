Functions and Rates of Change Revisited
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to determine a function's domain and range from a formula, a graph, or a table, and say which representation showed me the restriction.

  • I will be able to compute the average rate of change of a function over an interval from any representation, with correct units attached.

  • I will be able to interpret an average rate of change in context, and explain why it varies from interval to interval for every function that is not linear.

📚 VOCABULARY

Average rate of change: The change in a function's output divided by the change in its input over an interval: \(\dfrac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}\). It answers the question how fast, on average, did the output move between these two inputs?

Delta (Δ) notation: The Greek letter Δ (capital delta, for difference) abbreviates “change in”: \(\Delta y = f(b) - f(a)\) and \(\Delta x = b - a\), so the average rate of change is \(\Delta y / \Delta x\). You have written this ratio before — it is a slope.

Interval: The stretch of inputs between two values, written \([a, b]\). An average rate of change is meaningless until you name the interval it averages over.

Representation: A way of presenting a function — formula, graph, table, or verbal description. One function, several portraits; the mathematics must come out the same from every one.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

The word at the head of this unit has a birthday too. In the 1690s, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz — philosopher, diplomat, co-inventor of the calculus — needed a name for quantities that depend on other quantities, the way the area of a circle depends on its radius or a curve's height depends on where you stand. He chose the Latin functio, “a performing.” A function performs: feed it an input, and it executes its rule. For three units you have collected these performers one family at a time — polynomial, rational, exponential, logarithmic, radical, piecewise. Unit 3 closed by setting two functions side by side, comparing their features and finding where they intersect. Unit 4 goes further: for the next three weeks, functions stop performing solo. We study them in relationship — combined, chained, and undone — and it is a courteous business: every combination we build will succeed only where both partners are respected. But before functions can relate to each other, we need two skills sharpened to a fine edge: reading any function from any representation, and measuring how fast it changes.

Example 1

State the domain of \(f(x) = \dfrac{\sqrt{x - 2}}{x - 5}\), and name which part of the formula posts each restriction.

Solution:

Two guards from two familiar families. The even-index radical demands \(x - 2 \geq 0\), so \(x \geq 2\). The denominator demands \(x - 5 \neq 0\), so \(x \neq 5\). Both must be satisfied at once.

Answer: Domain \([2, 5) \cup (5, \infty)\). This is Unit 3's domain of honesty doing its standing work: the formula tells you exactly where it is willing to perform, and everything in this unit — every rate, every combination — will be computed only on ground where the function actually stands.

Example 2

Compute the average rate of change of \(f(x) = x^2 - 3x\) on the interval \([2, 5]\).

Solution:

Evaluate at both endpoints first, then divide the changes:

\(f(5) = 25 - 15 = 10, \qquad f(2) = 4 - 6 = -2\)

\(\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \dfrac{f(5) - f(2)}{5 - 2} = \dfrac{10 - (-2)}{3} = \dfrac{12}{3} = 4\)

Answer: The average rate of change is \(4\) — output units per input unit. Geometrically, you have computed the slope of the line through the two points \((2, -2)\) and \((5, 10)\) on the curve. Keep the subtraction in the same order top and bottom: \(f(5) - f(2)\) over \(5 - 2\), never mixed.

Example 3

A cup of coffee cools on a desk. The table records its temperature:

\(\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c} t \text{ (min)} & 0 & 5 & 10 & 20 \\ \hline T \text{ (}^\circ\text{F)} & 190 & 160 & 137 & 110 \end{array}\)

Compute the average rate of change on \([0, 10]\) and on \([10, 20]\), and interpret both.

Solution:

On \([0, 10]\): \(\dfrac{137 - 190}{10 - 0} = \dfrac{-53}{10} = -5.3\ ^\circ\text{F per minute}\)

On \([10, 20]\): \(\dfrac{110 - 137}{20 - 10} = \dfrac{-27}{10} = -2.7\ ^\circ\text{F per minute}\)

Answer: The coffee loses heat at an average of \(5.3\ ^\circ\)F per minute over the first ten minutes, but only \(2.7\ ^\circ\)F per minute over the next ten: cooling slows as the coffee approaches room temperature. Notice everything a table demands of you: the units come from the labels (degrees per minute, because output is \(^\circ\)F and input is minutes), and the negative sign is information, not decoration — it reports the direction of change. An average rate of change without units, in a context that has them, is an unfinished answer.

Example 4

A graph of a function \(g\) passes through the points \((-1, 4)\) and \((3, -2)\). Find the average rate of change of \(g\) on \([-1, 3]\).

Solution:

\(\dfrac{g(3) - g(-1)}{3 - (-1)} = \dfrac{-2 - 4}{4} = \dfrac{-6}{4} = -\dfrac{3}{2}\)

Answer: \(-\dfrac{3}{2}\). From a graph, the recipe is: read the two points, then slope. Draw the line segment connecting them and you can see the answer — a line tilting down 3 for every 2 across. Formula, table, graph: three representations, one computation, every time.

One Computation, Three Disguises

The average rate of change of \(f\) on \([a, b]\) is

\(\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \dfrac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}\)

  • From a formula: evaluate \(f\) at both endpoints, then divide the changes.
  • From a table: read the two rows, then divide the changes — and take your units from the column labels.
  • From a graph: read the two points, then compute the slope of the line through them.

It is always the slope of the line joining two points of the curve — which is why a linear function is precisely the function whose average rate of change is the same on every interval: for \(f(x) = mx + b\), the computation returns \(m\) no matter which \(a\) and \(b\) you pick. For every other function in your collection, the answer depends on the interval — and that dependence is the whole story of this week.

Example 5

Compute the average rate of change of \(f(x) = 2^x\) on \([0, 2]\), \([2, 4]\), and \([4, 6]\). What pattern appears?

Solution:

On \([0, 2]\): \(\dfrac{4 - 1}{2} = 1.5 \qquad\) On \([2, 4]\): \(\dfrac{16 - 4}{2} = 6 \qquad\) On \([4, 6]\): \(\dfrac{64 - 16}{2} = 24\)

Answer: Each average rate of change is four times the one before — exactly the factor by which \(2^x\) itself grows over each two-step interval. An exponential function's speed grows in the same proportion as the function: the bigger it is, the faster it gets bigger. You met this behavior in Unit 3 as the signature of exponential growth; now you can state it as a fact about rates. Hold that thought for the Excel table below — it is about to become something you can see.

💻 In Desmos: The Slope Between Two Points on a Curve 6 min

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), rebuild Example 2 so the picture and the number agree:

  1. Graph f(x) = x^2 - 3x.
  2. Plot the two endpoint points by typing (2, f(2)) and (5, f(5)) — Desmos evaluates the function for you and pins both points to the curve.
  3. Type m = (f(5) - f(2)) / (5 - 2). Desmos reports \(m = 4\) — your Example 2 answer, computed by the same arithmetic you did by hand.
  4. Now draw the line through the points: y = f(2) + m(x - 2). It cuts straight through the parabola's two pinned points — the average rate of change, visible as a tilt.
  5. Edit the \(5\)s to \(3\)s and watch both the line and \(m\) change: same function, different interval, different answer. The calculator confirms; your algebra convinces.

📊 In Excel: A Rate-of-Change Machine 10 min

Open a blank Excel workbook from your school Microsoft account and build a table that computes average rates of change for you:

  1. Column A: inputs. Put 0 in A2, =A2+1 in A3, and fill down to \(x = 10\).
  2. Column B: outputs. In B2 type =A2^2 and fill down — the squaring function, tabulated.
  3. Column C: rates. In C3 type =(B3-B2)/(A3-A2) — the average rate of change over each one-step interval — and fill down.

Read column C: 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, … — the odd numbers, marching in order. The squaring function's rate climbs by exactly 2 with every step. Now change B2 to =2^A2 and fill down again. Column C now reads 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, … — look closely: the rate column has reproduced the function itself, one row early. The speed of \(2^x\) is a copy of \(2^x\). No other kind of function does this, and the mystery of a function whose rate of change is itself leads directly to the number \(e\) you met in Unit 3 — and to a course you have not taken yet.

▶  Try it yourself: Find the average rate of change of \(f(x) = x^2 + 2x\) on \([1, 4]\). Work it on paper before you peek.
\(f(4) = 16 + 8 = 24\) and \(f(1) = 1 + 2 = 3\), so \(\dfrac{f(4) - f(1)}{4 - 1} = \dfrac{24 - 3}{3} = \dfrac{21}{3} = 7\). The line through \((1, 3)\) and \((4, 24)\) has slope 7.
▶  Try it yourself: A function \(g\) has domain \([0, \infty)\) on its graph, but its table only shows inputs 1 through 6, and its formula is \(g(x) = \sqrt{x}\). Which representation told the whole truth about the domain — and what did each of the others hide or suggest?
The formula tells the whole truth: \(\sqrt{x}\) demands \(x \geq 0\), so the domain is exactly \([0, \infty)\). The graph shows the same thing but only as far as the viewing window extends — you must trust the pattern to continue. The table is the least honest witness: it reports six inputs and stays silent about all others; a table shows samples, never the domain. When representations disagree in what they reveal, the formula is the deed of ownership; graph and table are photographs of the property.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Mismatching the order of subtraction: computing \(\dfrac{f(b) - f(a)}{a - b}\), with the outputs in one order and the inputs in the other. The result has the wrong sign — a cooling coffee reported as warming, a falling stone reported as rising. Choose an order and keep it in both numerator and denominator: second point minus first point, upstairs and down. The sign of an average rate of change is not cosmetic; it is the direction of the change, and in context it is often the most important part of the answer.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Leibniz named the function in the 1690s, but the familiar notation \(f(x)\) is younger than the calculus itself: Leonhard Euler introduced it in a paper written in 1734. For four decades, mathematicians did calculus on functions they had no compact way to write. Notation is not decoration — a good symbol is a thought made portable, and \(f(x)\) made the function a thing you could hold, rename, and — as you will see all this unit — combine with other functions like a number.

Conclusion

Today re-sharpened two old blades. Domain and range answer where a function performs, and the formula is the final authority on it — the domain of honesty, still on duty in a new unit. The average rate of change answers how fast the performance moves, and it is one computation wearing three disguises: the slope of the line through two points of the curve, whether those points come from a formula, a table, or a graph. You saw that only linear functions keep one speed forever, that a parabola's rate climbs through the odd numbers, and that an exponential's rate is a copy of itself — a fact strange enough that Excel had to show it to you twice. But everything today measured change between two points, and the interval was always specific: \([2,5]\), \([0,10]\), somewhere in particular. Tomorrow we ask the more ambitious question: can one piece of algebra compute the average rate of change over every interval at once? It can. It is called the difference quotient, it is four moves of pure algebra, and it is the doorstep of the calculus.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Formula, graph, table: what does each representation reveal honestly about a function's domain, and what does each one hide?

2. The average rate of change is “one computation in three disguises.” What is the one computation, and why is it always a slope?

3. Why is a linear function the only kind whose average rate of change ignores the interval — and what does a changing rate tell you about a graph's shape?

4. The Excel rate column for \(2^x\) reproduced the function itself. What might it mean for a function to be its own rate of change — and which number from Unit 3 is hiding in that question?
