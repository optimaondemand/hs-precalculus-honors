The Difference Quotient
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to write the difference quotient \(\dfrac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}\) for a given function and explain what each of its three parts means.

  • I will be able to simplify the difference quotient completely — through the four moves of substitute, expand, subtract, and divide — for polynomial, rational, and radical functions.

  • I will be able to verify difference-quotient algebra line by line, my own or a machine's, and recognize the warning signs of a slip before checking a single step.

📚 VOCABULARY

Difference quotient: The expression \(\dfrac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}\) — a difference of outputs divided by (quotient) the difference of inputs. It is yesterday's average rate of change computed on the interval \([x, x+h]\): every interval at once, held in algebra.

Increment: The small step \(h\) added to the input — from Latin incrementum, “growth.” Throughout today, \(h \neq 0\): an interval of zero width is not an interval.

Binomial expansion: Writing out a power of a two-term sum: \((x+h)^2 = x^2 + 2xh + h^2\), \((x+h)^3 = x^3 + 3x^2h + 3xh^2 + h^3\). The middle terms — the cross terms — are where the mathematics lives, and where careless hands (and careless machines) drop it.

Conjugate: For an expression \(\sqrt{A} - \sqrt{B}\), the companion \(\sqrt{A} + \sqrt{B}\). Multiplying the two clears the radicals — \((\sqrt{A} - \sqrt{B})(\sqrt{A} + \sqrt{B}) = A - B\) — the key that unlocks radical difference quotients.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Yesterday's rate computations all had the same shape: pick two inputs, subtract the outputs, divide by the gap. Leibniz, building his calculus in the 1670s, saw that this shape deserved a notation of its own. He wrote the change in the output as a difference — what we now write \(\Delta y\) — and the change in the input likewise, and he designed his symbols so that the notation would do part of the thinking: a well-made symbol, he believed, relieves the mind the way a well-made tool relieves the hand. Today we build the tool he was reaching for. Instead of computing the average rate of change on \([2, 5]\), then again on \([0,10]\), then again forever, we compute it once, in general: on the interval from \(x\) to \(x + h\), where \(x\) is any starting input and \(h\) is any nonzero step. The two endpoints have outputs \(f(x)\) and \(f(x+h)\), the gap between the inputs is exactly \(h\), and the average rate of change is

\(\dfrac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{(x + h) - x} = \dfrac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}\)

This is the difference quotient, and simplifying it is a four-move discipline: substitute (build \(f(x+h)\) by replacing every \(x\) in the rule), expand (multiply everything out, cross terms and all), subtract (take away all of \(f(x)\), distributing the minus sign over every term), and divide (factor \(h\) from what survives, and cancel it). Four moves of algebra you already own — arranged into the single most consequential computation you will meet before calculus.

Example 1

Simplify the difference quotient for the linear function \(f(x) = 3x + 5\).

Solution:

Substitute: \(f(x+h) = 3(x+h) + 5 = 3x + 3h + 5\).

Subtract: \(f(x+h) - f(x) = (3x + 3h + 5) - (3x + 5) = 3h\).

Divide:

\(\dfrac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} = \dfrac{3h}{h} = 3\)

Answer: The difference quotient is \(3\) — no \(x\), no \(h\), just the slope. A line changes at one rate on every interval, and the algebra says so by erasing both variables. Yesterday's claim, now a two-line proof.

Example 2

Simplify the difference quotient for \(f(x) = x^2 - 4x + 1\).

Solution:

Substitute and expand:

\(f(x+h) = (x+h)^2 - 4(x+h) + 1 = x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - 4x - 4h + 1\)

Subtract — the minus sign visits every term of \(f(x)\):

\(f(x+h) - f(x) = \left(x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - 4x - 4h + 1\right) - \left(x^2 - 4x + 1\right)\)

\(= 2xh + h^2 - 4h\)

Every term without an \(h\) has cancelled — \(x^2\) against \(-x^2\), \(-4x\) against \(+4x\), \(1\) against \(-1\). This is not luck; it is a law you will explain on the Focus set.

Divide — factor \(h\) first, then cancel:

\(\dfrac{2xh + h^2 - 4h}{h} = \dfrac{h(2x + h - 4)}{h} = 2x + h - 4\)

Answer: \(2x + h - 4\). Sanity check with yesterday's tools: on \([2, 5]\) we have \(x = 2\), \(h = 3\), so the formula predicts \(2(2) + 3 - 4 = 3\). Directly: \(f(5) = 25 - 20 + 1 = 6\), \(f(2) = 4 - 8 + 1 = -3\), and \(\frac{6 - (-3)}{3} = 3\). ✓ One expression, every interval.

Example 3

Simplify the difference quotient for \(f(x) = x^3\).

Solution:

Substitute and expand — the cube of a binomial has four terms, coefficients \(1, 3, 3, 1\):

\((x+h)^3 = x^3 + 3x^2h + 3xh^2 + h^3\)

Subtract:

\(f(x+h) - f(x) = 3x^2h + 3xh^2 + h^3\)

Divide:

\(\dfrac{h(3x^2 + 3xh + h^2)}{h} = 3x^2 + 3xh + h^2\)

Answer: \(3x^2 + 3xh + h^2\). Notice the shape of the work: the lone \(x^3\) cancels in the subtraction, and everything that survives wears an \(h\). Expand the cube fully every time — writing \((x+h)^3 = x^3 + h^3\) is the same crime as writing \((x+h)^2 = x^2 + h^2\), only one degree more expensive.

Example 4

Simplify the difference quotient for the rational function \(f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}\).

Solution:

Substitute and subtract — combine the fractions over a common denominator:

\(f(x+h) - f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x+h} - \dfrac{1}{x} = \dfrac{x - (x+h)}{x(x+h)} = \dfrac{-h}{x(x+h)}\)

Divide — dividing by \(h\) means multiplying by \(\frac{1}{h}\):

\(\dfrac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} = \dfrac{-h}{x(x+h)} \cdot \dfrac{1}{h} = \dfrac{-1}{x(x+h)}\)

Answer: \(\dfrac{-1}{x(x+h)}\). The negative sign is the graph of \(1/x\) confessing that it falls wherever it is defined. Check numerically: from \(x = 1\) to \(x = 2\) (so \(h = 1\)): directly, \(\frac{1/2 - 1}{1} = -\frac{1}{2}\); by formula, \(\frac{-1}{1 \cdot 2} = -\frac{1}{2}\). ✓

Example 5

Simplify the difference quotient for \(f(x) = \sqrt{x}\).

Solution:

Substitute:

\(\dfrac{\sqrt{x+h} - \sqrt{x}}{h}\)

No terms cancel and no \(h\) factors out — the radicals lock the numerator shut. The key is the conjugate: multiply top and bottom by \(\sqrt{x+h} + \sqrt{x}\), which changes the expression's form but not its value:

\(\dfrac{\sqrt{x+h} - \sqrt{x}}{h} \cdot \dfrac{\sqrt{x+h} + \sqrt{x}}{\sqrt{x+h} + \sqrt{x}} = \dfrac{(x + h) - x}{h\left(\sqrt{x+h} + \sqrt{x}\right)} = \dfrac{h}{h\left(\sqrt{x+h} + \sqrt{x}\right)}\)

Divide:

\(= \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x+h} + \sqrt{x}}\)

Answer: \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x+h} + \sqrt{x}}\). The conjugate did for the numerator exactly what expanding did for the polynomials: it forced the hidden \(h\) into the open so it could cancel. Check: from \(x = 1\) to \(x = 4\) (so \(h = 3\)): directly, \(\frac{2 - 1}{3} = \frac{1}{3}\); by formula, \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{4} + \sqrt{1}} = \frac{1}{3}\). ✓

The Law of the Surviving h

In every example, the same event: after the subtraction, every surviving term carried a factor of \(h\) — and so \(h\) factored out and cancelled cleanly. This is a law, not a coincidence:

  • Set \(h = 0\) in \(f(x+h) - f(x)\) and you get \(f(x) - f(x) = 0\). The whole numerator vanishes when \(h\) does — so every piece of it must be carried by \(h\). Any term that could survive \(h = 0\) would contradict the subtraction that built it.
  • Therefore a stray \(h\)-free term is an alarm bell. If, mid-computation, your numerator contains a term with no \(h\) in it, an error lives upstream — a dropped cross term, an undistributed minus sign — and no amount of downstream work will save the answer. Stop and audit.

This law is your quality control, and it works on anyone's algebra — yours, a classmate's, or a machine's. The finished, simplified form tells you the four moves were executed: everything cancelled that should, and \(h\) left the denominator on its own power, by algebra, not by wishful thinking.

⚠️ AI CHECKPOINT: THE MISSING CROSS TERM

A student asked an AI to simplify the difference quotient for \(f(x) = x^2 - 3x + 2\). It answered, fluently:

“Substitute: f(x+h) = (x+h)² − 3(x+h) + 2 = x² + h² − 3x − 3h + 2. Subtract f(x): everything without an h cancels, leaving h² − 3h. Divide by h: the difference quotient simplifies to h − 3.”

Audit it line by line before reading on. Line 1 sets up the substitution correctly — but expands it falsely: \((x+h)^2 = x^2 + 2xh + h^2\), and the AI wrote \(x^2 + h^2\). The cross term \(2xh\) is gone. Every line after is flawless arithmetic performed on a corpse. The correct run: \(f(x+h) = x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - 3x - 3h + 2\); subtracting gives \(2xh + h^2 - 3h\); dividing gives \(\mathbf{2x + h - 3}\).

Now notice what you could have seen without checking a single line. The AI's answer \(h - 3\) contains no \(x\) — it claims this function changes at the same average rate on every interval, wherever you stand. But only lines do that (Example 1), and \(f\) is a parabola. The answer's shape convicted it before the audit began. Quick numeric second witness: from \(x = 0\) to \(x = 1\), directly, \(\frac{f(1) - f(0)}{1} = \frac{0 - 2}{1} = -2\); the true formula gives \(2(0) + 1 - 3 = -2\) ✓; the AI's gives \(1 - 3 = -2\) — it even passes one spot check! At \(x = 2\), \(h = 1\): directly \(\frac{f(3) - f(2)}{1} = \frac{2 - 0}{1} = 2\); true formula: \(4 + 1 - 3 = 2\) ✓; AI: \(-2\) ✗. One passing test proves nothing; the machine's confidence proves less.

The lesson for the supervising mathematician: in difference-quotient work, the expansion line is where the money is — audit the cross terms and the distributed minus sign first, and use the shape of the answer (should it contain \(x\)?) as a free pre-check. On this week's Focus set you will audit a fresh transcript with a different slip and write the examiner's report yourself.

▶  Try it yourself: Simplify the difference quotient for \(f(x) = x^2 + 1\). All four moves on paper before you peek.
\(f(x+h) = (x+h)^2 + 1 = x^2 + 2xh + h^2 + 1\). Subtract: \(f(x+h) - f(x) = 2xh + h^2\) — the \(x^2\) and the 1 both cancel. Divide: \(\frac{h(2x + h)}{h} = 2x + h\). Every surviving term carried its \(h\); the law held.
▶  Try it yourself: Simplify the difference quotient for \(f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x+2}\), following Example 4's fraction technique.
\(f(x+h) - f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x+h+2} - \dfrac{1}{x+2} = \dfrac{(x+2) - (x+h+2)}{(x+h+2)(x+2)} = \dfrac{-h}{(x+h+2)(x+2)}\). Divide by \(h\): \(\dfrac{-1}{(x+h+2)(x+2)}\). Same shape as \(1/x\), shifted — the transformation traveled through the whole computation.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Writing \(f(x+h)\) as \(f(x) + h\). The notation \(f(x+h)\) commands you to feed the entire quantity \(x + h\) through the rule — replace every \(x\), then expand — not to compute \(f(x)\) and append an \(h\) to the result. For \(f(x) = x^2\): \(f(x+h) = (x+h)^2 = x^2 + 2xh + h^2\), while \(f(x) + h = x^2 + h\) — different objects entirely. If your numerator ever reads \(f(x) + h - f(x) = h\) and your difference quotient “simplifies” to 1 for every function, this is the error underneath.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The coefficients in your binomial expansions — \(1, 2, 1\) for the square and \(1, 3, 3, 1\) for the cube — are rows of the triangle named for Blaise Pascal, who studied it in the 1650s; Chinese and Persian mathematicians, including Omar Khayyam, knew the pattern centuries earlier. Each row is the shoulders the next stands on: every entry is the sum of the two above it. When you expand \((x+h)^4\) someday and need \(1, 4, 6, 4, 1\), the triangle will hand you the row — and the cross terms, the ones no honest expansion may drop, are everything between the two 1s.

Conclusion

Today you built the general instrument: the difference quotient \(\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}\), the average rate of change on every interval at once, simplified by four disciplined moves — substitute, expand, subtract, divide. Each function family demanded its own craftsmanship: polynomials wanted full expansions with every cross term intact, rational functions wanted common denominators, radicals wanted the conjugate — and all of them obeyed the law of the surviving \(h\), your built-in quality control. You also watched a fluent machine drop a cross term and reason beautifully ever after, and you learned the auditor's order of operations: check the answer's shape first, the expansion line second, everything else third. What we have not yet done is use the thing. The simplified forms you produced today — \(2x + h - 4\), \(3x^2 + 3xh + h^2\), \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt{x}}\) — are machines waiting for inputs. Tomorrow we feed them: evaluate at real points, shrink \(h\) toward zero, and watch a line through two points swing itself into a line that touches at one. Tomorrow the doorstep of the calculus becomes a doorway.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. The difference quotient is yesterday's average rate of change “computed once, in general.” What exactly do \(x\), \(h\), and the quotient each stand for?

2. Why must every term that survives the subtraction \(f(x+h) - f(x)\) carry a factor of \(h\) — and what does a stray \(h\)-free term prove about the work above it?

3. The conjugate multiplication in Example 5 changed the expression's form but not its value. Why is that legal, and what did it accomplish that expansion accomplished for polynomials?

4. The AI's answer was wrong in a way its shape revealed before any line-checking. What should a difference quotient's simplified form look like, and which features of an answer deserve the auditor's first glance?
