The Algebra of Functions
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to combine two functions with the four operations — forming \(f+g\), \(f-g\), \(fg\), and \(f/g\) — from formulas, tables, and graphs.

  • I will be able to determine the domain of a combined function as the common ground of its partners, with the quotient's extra exclusion where the denominator vanishes.

  • I will be able to solve problems with combined functions in context, including profit as the difference of revenue and cost.

📚 VOCABULARY

Arithmetic combination: A new function built from two others by an arithmetic operation: \((f+g)(x) = f(x) + g(x)\), \((f-g)(x) = f(x) - g(x)\), \((fg)(x) = f(x) \cdot g(x)\), \(\left(\tfrac{f}{g}\right)(x) = \tfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\). The operation happens to the outputs, input by input.

Intersection of domains: The set of inputs belonging to both functions' domains — the common ground. Written \(D_f \cap D_g\); the symbol \(\cap\) is read “intersect.”

Excluded value: An input barred from a combination's domain — either because one partner is undefined there, or (for quotients only) because the denominator function outputs zero there.

Break-even point: In business models, the quantity at which profit \(P = R - C\) equals zero — revenue and cost in exact balance, the difference function's zero.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

The word algebra is nine centuries older than the functions it now governs. It comes from al-jabr — “the restoration” — in the title of al-Khwarizmi's ninth-century treatise, where it named a concrete move: restoring a subtracted quantity to the other side of an equation. Algebra was the craft of operating on numbers hidden behind symbols. But once Euler's notation let a whole function be held in a single letter, a quiet question became possible: if \(f\) and \(g\) can be written like numbers, can they be added like numbers? Multiplied? Divided? They can — and today, with no calculator and no machinery beyond pencil and paper, we extend the oldest operations in mathematics to the newest objects in your course. It is a pure algebra day, and it is also a lesson in courtesy: two functions can only work together where both are willing to work at all.

The definitions are exactly what fairness suggests. To add two functions, add their outputs at each input: \((f+g)(x) = f(x) + g(x)\). Likewise for subtraction, multiplication, and division. Each combination is a genuine new function — with a rule you can simplify, a graph you can draw, and, crucially, a domain you must determine, because a combination is only defined where every ingredient is.

Example 1

Let \(f(x) = 3x - 2\) and \(g(x) = x^2 + x\). Find \((f+g)(x)\), \((f-g)(x)\), \((fg)(x)\), and \(\left(\tfrac{f}{g}\right)(x)\), with the domain of each.

Solution:

\((f+g)(x) = (3x - 2) + (x^2 + x) = x^2 + 4x - 2\)

\((f-g)(x) = (3x - 2) - (x^2 + x) = -x^2 + 2x - 2\)

\((fg)(x) = (3x - 2)(x^2 + x) = 3x^3 + 3x^2 - 2x^2 - 2x = 3x^3 + x^2 - 2x\)

\(\left(\dfrac{f}{g}\right)(x) = \dfrac{3x - 2}{x^2 + x} = \dfrac{3x - 2}{x(x + 1)}\)

Spot-check the sum at \(x = 2\): the combined rule gives \(4 + 8 - 2 = 10\); the partners give \(f(2) + g(2) = 4 + 6 = 10\). ✓ In the subtraction, note the minus sign visiting every term of \(g\) — the same distribution discipline the difference quotient drilled all week.

Answer: Both partners are polynomials with domain all reals, so \(f+g\), \(f-g\), and \(fg\) all have domain \((-\infty, \infty)\). The quotient adds its own exclusions where the denominator vanishes: domain of \(f/g\) is all reals except \(x = 0\) and \(x = -1\).

Example 2

Let \(f(x) = \sqrt{x + 4}\) and \(g(x) = \sqrt{3 - x}\). Find the domains of \(f + g\), \(fg\), \(\tfrac{f}{g}\), and \(\tfrac{g}{f}\).

Solution:

Each partner posts its own guard: \(f\) demands \(x + 4 \geq 0\), so \(D_f = [-4, \infty)\); \(g\) demands \(3 - x \geq 0\), so \(D_g = (-\infty, 3]\). The common ground is the overlap:

\(D_f \cap D_g = [-4, 3]\)

Answer: \(f+g\) and \(fg\) live on all of \([-4, 3]\). The quotients each lose one endpoint, and they lose different ones: \(\tfrac{f}{g}\) excludes where \(g(x) = 0\), namely \(x = 3\), giving \([-4, 3)\); \(\tfrac{g}{f}\) excludes where \(f(x) = 0\), namely \(x = -4\), giving \((-4, 3]\). Same two partners, four different territories — the domain belongs to the combination, not to the ingredients, and it must be computed fresh each time.

Example 3

The table gives values of two functions:

\(\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c} x & -1 & 0 & 1 & 2 \\ \hline f(x) & 3 & 1 & 0 & -2 \\ \hline g(x) & -2 & 0 & 4 & 5 \end{array}\)

Evaluate \((f+g)(2)\), \((fg)(-1)\), \(\left(\tfrac{f}{g}\right)(1)\), and \(\left(\tfrac{f}{g}\right)(0)\).

Solution:

\((f+g)(2) = f(2) + g(2) = -2 + 5 = 3\)

\((fg)(-1) = f(-1) \cdot g(-1) = 3 \cdot (-2) = -6\)

\(\left(\dfrac{f}{g}\right)(1) = \dfrac{f(1)}{g(1)} = \dfrac{0}{4} = 0\)

\(\left(\dfrac{f}{g}\right)(0) = \dfrac{f(0)}{g(0)} = \dfrac{1}{0} \quad \text{— undefined}\)

Answer: \(3\), \(-6\), \(0\), and undefined: the input \(0\) is not in the domain of \(f/g\), because the denominator partner outputs zero there. Mind the two zeros in that last pair of computations — a zero numerator is a perfectly good answer (namely 0); a zero denominator is no answer at all. Tables make combinations easy: operate on outputs, row by row — but the domain rules travel with you.

Example 4

Let \(h(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3}\), the quotient of \(f(x) = x^2 - 9\) and \(g(x) = x - 3\). Simplify \(h\), state its domain, and describe its graph.

Solution:

\(h(x) = \dfrac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x - 3} = x + 3 \quad \text{for } x \neq 3\)

Answer: The domain was fixed at the moment of construction: \(g(3) = 0\), so \(x = 3\) is excluded — and no later simplification can repeal it. The graph is the line \(y = x + 3\) with a hole at \((3, 6)\). The quotient remembers where it came from: this is Unit 2's holes-versus-asymptotes distinction meeting Unit 3's domain of honesty, and the honest report of \(h\) is “the line \(y = x+3\), except at 3.” Writing \(h(x) = x + 3\) with no exception is a small untruth about a single point — and precision about single points is much of what this unit is for. (You cancelled \(x - 3\) exactly the way you cancelled \(h\) yesterday: legally, because it is nonzero on the domain where the cancellation is claimed.)

Example 5

A student's print-on-demand poster shop has fixed monthly costs of \$900 and materials costing \$6 per poster, so the monthly cost of producing \(x\) posters is \(C(x) = 6x + 900\). Each poster sells for \$18, so revenue is \(R(x) = 18x\). Build the profit function, find the break-even point, and compute the profit at 150 posters.

Solution:

Profit is revenue minus cost — a difference of functions:

\(P(x) = (R - C)(x) = 18x - (6x + 900) = 12x - 900\)

Break-even where \(P(x) = 0\):

\(12x - 900 = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x = 75\)

At 150 posters:

\(P(150) = 12(150) - 900 = 1800 - 900 = 900\)

Answer: \(P(x) = 12x - 900\); the shop breaks even at 75 posters per month and earns \$900 at 150. Note the distribution again — subtracting \(C\) means subtracting all of it, the \$900 included. And note what the combination did that neither partner could do alone: \(R\) knows only what comes in, \(C\) only what goes out; the difference function knows whether the enterprise lives. In context, one more courtesy is due to honesty: the sensible domain here is whole numbers of posters, \(x = 0, 1, 2, \ldots\) — the model's algebra is happy with \(x = 74.3\), but the print shop is not.

The Common-Ground Rule

A combined function is defined at an input only if the computation it promises can actually be carried out there. That yields one rule and one rider:

  • Rule (all four operations): the domain of \(f+g\), \(f-g\), and \(fg\) is the intersection \(D_f \cap D_g\) — the common ground. Both partners must be defined: an undefined value is not zero, it is silence, and you cannot add, subtract, or multiply silence.
  • Rider (quotients only): \(\tfrac{f}{g}\) additionally excludes every \(x\) in the common ground where \(g(x) = 0\). Two independent kinds of exclusion, from two different causes: a partner is absent (not in a domain), or the divisor is zero (present, but outputs the one number division cannot accept).

This is the unit's virtue in working clothes: no combination may drag a function where it does not go, and the quotient must also respect what its partner says — a zero in the denominator is a refusal, and the domain records it. Compute the domain from the original partners, before simplifying; Example 4 shows why the order matters.

✍️ No Calculator Today — and That Is the Point 2 min

Every other lesson this week leaned on a tool — Desmos drew the secants, Excel tabulated the rates. Today needs neither, and the omission is deliberate: the algebra of functions is definitions plus arithmetic, and you should feel it run entirely under your own power. One habit to build now, while the work is light: write the domain down at the moment you build the combination, before any simplifying, the way you posted guards before solving radical equations. Next week the combinations get deeper — one function's output feeding another's input — and hidden domain restrictions become genuinely sneaky. The habit you practice today on easy ground is the one that will save you there.

▶  Try it yourself: For \(f(x) = x^2 + 2\) and \(g(x) = \sqrt{x - 1}\), find \((f+g)(5)\), the domain of \(f+g\), and the domain of \(f/g\).
\((f+g)(5) = f(5) + g(5) = 27 + 2 = 29\). Domains: \(D_f\) is all reals, \(D_g = [1, \infty)\), so the common ground is \([1, \infty)\) — the domain of \(f+g\). For \(f/g\), also exclude where \(g(x) = 0\): \(\sqrt{x-1} = 0\) at \(x = 1\), so the domain is \((1, \infty)\). The quotient lost the endpoint the sum kept.
▶  Try it yourself: Let \(f(x) = \sqrt{x+4}\) and \(g(x) = \sqrt{x-4}\). The product rule of radicals suggests \((fg)(x) = \sqrt{(x+4)(x-4)} = \sqrt{x^2 - 16}\), and \(\sqrt{x^2-16}\) is defined whenever \(x \leq -4\) or \(x \geq 4\). Is the domain of \(fg\) really both pieces?
No — the domain of \(fg\) is only \([4, \infty)\). The common-ground rule runs on the original partners: \(D_f = [-4, \infty)\) and \(D_g = [4, \infty)\), whose intersection is \([4, \infty)\). At \(x = -5\), the formula \(\sqrt{x^2 - 16} = \sqrt{9} = 3\) happily computes — but \(g(-5) = \sqrt{-9}\) is not a real number, so the product of the two functions was never defined there. The simplified formula enlarged the territory; the construction did not. Same moral as Example 4, pointed the other way: simplification can invent ground as well as forget holes, and the original construction is always the deed.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Reading \((fg)(x)\) as “\(f\) of \(g\) of \(x\).” It is not. \((fg)(x)\) means multiplication of outputs: \(f(x) \cdot g(x)\), both functions fed the same input \(x\), their results multiplied. “\(f\) of \(g\) of \(x\)” — one function's output fed into the other — is a different and deeper operation called composition, written \((f \circ g)(x)\) with a small circle, and it is precisely next lesson's subject. For \(f(x) = 3x - 2\), \(g(x) = x^2 + x\) at \(x = 2\): \((fg)(2) = 4 \cdot 6 = 24\), while \(f(g(2)) = f(6) = 16\). Different numbers, different ideas. When the circle is absent, multiply; when it appears, everything changes — tomorrow.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
In al-Khwarizmi's Baghdad, al-jabr was such a concrete craft that the word escaped mathematics entirely: in medieval Spain, an algebrista was a bone-setter — a restorer of broken limbs — and barbershops advertised the service. The word's journey from setting bones, to balancing equations, to today's algebra of whole functions is the history of mathematics in miniature: the same few operations, applied to ever more abstract objects — numbers, then unknowns, then functions themselves. Modern mathematics goes further still and calls any well-behaved system of addition and multiplication an algebra; today you worked inside the algebra of functions, and it earned its ancient name.

Conclusion

Today functions learned to do arithmetic with each other. The four combinations operate on outputs, input by input; each produces a genuine new function; and each lives only on common ground — the intersection of its partners' domains, with the quotient posting one further exclusion wherever its denominator falls silent at zero. You saw that the domain belongs to the construction, not the simplified formula: a cancelled factor leaves a hole the algebra must remember, and a slick radical identity can claim territory the original partners never held. The profit model showed why any of this matters outside the page: revenue and cost, each blind alone, see the whole enterprise the moment they cooperate. And one deliberate absence: no calculator today, because definitions plus arithmetic should run under your own power. Next week the cooperation deepens into the most consequential relationship two functions can have: composition, where one function's output becomes the other's input — machines chained in sequence, \(f \circ g\), the operation the common mistake box warned you not to confuse with today's product. Chained machines raise a delicate courtesy question — what may the first machine hand the second? — and answering it carefully is where this unit starts earning its name.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. What does it mean, precisely, to add two functions — and why is the common-ground rule for the domain a matter of necessity rather than convention?

2. A number can be excluded from the domain of \(f/g\) for two entirely different reasons. What are they, and why is a zero numerator not one of them?

3. Why must a combination's domain be computed from the original partners rather than from the simplified formula — and what can simplification both forget and invent?

4. How is \((fg)(x)\) different from \(f(g(x))\) — and why might the second operation, coming next week, deserve to be called the deepest form of cooperation between functions?
