🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to trace the semester's five units as one connected story, naming the specific threads that tie sequences to exponentials, logarithms to inverses, and quadratics to every conic.

  • I will be able to solve mixed-review problems that cross unit boundaries, choosing the right tool without being told which unit it comes from.

  • I will be able to rehearse the semester's standing disciplines on demand — guard-and-judge, the four-line model defense, and completing the square — ready for the final.

📚 VOCABULARY, REVISITED

These terms did not stay in the units where you met them. Watch how far each one traveled.

Common ratio: The constant multiplier \(r\) of a geometric sequence (Unit 1) — which reappeared in Unit 3 wearing a new name: the base \(b\) of an exponential function. Same growth, two languages.

Asymptote: A line a graph commits to in the long run. Vertical and horizontal asymptotes organized rational functions (Unit 2), a horizontal asymptote anchored every exponential and a vertical one every logarithm (Unit 3), and a crossed pair of them guided every hyperbola (Unit 5).

Inverse function: The function that undoes another, verified by both compositions returning \(x\) (Unit 4). The logarithm (Unit 3) was an inverse before you had the word for it.

Extraneous solution: A candidate manufactured by an irreversible step — clearing a denominator (Unit 2), squaring both sides (Unit 3), condensing logarithms (Unit 3), or substituting inside a conic system (Unit 5). The original equation is always the judge.

Completing the square: The algebraic move that rebuilds any quadratic expression around a perfect square — the master key that unlocked the general form of the circle, the ellipse, the parabola, and the hyperbola alike (Unit 5), and located the fold of a parabola before restricting its domain (Unit 4).

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Eighteen weeks ago this course began with Fibonacci copying out a rabbit puzzle in the Liber Abaci, and it paused last week with Apollonius's curves riding aboard the Hubble Space Telescope. In between: Gauss pairing off the integers, Cardano dueling over cubics, Napier spending twenty years building the logarithm to spare astronomers their arithmetic, Leibniz sharpening the idea of a function, Kepler finding the planets on ellipses. It can feel like five separate countries. It is not. This week — finals week — there is no new mathematics. There is instead the work of seeing the semester as one story, because the final will hand you problems without unit labels, and the mathematician's first skill is recognizing which tool a problem is quietly asking for.

The week has exactly three parts: this review lesson, a paired review activity (Focus-style — your teacher method-grades its two starred problems), and the Semester 1 final. Here is the story so far, unit by unit, followed by the threads that stitch it together and three worked examples that each live in two units at once.

Unit 1 — Sequences and Series: What You Must Be Able to Do

  • Identify a sequence's family — common difference (arithmetic), common ratio (geometric), or neither — and say how you know.
  • Write and convert between explicit and recursive definitions; evaluate factorial expressions by cancellation.
  • Read and write sigma notation; apply \(S_n = \frac{n}{2}(a_1 + a_n)\) and \(S_n = \frac{a_1(1 - r^n)}{1 - r}\) — and derive both (Gauss's pairing; multiply-and-subtract).
  • Test an infinite geometric series against the license \(|r| < 1\) and compute the exact sum \(S = \frac{a_1}{1 - r}\) when it converges.
  • Model accumulation and growth contexts, telling a term question from a series question by the wording alone.

Unit 2 — Polynomial and Rational Functions: What You Must Be Able to Do

  • Read end behavior from the leading term alone — the truth about the long run — and connect each zero's multiplicity to cross, touch, or flatten.
  • Divide polynomials long and synthetically; state, prove, and apply the Remainder and Factor Theorems.
  • Run the full zero hunt: Rational Zero candidates, a successful division, the factored finish — with conjugate pairs when complex zeros appear.
  • Give a rational function's complete anatomy: domain, holes versus vertical asymptotes (both traced to factors), horizontal or slant asymptote, intercepts.
  • Solve rational equations and inequalities with the discipline — forbidden list posted first, every candidate judged, sign charts for inequalities.

Unit 3 — Other Functions and Their Graphs: What You Must Be Able to Do

  • Construct an exponential function from two points or a growth story; graph it with its asymptote; use \(e\) and the compound-interest family of models.
  • Evaluate logarithms exactly; prove and deploy the product, quotient, and power rules; expand, condense, and change base.
  • Solve exponential and logarithmic equations exactly, guards posted before any condensing, every candidate judged against the original.
  • Graph radical functions and solve radical equations with the full guard-and-judge discipline — and explain where the ghosts come from.
  • Evaluate, graph, and build piecewise functions with deliberate open and closed dots at every border.
  • Compare functions across representations, and solve linear and nonlinear systems — verifying every ordered pair in both equations.

Unit 4 — Functional Relationships: What You Must Be Able to Do

  • Compute and interpret average rate of change; write and completely simplify the difference quotient — the doorstep of the calculus.
  • Combine functions with the four operations and determine the resulting domains, every exclusion justified.
  • Build and evaluate compositions from formulas and tables; catch the hidden domain restriction the inner function imposes.
  • Find inverses by swap-and-solve; prove an inverse pair by running both compositions to \(x\); graph \(f\) and \(f^{-1}\) as reflections across \(y = x\).
  • Restrict a non-invertible function's domain at its fold (complete the square to find it) and resolve the \(\pm\) fork against the declared branch.

Unit 5 — Conics: What You Must Be Able to Do

  • Describe how one plane slicing one double cone produces the whole family — circle, ellipse, parabola, hyperbola — and what tilt produces which.
  • Derive each conic from its locus definition, and read every feature (center, vertices, foci, asymptotes, directrix, eccentricity) from standard form.
  • Classify and convert any general second-degree equation by completing the square — the master key — predicting the type from the coefficients first.
  • Apply the service properties: parallel-to-point (parabola), point-to-point (ellipse), point-past-point (hyperbola) — and defend a design in four lines.
  • Solve conic systems exactly, judging every candidate and defending the crossing count.

The Five Threads That Tie the Semester

These are the connections the final will reward you for seeing. Each one is a fact, not a slogan — and each is rehearsed in the worked examples below.

  • Geometric sequences are exponential functions — sampled at integers. The common ratio \(r\) of Unit 1 and the base \(b\) of Unit 3 are the same number doing the same work; the sequence answers questions at the counting numbers, the function answers them everywhere in between.
  • Inverses ↔ logarithms. Unit 3 asked you to trust that “the logarithm undoes the exponential”; Unit 4 built the machinery — one-to-one functions, swap-and-solve, the composition test — that turns the trust into a theorem: \(\log_b(b^x) = x\) is Unit 4's composition test passed.
  • Two roads point at calculus. The difference quotient (Unit 4) measures a secant slope collapsing toward a tangent; end behavior and asymptotes (Unit 2) describe where a function is headed as \(x\) runs away. Both are questions about limits — the subject waiting one course ahead.
  • Completing the square is the master key. One move — rebuild a quadratic around a perfect square — found the vertex fold in Unit 4 and unlocked the standard form of every conic in Unit 5. Four doors, one key.
  • Systems returned with teeth. The intersection machinery of Unit 3 — substitute, solve, verify in both equations — came back in Unit 5 to count where conics cross, with guards judging the candidates that substitution manufactures.

Now the rehearsal: three worked examples, each deliberately straddling at least two units, presented exactly as your final-exam work should look.

Example 1 (Unit 1 meets Units 3 and 4)

A wildlife preserve counts its bison herd every three years. The counts so far: 500, 1000, 2000, 4000.

① Show the counts form a geometric sequence and write its explicit formula. ② Write the continuous model \(P(t)\), with \(t\) in years, and confirm it reproduces the fourth count. ③ Find exactly when the model predicts a herd of 32,000. ④ Name the thread this problem walks.

Solution:

① Test the ratios: \(\frac{1000}{500} = \frac{2000}{1000} = \frac{4000}{2000} = 2\). A constant ratio means geometric, with \(a_1 = 500\) and \(r = 2\):

\(a_n = 500 \cdot 2^{\,n-1}\), where \(n\) counts the censuses.

② The herd doubles every 3 years, so in \(t\) years it doubles \(t/3\) times:

\(P(t) = 500 \cdot 2^{t/3}\)

Check the fourth count, taken at \(t = 9\): \(P(9) = 500 \cdot 2^{9/3} = 500 \cdot 2^3 = 4000\) ✓

③ Set \(P(t) = 32000\) and free the captive exponent:

\(500 \cdot 2^{t/3} = 32000\)

\(2^{t/3} = 64 = 2^6\)

\(\frac{t}{3} = 6 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; t = 18 \text{ years}\)

No calculator needed — 64 confessed to being a power of 2. Had it not, the logarithm stood ready: the inverse function \(t(P) = 3\log_2\!\left(\frac{P}{500}\right)\) answers every “when?” question at once, and \(t(32000) = 3\log_2 64 = 3 \cdot 6 = 18\) ✓ agrees.

Answer: \(a_n = 500 \cdot 2^{n-1}\); \(P(t) = 500 \cdot 2^{t/3}\); the herd reaches 32,000 at \(t = 18\) years. ④ The thread: the sequence and the function are the same growth in two languages — the census samples the exponential at \(t = 0, 3, 6, \ldots\) — and the “when?” question is answered by the exponential's inverse, which is exactly what a logarithm is.

Example 2 (Unit 2 meets Unit 4)

For \(f(x) = \dfrac{2x + 3}{x - 1}\): ① state both asymptotes with their reasons; ② find \(f^{-1}\) by swap-and-solve; ③ state both asymptotes of \(f^{-1}\) and explain the exchange; ④ verify the pair with one composition and one point.

Solution:

① Vertical asymptote at \(x = 1\): the denominator vanishes there while the numerator equals \(2(1) + 3 = 5 \neq 0\). Horizontal asymptote at \(y = 2\): the degrees match, so the long-run value is the ratio of leading coefficients, \(\frac{2}{1}\).

② Swap and solve:

\(x = \dfrac{2y + 3}{y - 1}\)

\(x(y - 1) = 2y + 3\)

\(xy - x = 2y + 3\)

\(xy - 2y = x + 3\)

\(y(x - 2) = x + 3 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; f^{-1}(x) = \dfrac{x + 3}{x - 2}\)

③ \(f^{-1}\) has vertical asymptote \(x = 2\) and horizontal asymptote \(y = 1\). The asymptotes traded places. This is no coincidence: an inverse swaps inputs with outputs, so the output \(f\) can never reach (\(y = 2\), its horizontal asymptote) becomes the input \(f^{-1}\) can never accept (\(x = 2\), its vertical asymptote) — and the forbidden input \(x = 1\) of \(f\) becomes the unreachable output \(y = 1\) of \(f^{-1}\). Unit 2's honesty about the long run and Unit 4's courtesy of mutual undoing are describing the same two lines.

④ Composition test, one direction shown in full:

\(f\!\left(f^{-1}(x)\right) = \dfrac{2 \cdot \frac{x+3}{x-2} + 3}{\frac{x+3}{x-2} - 1} = \dfrac{\frac{2(x+3) + 3(x-2)}{x-2}}{\frac{(x+3) - (x-2)}{x-2}} = \dfrac{5x}{5} = x\) ✓

Point check: \(f(2) = \frac{7}{1} = 7\), and \(f^{-1}(7) = \frac{10}{5} = 2\) ✓ — the pair \((2, 7)\) reflects to \((7, 2)\), exactly as \(y = x\) symmetry demands.

Answer: \(f^{-1}(x) = \dfrac{x + 3}{x - 2}\); asymptotes \(x = 1, y = 2\) for \(f\) exchange into \(y = 1, x = 2\) for \(f^{-1}\) — the reflection across \(y = x\) carries every feature with it, asymptotes included.

Example 3 (Unit 5 meets Units 3 and 2)

A ranger station at the origin monitors everything within the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 10\). A straight-walled canyon road follows the parabola \(y = x^2 - 4\). Find every point where the road crosses the monitoring boundary, judging all candidates and defending the count.

Solution:

Guard first. The road gives \(x^2 = y + 4\), and since \(x^2 \geq 0\), any crossing must have \(y \geq -4\). Posted.

Substitute \(x^2 = y + 4\) into the circle — Unit 3's machinery pointed at Unit 5's curves:

\((y + 4) + y^2 = 10\)

\(y^2 + y - 6 = 0\)

\((y + 3)(y - 2) = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; y = -3 \;\text{ or }\; y = 2\)

Judge. Both candidates satisfy the guard \(y \geq -4\) — both stand. Recover the \(x\)-coordinates:

\(y = -3\): \(\; x^2 = 1 \Rightarrow x = \pm 1\) — the points \((1, -3)\) and \((-1, -3)\)

\(y = 2\): \(\; x^2 = 6 \Rightarrow x = \pm\sqrt{6}\) — the points \((\sqrt{6}, 2)\) and \((-\sqrt{6}, 2)\)

Defend the count. Two conics can meet in as many as four points, and here they use their full allowance: the quadratic offered two values of \(y\), the guard convicted neither, and each surviving \(y\) yields \(x^2 > 0\) — two symmetric points apiece. Four crossings. Verify one in both equations: \((\sqrt 6, 2)\) gives \(6 + 4 = 10\) ✓ on the circle and \((\sqrt6)^2 - 4 = 2\) ✓ on the road.

Answer: The road crosses the boundary at \((\pm 1, -3)\) and \((\pm\sqrt{6}, 2)\) — four points, exact values kept. Every semester skill fired at once: a conic, a substitution, a factored quadratic, a posted guard, and a verified ordered pair. This is what the final's synthesis problems feel like.

The Semester's Standing Disciplines — Gathered in One Place

Five phrases have followed you since September. They are not slogans; each is a method with a reason, and the final assumes all five.

  • “The calculator confirms; your algebra convinces.” Desmos may check any answer, but only written reasoning establishes one. On the final the calculator handles arithmetic; the convincing is yours.
  • The domain of honesty. Every model — polynomial, exponential, conic — describes reality only on an interval, and an honest solver names where the model stops. A defense that hides its limits is an advertisement, not an argument.
  • Guard-and-judge. Post the domain conditions before the algebra; judge every candidate against the original equation after. Irreversible steps — clearing denominators, squaring, condensing logs — manufacture outsiders, predictably.
  • The four-line model defense. Claim, structure, long-run, domain of honesty — born in Unit 2's model choices, given geometric teeth in Unit 5. Any “which model?” question on the final deserves these four lines.
  • Completing the square as master key. When a second-degree equation hides its identity, rebuild the squares. The center, the family, and every feature follow.

💻 In Desmos: The Whole Semester on One Screen 8 min

At desmos.com/calculator, plot one ambassador from every family you have met:

  1. y = 2^x and y = log₂(x) (type log_2(x)), plus the dashed line y = x. Watch the exponential and the logarithm reflect into each other — the Unit 3–Unit 4 thread in pixels.
  2. y = x³ − 4x and y = (2x+3)/(x−1). Trace the polynomial's end behavior and the rational function's two asymptotes — the long run telling the truth.
  3. x² + y² = 10 and y = x² − 4. Count the four crossings of Example 3 and click each one — the calculator confirms what your algebra proved, radicals and all.

One screen, every family, all still obeying the rules you learned to read. That screen is your semester.

▶  Try it yourself: Solve \(\log_3(x + 6) + \log_3 x = 3\) with the full discipline — guards, solution, judgment. Work it on paper before you peek.
Guards: \(x + 6 > 0\) and \(x > 0\), so \(x > 0\). Condense: \(\log_3\!\left(x(x+6)\right) = 3\), so \(x^2 + 6x = 27\), giving \(x^2 + 6x - 27 = 0\), so \((x + 9)(x - 3) = 0\): candidates \(x = 3\) and \(x = -9\). Judge: \(x = 3\): \(\log_3 9 + \log_3 3 = 2 + 1 = 3\) ✓. \(x = -9\) violates the guard \(x > 0\) — extraneous, manufactured when condensing enlarged the domain. Solution: \(x = 3\).

Conclusion

A semester ago, a function was a rule and a curve was a picture. Now a list of numbers declares its family to you; a leading term confesses a polynomial's destiny; an exponent surrenders to a logarithm you have proved; a function and its inverse walk each other home through both compositions; and one completed square opens any conic's general form. None of that was five separate subjects — it was one education in reading the truth a formula tells and refusing the lies a careless step can manufacture. The final is the semester's capstone act of intellectual honesty: one quiet sitting, notes closed, no tools but arithmetic, where you show — to your teacher and to yourself — that the mathematics is genuinely yours. Work the review activity with full method, rest well, and then write a final that a stranger could follow and a mathematician would trust. Next semester the circle you built this fall becomes a coordinate system of its own, and the story continues — in triangles, waves, and a number that took courage to imagine.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. In what precise sense is a geometric sequence “the same growth” as an exponential function — and what can the continuous function do that the sequence cannot?

2. Asymptotes, end behavior, and the domain of honesty all make claims about the long run. Why is the long run where a model's truthfulness is tested — and where its limits must be confessed?

3. One algebraic move — completing the square — found a parabola's fold in Unit 4 and unlocked all four conics in Unit 5. What makes a single technique a master key across so many doors?

4. The final asks you to work alone, notes closed, guards posted, every candidate judged. Why is a cumulative exam an act of intellectual honesty rather than a mere measurement?
