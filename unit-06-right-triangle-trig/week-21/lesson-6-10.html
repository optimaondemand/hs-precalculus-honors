Building the Complete Unit Circle
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to generate every special-angle coordinate on the full unit circle from the three first-quadrant seed values, using reference angles and ASTC sign patterns.

  • I will be able to audit an unfamiliar unit-circle diagram or table for errors, by checking each entry's magnitude against its seed and its sign against its quadrant.

  • I will be able to read any trig function's value at a special angle directly from the generated coordinates, without re-deriving it from scratch.

📚 VOCABULARY

Seed value: One of the three first-quadrant special-angle coordinate pairs — at \(\frac{\pi}{6}\), \(\frac{\pi}{4}\), and \(\frac{\pi}{3}\) — from which every other special-angle point on the circle is generated by reflection and sign change.

Family of an angle: The set of four angles, one per quadrant, that all share the same reference angle. Every family has exactly one seed and three sign-flipped copies of it.

Coordinate pair on the circle: For an angle \(\theta\) in standard position, the point \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) where its terminal side meets the unit circle — cosine is always the \(x\)-coordinate, sine always the \(y\)-coordinate.

Audit: The habit of checking a claimed result — your own, a classmate's, or an AI's — against an independent standard (here, magnitude from the seed, sign from ASTC) before accepting it.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Ptolemy's Almagest, compiled in Alexandria around AD 150, contains a table of chords accurate to five decimal places, covering the full circle in steps of a half-degree — a staggering feat of hand computation for its era. Ptolemy did not measure each entry separately with a physical instrument. He built the whole table from a small starter set of geometric facts — chords of \(36^\circ\), \(72^\circ\), and a few others he could construct exactly — and then used identities (angle-sum and angle-difference relationships, the ancestors of formulas you will meet formally in Unit 7) to generate everything else. A handful of exact seeds, multiplied out by pattern, became a complete and trustworthy table. You are about to do the same thing today, at a smaller and friendlier scale: three seed values become an entire circle of exact coordinates.

The Three Seeds and Their Families

Every non-axis special angle on the circle belongs to a family of four, sharing one of these three reference angles:

\(\dfrac{\pi}{6}\!: \left(\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}, \dfrac{1}{2}\right) \qquad \dfrac{\pi}{4}\!: \left(\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}, \dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right) \qquad \dfrac{\pi}{3}\!: \left(\dfrac{1}{2}, \dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)\)

To generate the other three members of a family from its seed \((\cos\theta_R, \sin\theta_R)\):

  • QII (angle \(= \pi - \theta_R\)): flip the sign of \(x\) only — \((-\cos\theta_R,\ \sin\theta_R)\)
  • QIII (angle \(= \pi + \theta_R\)): flip both signs — \((-\cos\theta_R,\ -\sin\theta_R)\)
  • QIV (angle \(= 2\pi - \theta_R\)): flip the sign of \(y\) only — \((\cos\theta_R,\ -\sin\theta_R)\)

This is ASTC again, written as coordinate arithmetic instead of a mnemonic sentence: cosine is the \(x\)-coordinate, sine is the \(y\)-coordinate, and the sign each one carries in a quadrant is exactly the sign \(x\) or \(y\) actually has there.

Example 1

Generate the coordinates of all four angles in the \(\frac{\pi}{6}\)-family: \(\frac{\pi}{6}\), \(\frac{5\pi}{6}\), \(\frac{7\pi}{6}\), \(\frac{11\pi}{6}\).

Solution:

Seed (QI): \(\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \frac12\right)\). Apply the sign pattern to each quadrant:

\(\dfrac{\pi}{6}: \left(\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}, \dfrac{1}{2}\right) \qquad \dfrac{5\pi}{6} = \pi - \dfrac{\pi}{6}: \left(-\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}, \dfrac{1}{2}\right)\)

\(\dfrac{7\pi}{6} = \pi + \dfrac{\pi}{6}: \left(-\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}, -\dfrac{1}{2}\right) \qquad \dfrac{11\pi}{6} = 2\pi - \dfrac{\pi}{6}: \left(\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}, -\dfrac{1}{2}\right)\)

Answer: Four points, one seed, four sign patterns — \((+,+)\), \((-,+)\), \((-,-)\), \((+,-)\) — walked counterclockwise around the circle exactly as the quadrants themselves are numbered.

Example 2

Generate the coordinates of all four angles in the \(\frac{\pi}{4}\)-family and the \(\frac{\pi}{3}\)-family.

Solution:

\(\dfrac{\pi}{4}: \left(\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}, \dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right) \quad \dfrac{3\pi}{4}: \left(-\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}, \dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right) \quad \dfrac{5\pi}{4}: \left(-\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}, -\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right) \quad \dfrac{7\pi}{4}: \left(\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}, -\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)\)

\(\dfrac{\pi}{3}: \left(\dfrac{1}{2}, \dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) \quad \dfrac{2\pi}{3}: \left(-\dfrac{1}{2}, \dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) \quad \dfrac{4\pi}{3}: \left(-\dfrac{1}{2}, -\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) \quad \dfrac{5\pi}{3}: \left(\dfrac{1}{2}, -\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)\)

Answer: Twelve non-axis points total, across the three families — every one of them earned from three seeds and one sign rule, not memorized one at a time. Add the four axis points \((1,0)\), \((0,1)\), \((-1,0)\), \((0,-1)\) at \(0\), \(\frac{\pi}{2}\), \(\pi\), \(\frac{3\pi}{2}\), and the entire sixteen-point circle is complete.

Example 3

Use the generated coordinates to evaluate \(\tan\left(\frac{7\pi}{6}\right)\) and \(\csc\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}\right)\).

Solution:

From Example 1, the point at \(\frac{7\pi}{6}\) is \(\left(-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, -\frac12\right)\), so \(\cos\frac{7\pi}{6} = -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\) and \(\sin\frac{7\pi}{6} = -\frac12\):

\(\tan \dfrac{7\pi}{6} = \dfrac{\sin(7\pi/6)}{\cos(7\pi/6)} = \dfrac{-1/2}{-\sqrt3/2} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt3} = \dfrac{\sqrt3}{3}\)

From Example 2, the point at \(\frac{2\pi}{3}\) is \(\left(-\frac12, \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)\), so \(\sin\frac{2\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\):

\(\csc \dfrac{2\pi}{3} = \dfrac{1}{\sin(2\pi/3)} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt3/2} = \dfrac{2}{\sqrt3} = \dfrac{2\sqrt3}{3}\)

Answer: \(\tan\frac{7\pi}{6} = \frac{\sqrt3}{3}\) (positive, as ASTC requires in QIII) and \(\csc\frac{2\pi}{3} = \frac{2\sqrt3}{3}\) (positive, as ASTC requires in QII). Once the coordinate pair is generated, all six functions of that angle follow from arithmetic on two numbers — no fresh reference-angle work needed.

Two Checks, Every Time

Whenever you generate — or audit — a point on the unit circle, run both checks before you move on:

  • Magnitude check: do the unsigned numbers match one of the three seeds — \(\frac12\) and \(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\), or \(\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\) twice, or \(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\) and \(\frac12\)? A magnitude that matches no seed at all is an automatic error.
  • Sign check: does the sign of \(x\) (cosine) and the sign of \(y\) (sine) match the quadrant the angle is actually in?

A single point can fail either check independently — correct magnitude, wrong sign; or correct sign, wrong magnitude (the seeds for \(\frac{\pi}{6}\) and \(\frac{\pi}{3}\) are easy to swap, since both use \(\frac12\) and \(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\), just in opposite coordinate slots). Run both checks, always, on every point.

⚠️ AI CHECKPOINT: FIND THE PLANTED ERROR

A student asked an AI to generate a table of \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) for eight special angles. Audit the table below — by the two checks above — before reading on:

Angle AI's claimed \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\)
\(\pi/6\)\(\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \frac12\right)\)
\(\pi/3\)\(\left(\frac12, \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)\)
\(2\pi/3\)\(\left(-\frac12, \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)\)
\(5\pi/6\)\(\left(-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \frac12\right)\)
\(5\pi/4\)\(\left(-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}, -\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)\)
\(7\pi/6\)\(\left(-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \frac12\right)\)
\(4\pi/3\)\(\left(-\frac12, -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)\)
\(5\pi/3\)\(\left(\frac12, -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)\)

Run the two checks on every row. The magnitude check passes everywhere — every entry uses \(\frac12\) and \(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\), a legitimate seed pair. But the sign check fails at \(7\pi/6\): that angle sits in Quadrant III (between \(\pi\) and \(\frac{3\pi}{2}\)), where both cosine and sine must be negative. The AI kept sine positive — the table shows \(\left(-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \frac12\right)\), which is actually the correct point for \(5\pi/6\) (QII), copied into the wrong row.

The correct entry is \(7\pi/6: \left(-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, -\frac12\right)\). Name the failure precisely: the AI's magnitude was right and its \(x\)-sign was right, but it silently reused a QII sign pattern for a QIII angle. A table that is seven-eighths correct is exactly the kind of error a rushed reader accepts wholesale — audit every row, not just the ones that look unfamiliar.

💻 In Desmos: Watch the Family Walk the Circle 8 min

At desmos.com/calculator (or below):

  1. Graph the unit circle: x^2 + y^2 = 1.
  2. Plot all four points of the \(\pi/6\)-family from Example 1: (sqrt(3)/2, 1/2), (-sqrt(3)/2, 1/2), (-sqrt(3)/2, -1/2), (sqrt(3)/2, -1/2). They land at the four corners of a rectangle inscribed in the circle — the visible signature of one seed reflected through both axes.
  3. Now plot the corrected \(7\pi/6\) point, \((-\sqrt3/2, -1/2)\), next to the AI's erroneous claim, \((-\sqrt3/2, 1/2)\). The erroneous point sits in the wrong quadrant entirely — a mistake the picture catches instantly, even though the table's numbers looked plausible.

▶  Try it yourself: Generate the coordinate pair at \(\frac{11\pi}{6}\) from its seed, and use it to evaluate \(\sec\frac{11\pi}{6}\).
\(\frac{11\pi}{6} = 2\pi - \frac{\pi}{6}\): QIV, sign pattern \((+,-)\) on the \(\pi/6\) seed \(\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \frac12\right)\), giving \(\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, -\frac12\right)\). So \(\cos\frac{11\pi}{6} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\), and \(\sec\frac{11\pi}{6} = \frac{1}{\sqrt3/2} = \frac{2}{\sqrt3} = \frac{2\sqrt3}{3}\).
▶  Try it yourself: A classmate claims the coordinate pair at \(\frac{4\pi}{3}\) is \(\left(-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, -\frac12\right)\). Audit the claim with both checks.
\(\frac{4\pi}{3} = \pi + \frac{\pi}{3}\): its seed is the \(\pi/3\) seed \(\left(\frac12, \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)\), not the \(\pi/6\) seed. The magnitude check fails: the classmate used \(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\) and \(\frac12\) in the wrong slots — the classic \(\pi/6\)-vs-\(\pi/3\) mixup. The sign check (QIII, both negative) is correctly applied, but to the wrong seed. Correct: \(\left(-\frac12, -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)\).
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Swapping the \(\pi/6\) seed \(\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \frac12\right)\) with the \(\pi/3\) seed \(\left(\frac12, \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)\) — the same two numbers, in reversed coordinate slots. The two seeds are easy to confuse precisely because they use identical magnitudes. The reliable fix: at \(\pi/6\) (the smaller angle, closer to the \(x\)-axis), the point should sit closer to the \(x\)-axis than the \(y\)-axis — so its \(x\)-coordinate (\(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\), the larger number) should be larger than its \(y\)-coordinate (\(\frac12\)). If your pair has the larger number in the \(y\)-slot for a \(\pi/6\)-family angle, you have the seeds reversed.

Conclusion

Three seeds, one sign rule, sixteen points — the whole special-angle unit circle, generated rather than memorized one entry at a time, and audited the same way Ptolemy's identities let him extend Hipparchus's original table without re-measuring a single chord. Tomorrow the unit's virtue of responsibility comes fully into view: you take this machinery — right-triangle ratios, the unit circle, and reference angles — out of the notebook and into the field, solving the kind of multi-step navigation and surveying problems where a documented, defensible answer is not a nicety but an obligation to whoever relies on your measurement.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why do only three seed values, together with one sign rule, suffice to generate every special-angle point on the entire circle?

2. The AI's table in this lesson was correct in seven of eight rows. Why is a mostly-correct table more dangerous to trust than an obviously wrong one?

3. What is the difference between the magnitude check and the sign check, and why must both be run independently on every point?

4. Why are the \(\pi/6\) and \(\pi/3\) seeds especially easy to confuse, and what single habit protects against that particular mixup?
