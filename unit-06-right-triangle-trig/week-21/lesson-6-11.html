🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to solve multi-step navigation and surveying problems that combine right-triangle ratios, the unit circle, and reference angles.

  • I will be able to convert a compass heading into a standard-position angle correctly, and use reference angles to find the signed displacement components it produces.

  • I will be able to document a field computation to a professional standard — declared setup, every step shown, units carried throughout, and a check that the answer is physically reasonable.

📚 VOCABULARY

Angle of elevation / depression: The angle between a horizontal line of sight and a line of sight up to a higher object (elevation) or down to a lower one (depression). The two are equal by alternate interior angles whenever the sightlines connect the same two points.

Bearing / heading: A direction expressed as an angle measured clockwise from north, from \(0^\circ\) to \(360^\circ\) — the convention navigators and surveyors actually use, and different from the standard-position angle (measured counterclockwise from the positive \(x\)-axis, i.e., from east) that the unit circle uses.

Heading-to-standard conversion: The formula \(\theta_{\text{standard}} = 90^\circ - \text{heading} \pmod{360^\circ}\), which translates a compass heading into the angle the unit circle actually expects — because north sits at standard-position \(90^\circ\), not \(0^\circ\).

Traverse: A surveyed path made of straight legs, each with its own distance and heading, whose total displacement is found by adding up every leg's east–west and north–south components separately — exactly the kind of running total a spreadsheet is built for.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In 1792, two French astronomers set out to measure the exact distance from Dunkirk to Barcelona, along the meridian that runs through Paris — not out of idle curiosity, but because the newly proposed metric system needed a definition of the meter that belonged to no king and no nation: one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator. Jean-Baptiste Delambre and Pierre Méchain spent seven years carrying that responsibility across a war-torn countryside, station by station, each leg of the survey a triangle solved by the same right-triangle trigonometry you have spent this unit learning. A single sloppy angle, uncaught, would have propagated through every triangle after it and quietly corrupted a unit of measure the whole world still uses today. Their work is the origin of this unit's virtue in its purest form: responsibility is not a feeling a surveyor has about their work; it is the documented, checkable trail of reasoning that lets someone else trust a number they did not compute themselves. Today you carry that same trail — right-triangle ratios, the unit circle, and reference angles, all three, in the field.

From Compass Heading to the Unit Circle

A navigator's heading is measured clockwise from north; the unit circle's standard position is measured counterclockwise from east. The two systems do not line up by accident — you must convert on purpose, every time:

\(\theta_{\text{standard}} = 90^\circ - \text{heading} \pmod{360^\circ}\)

Once converted, the displacement after traveling distance \(d\) on that heading has an eastward component \(d\cos\theta_{\text{standard}}\) and a northward component \(d\sin\theta_{\text{standard}}\) — negative values meaning west or south, exactly as ASTC's sign pattern dictates for whatever quadrant \(\theta_{\text{standard}}\) lands in. Skip the conversion, and every downstream sign and quadrant is wrong — a heading is not a standard-position angle, no matter how tempting it is to treat it as one.

Example 1

A surveyor stands at a point and measures the angle of elevation to the top of a tower as \(35^\circ\). She then walks \(40\) m directly away from the tower and measures the angle of elevation again: \(20^\circ\). Find the height of the tower.

Solution:

Let \(x\) be the (unknown) distance from the first point to the base, and \(h\) the tower's height. From the first point, \(\tan 35^\circ = \dfrac{h}{x}\); from the second point, \(40\) m farther back, \(\tan 20^\circ = \dfrac{h}{x + 40}\). Both equations describe the same height \(h\) — solve them together, not separately:

\(h = x\tan 35^\circ = (x + 40)\tan 20^\circ\)

\(x(\tan 35^\circ - \tan 20^\circ) = 40\tan 20^\circ\)

\(x(0.7002 - 0.3640) = 40(0.3640) \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x(0.3362) = 14.559 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x \approx 43.30 \text{ m}\)

\(h = x\tan 35^\circ \approx 43.30(0.7002) \approx 30.32 \text{ m}\)

Verify: from the second point, \((x+40)\tan 20^\circ = 83.30(0.3640) \approx 30.32\) m — matches.

Answer: The tower is approximately \(30.3\) m tall. Notice why one equation alone was never enough: either measurement, taken by itself, has an unknown distance and an unknown height — two unknowns need two equations, exactly the system-solving discipline of a two-station sight.

Example 2

A ship holds a compass heading of \(250^\circ\) for \(200\) nautical miles. Find its eastward and northward displacement components.

Solution:

Convert first. \(\theta_{\text{standard}} = 90^\circ - 250^\circ = -160^\circ\), which is coterminal with \(-160^\circ + 360^\circ = 200^\circ\).

Locate and reduce. \(200^\circ\) is Quadrant III; reference angle \(= 200^\circ - 180^\circ = 20^\circ\). QIII: both cosine and sine are negative.

\(\cos 200^\circ = -\cos 20^\circ \approx -0.9397 \qquad \sin 200^\circ = -\sin 20^\circ \approx -0.3420\)

\(\text{east component} = 200(-0.9397) \approx -187.9 \text{ nm} \qquad \text{north component} = 200(-0.3420) \approx -68.4 \text{ nm}\)

Answer: The ship moves about \(187.9\) nm west and \(68.4\) nm south (the negative signs telling direction, not error). Sanity check: a heading of \(250^\circ\) sits between due west (\(270^\circ\)) and due south (\(180^\circ\)), closer to west — and the components agree, with the westward leg far larger than the southward one. A displacement that came out mostly northward would have signaled a conversion mistake before a single sign was checked.

Example 3

An aircraft flies at a constant altitude of \(3.2\) km. It spots two boats along the same line of flight: the angle of depression to the nearer boat is \(52^\circ\), and to the farther boat, \(29^\circ\). Find the distance between the boats.

Solution:

The angle of depression from the plane equals the angle of elevation from each boat, so each boat's horizontal distance from the point directly below the aircraft satisfies \(\tan(\text{angle}) = \dfrac{\text{altitude}}{\text{horizontal distance}}\), giving horizontal distance \(= \dfrac{\text{altitude}}{\tan(\text{angle})}\):

\(d_{\text{near}} = \dfrac{3.2}{\tan 52^\circ} \approx \dfrac{3.2}{1.2799} \approx 2.500 \text{ km} \qquad d_{\text{far}} = \dfrac{3.2}{\tan 29^\circ} \approx \dfrac{3.2}{0.5543} \approx 5.773 \text{ km}\)

Answer: Distance between the boats \(= d_{\text{far}} - d_{\text{near}} \approx 5.773 - 2.500 \approx 3.27\) km. The steeper angle belongs to the nearer boat, and the shallower angle to the farther one — if your two distances came out reversed, the labeling of near and far is the first place to look.

📊 In Excel: The Traverse Table 10 min

A hiking traverse runs three legs: \(500\) m on heading \(070^\circ\); \(350\) m on heading \(140^\circ\); \(620\) m on heading \(200^\circ\). Build the surveyor's table in Excel, one row per leg:

Columns: Leg · Distance · Heading · Standard angle =90-C2 (add 360 if negative) · East component =B2*COS(RADIANS(D2)) · North component =B2*SIN(RADIANS(D2)). A final row sums the two component columns.

Working it: Leg 1 (\(20^\circ\), QI) gives \((+469.8, +171.0)\); Leg 2 (\(310^\circ\), QIV, reference \(50^\circ\)) gives \((+225.0, -268.1)\); Leg 3 (\(250^\circ\), QIII, reference \(70^\circ\)) gives \((-212.0, -582.6)\). Totals: east \(\approx 482.9\) m, north \(\approx -679.7\) m (i.e., \(679.7\) m south). Net displacement \(= \sqrt{482.9^2 + 679.7^2} \approx 833.7\) m, at a compass bearing of about \(144.6^\circ\) (mostly southeast, since the southward leg dominates the eastward one).

This is the professional standard in miniature: every leg's angle converted the same declared way, every component computed by an identical formula copied down the column, and a final total that a second surveyor could recompute from your table alone — without ever asking you a clarifying question. (If you save the workbook, name it LastName_FirstName_Traverse_Precalculus.xlsx in Optima → Math → PrecalculusHonors.)

⚠️ AI CHECKPOINT: THE UNCONVERTED HEADING

A student asked an AI to find the eastward and northward components of a \(400\) m displacement on heading \(155^\circ\). It answered:

“East component = 400·cos(155°) ≈ −362.5 m. North component = 400·sin(155°) ≈ 169.0 m. So the displacement is about 362.5 m west and 169.0 m north.”

The arithmetic is flawless — but the AI fed the heading directly into cosine and sine as if it were already a standard-position angle, skipping the conversion \(\theta_{\text{standard}} = 90^\circ - 155^\circ = -65^\circ \equiv 295^\circ\) entirely. Sanity check the claim first: a heading of \(155^\circ\) lies between due east (\(90^\circ\)) and due south (\(180^\circ\)) — the ship should move southeast. "West and north" is not southeast; it is nearly the opposite direction.

The corrected standard angle is \(295^\circ\) (QIV, reference angle \(65^\circ\)): \(\cos 295^\circ = \cos 65^\circ \approx 0.4226\), \(\sin 295^\circ = -\sin 65^\circ \approx -0.9063\). East component \(= 400(0.4226) \approx 169.0\) m; north component \(= 400(-0.9063) \approx -362.5\) m — that is, \(169.0\) m east and \(362.5\) m south.

Notice the AI's two numbers, \(362.5\) and \(169.0\), are exactly the correct magnitudes — just attached to the wrong directions entirely, because the conversion step was silently skipped. In fieldwork this is not an academic slip: a survey crew that trusted this output would have walked the wrong way. Before accepting any displacement calculation — yours or a machine's — ask first whether the heading was actually converted to standard position, and second whether the resulting direction makes physical sense.

▶  Try it yourself: From a point 60 m from a flagpole's base, the angle of elevation to the top is 38°. Find the flagpole's height.
\(h = 60\tan 38^\circ \approx 60(0.7813) \approx 46.9\) m.
▶  Try it yourself: Convert a compass heading of 310° to standard position, name its quadrant and reference angle, and find the sign of both displacement components.
\(\theta_{\text{standard}} = 90^\circ - 310^\circ = -220^\circ \equiv 140^\circ\) (add \(360^\circ\)). \(140^\circ\) is QII, reference angle \(40^\circ\). QII: cosine negative, sine positive — so the east component is negative (a westward displacement) and the north component is positive, matching the intuition that heading \(310^\circ\) points mostly northwest.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Feeding a compass heading straight into cosine and sine without converting to standard position first — exactly the AI's error above. A heading and a standard-position angle are measured from different starting rays (north vs. east) and rotate in opposite directions (clockwise vs. counterclockwise); treating them as interchangeable produces numbers of the right size attached to the wrong direction, which is often more dangerous than an obviously wrong answer because it looks plausible until someone checks it against a compass.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The meter Delambre and Méchain's survey produced was later found to be off by about a fifth of a millimeter per meter — not because their trigonometry was wrong, but because the Earth is not a perfect sphere, an error in the assumed model, not the mathematics applied to it. The metal bar cast to represent their result, the Mètre des Archives, nonetheless defined the meter for the better part of a century. Even an imperfect model, honestly documented, can serve for generations — which is exactly why this unit has insisted, all along, on showing the work behind every number.
