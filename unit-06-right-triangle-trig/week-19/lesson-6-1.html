Angles in Degrees and Radians
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to define a radian as the measure of a central angle whose arc is exactly as long as the circle's radius.

  • I will be able to convert angle measures fluently between degrees and radians, in both directions.

  • I will be able to explain why the radian — not the degree — is the unit that mathematics itself eventually requires.

📚 VOCABULARY

Angle: The figure formed when a ray is rotated about its endpoint. The starting position of the ray is the initial side; the position after rotation is the terminal side; the shared endpoint is the vertex.

Standard position: An angle drawn with its vertex at the origin and its initial side lying along the positive \(x\)-axis. A positive angle rotates counterclockwise from there; a negative angle rotates clockwise.

Degree: A unit of angle measure obtained by dividing one full rotation into 360 equal parts. The number 360 is a human choice, not a mathematical necessity — an inheritance from Babylonian sky-watchers, not a law of geometry.

Radian: The measure of a central angle that cuts off (subtends) an arc exactly as long as the circle's own radius. Unlike the degree, the radian is not a human convention — it falls directly out of the circle's geometry.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Around 150 BC, an astronomer named Hipparchus of Nicaea sat down to solve a problem no one had solved completely before: he wanted a table that would let him find the length of any chord of a circle, for any central angle, on demand. To build it, he needed a fixed way to measure angles — and he reached for the system the Babylonian astronomers had already handed down through centuries of watching the sky: divide the full circle into 360 parts. Nobody is entirely sure why 360 won out over other choices — it is close to the number of days in a year, and it is a wonderfully divisible number (by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12…), which matters enormously if you are doing astronomy with no calculator. Hipparchus's chord table was, in every meaningful sense, the first trigonometric table in history — and it was built entirely in degrees, a unit whose value is convenience, not necessity.

This week you inherit that 2,000-year-old convention. But you are also going to meet a second way to measure an angle — one that was not fixed by any astronomer's habit, but falls directly out of the circle itself. It emerged much later: the mathematician Roger Cotes described the idea as early as 1714, though the name we now use, radian, was not coined until 1873, by the physicist James Thomson. What Cotes and Thomson were both reaching for is this: a circle already has a natural unit of length built into it — its own radius. Why not measure angles by asking how many radius-lengths of arc they cut off?

The Definition of a Radian

Draw a circle of radius \(r\), centered at a point. Mark off an arc along the circle whose length is exactly \(r\) — one radius-length of arc. The central angle that this arc subtends has a measure of 1 radian.

Because the circumference of the whole circle is \(2\pi r\) — that is, exactly \(2\pi\) radius-lengths — a full rotation measures exactly \(2\pi\) radians. And since a full rotation is also \(360^\circ\), the two systems are tied together by one master equation:

\(2\pi \text{ radians} = 360^\circ\)

Divide that master equation by 2 and you get \(\pi\) radians \(= 180^\circ\) — the single fact from which every conversion in this lesson is built. From it:

To convert degrees → radians, multiply by \(\dfrac{\pi}{180}\).   To convert radians → degrees, multiply by \(\dfrac{180}{\pi}\).

One radian, worked out numerically, is \(\dfrac{180}{\pi} \approx 57.2958^\circ\) — a little less than \(60^\circ\). Picture it: lay the radius along the circle's own edge, starting from a point, and mark where it ends. You will land just a bit short of one-sixth of the way around — because \(60^\circ\) is exactly one-sixth of a circle, and one radian is slightly less than that. This is not a coincidence to memorize; it is the definition itself, made visible.

Example 1

Convert \(135^\circ\) and \(-45^\circ\) to radians.

Solution:

\(135^\circ \times \dfrac{\pi}{180} = \dfrac{135\pi}{180} = \dfrac{3\pi}{4}\)

\(-45^\circ \times \dfrac{\pi}{180} = \dfrac{-45\pi}{180} = -\dfrac{\pi}{4}\)

Answer: \(135^\circ = \dfrac{3\pi}{4}\) rad, and \(-45^\circ = -\dfrac{\pi}{4}\) rad. Always reduce the fraction — an unreduced radian measure is correct but unfinished.

Example 2

Convert \(\dfrac{5\pi}{6}\) radians to degrees.

Solution:

\(\dfrac{5\pi}{6} \times \dfrac{180}{\pi} = \dfrac{5 \cdot 180}{6} = 5 \cdot 30 = 150\)

Answer: \(\dfrac{5\pi}{6}\) rad \(= 150^\circ\). Notice the \(\pi\)'s cancel — they always do when you convert radians to degrees, which is exactly why "radians" are often left with a bare \(\pi\) in them rather than a decimal.

Example 3

Convert \(200^\circ\) to radians. (This one will not reduce to a familiar friend — and that is the point.)

Solution:

\(200^\circ \times \dfrac{\pi}{180} = \dfrac{200\pi}{180} = \dfrac{10\pi}{9}\)

Answer: \(200^\circ = \dfrac{10\pi}{9}\) rad. Not every angle converts to a tidy multiple of \(\dfrac{\pi}{6}\) or \(\dfrac{\pi}{4}\); the conversion factor works on any angle at all, familiar or not.

Key Facts: The Standard Angles, in Both Languages

These conversions recur so often in this unit and the next that they are worth knowing outright, not re-deriving every time:

Degrees 0° 30° 45° 60° 90° 120° 180° 270° 360°
Radians \(0\) \(\frac{\pi}{6}\) \(\frac{\pi}{4}\) \(\frac{\pi}{3}\) \(\frac{\pi}{2}\) \(\frac{2\pi}{3}\) \(\pi\) \(\frac{3\pi}{2}\) \(2\pi\)

Notice the pattern: \(30^\circ, 45^\circ, 60^\circ\) become \(\frac{\pi}{6}, \frac{\pi}{4}, \frac{\pi}{3}\) — the denominator is the number you'd divide 180 by to get the degree measure's "partner." You will lean on this table constantly once the unit circle arrives next week.

💻 On Your Calculator: Degree Mode vs. Radian Mode

Every scientific or graphing calculator — including Desmos's scientific calculator — has a hidden setting that decides how it interprets every angle you type: degree mode or radian mode. The calculator has no way of knowing which one you intend; it simply trusts the mode switch. This is the single most common source of wrong answers in a trigonometry course — not bad algebra, but a silent mode mismatch.

In the Desmos graphing calculator (or the embed below), open the keyboard's wrench/settings icon and look for the angle-mode toggle. Practice switching it, then type sin(30) in degree mode and again in radian mode. The two results will not be close — they will not even have the same sign, as the Common Mistake box below shows. Get in the habit of checking the mode before you evaluate any trig expression, every single time.

▶  Try it yourself: Convert \(-150^\circ\) to radians, and convert \(\dfrac{7\pi}{4}\) to degrees. Work both before you peek.
\(-150^\circ \times \frac{\pi}{180} = -\frac{150\pi}{180} = -\frac{5\pi}{6}\).   \(\frac{7\pi}{4} \times \frac{180}{\pi} = \frac{7 \cdot 180}{4} = \frac{1260}{4} = 315^\circ\).
▶  Try it yourself (honors): Without a calculator, decide whether 2 radians is more or less than \(120^\circ\), and justify your reasoning from the definition alone.
One radian is a little under \(60^\circ\) (about \(57.3^\circ\)), so 2 radians is a little under \(120^\circ\) (about \(114.6^\circ\)) — meaning 2 radians is less than \(120^\circ\). You can reason this out entirely from "1 radian \(\approx\) a bit less than \(60^\circ\)" without ever multiplying by \(\frac{180}{\pi}\) — that is what it means to actually understand the definition, rather than just operate a formula.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Leaving the calculator in the wrong mode. If your calculator is set to radian mode and you type sin(30) meaning "thirty degrees," it computes \(\sin(30 \text{ radians})\) instead — and 30 radians, reduced by subtracting multiples of \(2\pi\), lands deep in the fourth quadrant of the circle. The result is \(\sin(30 \text{ rad}) \approx -0.988\), a world away from the \(\sin(30^\circ) = 0.5\) you actually wanted — not a rounding error, but a completely different number with the opposite sign. The fix is a habit, not a formula: state the mode you need before you press a single key, and if an answer looks strange, the mode switch is the first thing to check — before you doubt your algebra.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The 360-degree circle and the base-60 (sexagesimal) way of splitting a degree into 60 minutes and each minute into 60 seconds both trace back to Babylonian astronomers, who did their arithmetic in base 60 rather than base 10. That inheritance is still alive today in far more than angles: it is the reason an hour has 60 minutes and a minute has 60 seconds. When a navigator today reads a latitude of \(40^\circ 42' 51''\), they are reading a number system built for the sky nearly four thousand years ago.

Conclusion

You now hold two languages for the same idea — the ancient, human-scaled degree, and the radian, which the circle itself hands you for free. Neither is "more correct"; they measure the same rotations, and you can walk between them fluently in either direction. But the radian's real justification is still ahead of you. Tomorrow, that definition — an arc exactly one radius long — stops being a curiosity and starts doing work: it is the reason the arc-length formula has no extra constant to remember, and the reason a sector's area falls out just as cleanly. Hipparchus needed degrees to build his chord table nearly two thousand years ago; you are about to see exactly why the mathematics that came after him needed something else.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. A radian is defined by a specific geometric relationship between arc and radius. State that relationship in your own words, and explain why it guarantees \(2\pi\) radians in one full rotation.

2. The number 360 is a human choice with a plausible history; \(2\pi\) is not a choice at all. What does that difference tell you about which unit is "built into" the circle and which is layered on top of it?

3. Why does typing an angle into a calculator in the wrong mode produce an answer that is not just slightly off, but wildly different — sometimes even the wrong sign?

4. Without converting to decimals, how could you tell whether 3 radians is more or less than \(180^\circ\)? What does your reasoning reveal about whether you understand the definition or are only running a formula?
