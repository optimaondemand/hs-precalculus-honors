Solving with Right Triangles: Elevation, Depression, Surveying
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to set up and solve angle-of-elevation and angle-of-depression problems using right-triangle trigonometry.

  • I will be able to solve a two-observation surveying problem, in which a common height is found from two right triangles that share it.

  • I will be able to carry the responsibility of a surveyor: stating my assumptions plainly, keeping exact and rounded values distinct, and explaining what a small measurement error would cost.

📚 VOCABULARY

Angle of elevation: The angle measured upward from a horizontal line of sight to an object above the observer.

Angle of depression: The angle measured downward from a horizontal line of sight to an object below the observer.

Line of sight: The straight segment connecting the observer's eye to the object being observed — the hypotenuse of every triangle in this lesson.

Baseline: In surveying, a carefully measured reference distance between two observation points, used together with angle measurements to locate a third point that cannot be reached directly.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In 1615, a Dutch astronomer named Willebrord Snellius set out to do what Eratosthenes had done nearly two thousand years earlier — measure the size of the Earth — but with a new technique. Instead of one angle and one long-distance measurement, Snellius built a chain of triangles stretching across the Dutch countryside: he measured one short baseline precisely with rods, then measured only angles from each triangle's vertices to the next, letting trigonometry compute every other distance in the chain without a single additional tape measure. This method, triangulation, became the standard tool for mapping nations for the next three centuries. A single measured baseline, extended honestly through hundreds of connected triangles, could place mountains, rivers, and borders on a map with a precision no chain of individual distance-measurements could ever match — because angles, unlike ropes and chains stretched across rough terrain, do not stretch, sag, or wear out.

Today you take the three ratios from yesterday's lesson out of the geometry classroom and into the field. Every problem below reduces to the same right triangle you have already mastered — the only new step is recognizing where the horizontal line of sight sits, and whether you are looking up from it or down.

Elevation and Depression Are the Same Angle, Seen from Two Ends

If an observer at point \(A\) looks up at an object at point \(B\) with an angle of elevation \(\theta\), then an observer standing at \(B\) looking down at \(A\) measures an angle of depression that is also \(\theta\) — the two horizontal lines of sight (one at \(A\), one at \(B\)) are parallel, and the line of sight \(AB\) is a transversal crossing both. The angle of elevation and the angle of depression are alternate interior angles, and alternate interior angles cut by a transversal across parallel lines are always congruent.

In every problem, the angle is always measured from the horizontal — never from the vertical, and never from the line of sight itself. Draw the horizontal line first, every time, before you draw anything else.

Example 1 — Angle of Elevation

A surveyor stands 200 feet from the base of a water tower and measures the angle of elevation to the top as \(35^\circ\). Find the height of the tower.

Solution:

The horizontal distance (200 ft) is adjacent to the \(35^\circ\) angle; the tower's height is opposite it. That is a tangent relationship:

\(\tan 35^\circ = \dfrac{h}{200} \;\Rightarrow\; h = 200\tan 35^\circ \approx 200(0.7002) \approx 140.04 \text{ ft}\)

Answer: The tower is approximately 140.0 ft tall.

Example 2 — Angle of Depression

A lighthouse keeper stands at the top of a lighthouse 60 meters above sea level and sights a boat at an angle of depression of \(12^\circ\). Find the horizontal distance from the base of the lighthouse to the boat.

Solution:

Draw the horizontal line of sight at the keeper's eye level. The angle of depression to the boat is \(12^\circ\), and by the alternate-interior-angles fact above, the angle of elevation from the boat up to the keeper is also \(12^\circ\). In the right triangle formed by the lighthouse, the sea, and the line of sight, the 60 m height is opposite that \(12^\circ\) angle, and the unknown horizontal distance \(d\) is adjacent to it:

\(\tan 12^\circ = \dfrac{60}{d} \;\Rightarrow\; d = \dfrac{60}{\tan 12^\circ} \approx \dfrac{60}{0.2126} \approx 282.3 \text{ m}\)

Answer: The boat is approximately 282.3 m from the base of the lighthouse.

Example 3 — A Surveying Problem: Two Stations, One Height

A survey crew wants the height of a tower but cannot reach its base (a river is in the way). From point \(A\), they measure the angle of elevation to the top of the tower as \(25^\circ\). They then walk 150 ft directly toward the tower to point \(B\) and measure the angle of elevation as \(40^\circ\). Find the height of the tower and the distance from \(B\) to the base of the tower.

Solution:

Let \(h\) be the tower's height and \(x\) be the (unknown) distance from \(B\) to the base. Since \(A\) is 150 ft farther back than \(B\), the distance from \(A\) to the base is \(x + 150\). Two right triangles share the same height \(h\):

From \(B\): \(\tan 40^\circ = \dfrac{h}{x} \;\Rightarrow\; h = x\tan 40^\circ\)

From \(A\): \(\tan 25^\circ = \dfrac{h}{x + 150} \;\Rightarrow\; h = (x + 150)\tan 25^\circ\)

Both expressions equal \(h\), so set them equal to each other and solve for \(x\):

\(x\tan 40^\circ = (x + 150)\tan 25^\circ\)

\(x\tan 40^\circ - x\tan 25^\circ = 150\tan 25^\circ\)

\(x(\tan 40^\circ - \tan 25^\circ) = 150\tan 25^\circ\)

\(x = \dfrac{150\tan 25^\circ}{\tan 40^\circ - \tan 25^\circ} \approx \dfrac{150(0.4663)}{0.8391 - 0.4663} = \dfrac{69.945}{0.3728} \approx 187.6 \text{ ft}\)

Now find \(h\) from either equation:

\(h = x\tan 40^\circ \approx 187.6(0.8391) \approx 157.4 \text{ ft}\)

Check with the other equation: \(h = (x + 150)\tan 25^\circ \approx (337.6)(0.4663) \approx 157.4\) ft ✓ — both triangles agree.

Answer: The tower is approximately 157.4 ft tall, and point \(B\) is approximately 187.6 ft from its base. Notice this problem needed no baseline touching the tower at all — exactly the situation a real survey crew faces when a river, a fence, or private property blocks direct access.

Key Fact: Two Triangles, One Shared Side

Whenever two right triangles are built from two different observation points to the same object, they share the object's height (or distance) as a common side. Writing that shared quantity two different ways — once from each triangle — turns two separate equations into one solvable equation in one unknown. This is the core move behind every two-station surveying problem, and it needs no tool beyond the tangent ratio you already know.

💻 On Your Calculator: Checking a Multi-Step Answer

6 min

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), verify Example 3 two ways:

  1. Type x = 150tan(25)/(tan(40)-tan(25)) in degree mode and confirm Desmos returns \(\approx 187.6\).
  2. Then type h = xtan(40) using that same \(x\), and separately h2 = (x+150)tan(25). If your algebra was correct, Desmos will show h and h2 as the same number — a built-in check that costs nothing extra.

▶  Try it yourself: From a point on the ground 40 ft from the base of a flagpole, the angle of elevation to the top is \(52^\circ\). Find the flagpole's height. Work it before you peek.
\(\tan 52^\circ = \frac{h}{40} \Rightarrow h = 40\tan 52^\circ \approx 40(1.2799) \approx 51.2\) ft.
▶  Try it yourself (honors): From point \(A\), the angle of elevation to a cliff top is \(20^\circ\). Walking 100 m toward the cliff to point \(B\), the angle becomes \(35^\circ\). Find the cliff's height and the distance from \(B\) to its base.
\(x\tan 35^\circ = (x+100)\tan 20^\circ \Rightarrow x = \frac{100\tan 20^\circ}{\tan 35^\circ - \tan 20^\circ} \approx \frac{100(0.3640)}{0.7002 - 0.3640} \approx \frac{36.40}{0.3362} \approx 108.3\) m. Then \(h = x\tan 35^\circ \approx 108.3(0.7002) \approx 75.8\) m. Check: \((x+100)\tan 20^\circ \approx (208.3)(0.3640) \approx 75.8\) m ✓.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Measuring the angle from the wrong reference line. Both the angle of elevation and the angle of depression are measured from a horizontal line of sight — never from the vertical side of the triangle, and never from the line of sight (the hypotenuse) itself. A student who mistakenly treats a given angle as measured from the vertical will swap sine and cosine (or the two legs of the tangent ratio) throughout the entire problem. Before writing any ratio, draw the horizontal dashed line at the observer's eye level first; only then mark the angle between that horizontal line and the line of sight to the object.
⚠️ AI CHECKPOINT: THE SUPERVISED TUTOR

A survey crew never gets to practice on the exact tower they'll measure tomorrow — they build skill on other problems first. Let your AI tutor generate you a fresh scenario in the style of Example 3, then put it on trial before you trust it:

“Write one two-station surveying problem: a tower or cliff of unknown height, an observer who walks a given distance toward it between two angle-of-elevation readings. Give the setup only — no solution — and choose numbers so the answer comes out as a clean decimal, not something wild.”

Before you touch the AI's numbers, do three things, in this order: (1) verify the problem itself is physically sensible — is the closer angle actually larger than the farther one? (It must be: standing closer, the same tower looks steeper.) (2) solve it yourself, in full, the way you solved Example 3. (3) ask the AI to solve its own problem, and compare its work to yours line by line — not just the final answer, but where it set up the shared height, and how it handled the algebra.

A model that gets the setup right nine times out of ten is exactly the kind that needs a human checking the tenth — and in surveying, an unnoticed error doesn't cost a homework point, it costs a foundation poured in the wrong place. The mathematician supervises the tool; the tool never supervises the mathematician.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The Great Trigonometric Survey of nineteenth-century India used exactly the two-station method of Example 3, scaled up across enormous distances, to measure the heights of Himalayan peaks that no one could climb to summit with instruments. In the 1850s, a mathematician on the survey team named Radhanath Sikdar calculated, from a network of distant angle-of-elevation readings, that a peak then known only as "Peak XV" was the tallest mountain on Earth — the peak later named Mount Everest. The story goes that the computed height came out to a suspiciously round 29,000 feet, and the survey published 29,002 feet instead, precisely so that no one would mistake a genuine trigonometric measurement for a careless guess. That is responsibility, recorded in a published number.

Conclusion

Sine, cosine, and tangent have now done real work: found a tower's height across a river no one could cross, placed a boat on the open sea, and reached a mountaintop no survey crew ever set foot on. Every one of this week's four lessons has stayed inside a single triangle at a time — a static picture, one angle, one calculation. Next week that constraint dissolves. The unit circle lets a single angle sweep continuously through every possible value, and sine and cosine stop being ratios trapped inside a triangle's corner and become full functions of a real number, ready to model anything that turns, swings, or repeats.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why are the angle of elevation from \(A\) to \(B\) and the angle of depression from \(B\) to \(A\) always equal? Name the geometric fact that guarantees it.

2. In the two-station surveying problem, why must the closer observation point always produce the larger angle of elevation? What would it mean if a problem gave you the opposite?

3. A survey crew's baseline measurement is off by just one percent. Walk through, in general terms, how that small error would travel through the tangent ratio and affect the final height. Why does this make careful measurement a matter of responsibility rather than mere procedure?

4. An AI tutor can generate a plausible-looking surveying problem in seconds. What two checks from today's AI Checkpoint protect you from trusting a plausible-looking problem that is actually wrong?
