Right Triangle Trigonometry

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to define sine, cosine, and tangent as ratios of sides in a right triangle (SOH-CAH-TOA).

  • I will be able to explain, using similar triangles, why these ratios depend only on the angle — never on the size of the triangle.

  • I will be able to find a missing side of a right triangle, given one side and one acute angle.

  • I will be able to find a missing acute angle of a right triangle, given two sides, using inverse trigonometric functions.

📚 VOCABULARY

Hypotenuse: The side of a right triangle opposite the right angle — always the longest side, and the side every ratio in this lesson is measured against.

Opposite side: For a chosen acute angle \(\theta\), the leg that does not touch \(\theta\) — the one "across the room" from it.

Adjacent side: For that same angle \(\theta\), the leg that does touch \(\theta\) (and is not the hypotenuse).

Sine, cosine, tangent: The three ratios \(\sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}\), \(\cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}\), \(\tan\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}\) — remembered by the mnemonic SOH-CAH-TOA.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Hipparchus built his chord table because a fixed angle, he realized, always comes bundled with a fixed relationship between the sides of the triangle it sits inside — no matter how large or small you draw that triangle. Today you meet the modern, right-triangle version of exactly that insight, distilled into three ratios so useful that surveyors, sailors, and engineers have carried them in their pockets for well over two thousand years.

Here is the claim, stated plainly: draw a right triangle with one acute angle equal to \(\theta\). Measure the ratio of the leg opposite \(\theta\) to the hypotenuse. Now draw a completely different right triangle — bigger, smaller, rotated, it makes no difference — as long as it also has an acute angle of \(\theta\). Measure the same ratio. It comes out identical, every time. That single fact is what allows sine, cosine, and tangent to be genuine functions of an angle alone: whatever triangle you happen to draw disappears from the answer, and only \(\theta\) survives.

Why the Ratios Don't Care About Size: Similar Triangles

Two right triangles that share an acute angle \(\theta\) automatically share two angles — that angle, and the right angle each one carries. By the Angle-Angle (AA) Similarity theorem, two triangles that agree on two angles are similar: same shape, possibly different size. And the defining property of similar triangles is that corresponding sides are all scaled by the same factor — so any ratio of two sides is unaffected by that scaling. It cancels out.

This is why \(\sin\theta\), \(\cos\theta\), and \(\tan\theta\) deserve to be called functions of \(\theta\) in the first place: for each value of \(\theta\), similar triangles guarantee there is exactly one value for each ratio, regardless of which triangle happens to be sitting in front of you.

Example 1 — The Similarity Guarantee, Made Concrete

Triangle A has legs 3 and 4 with hypotenuse 5. Triangle B has legs 6 and 8 with hypotenuse 10 — exactly twice the size. Both share the same acute angle \(\theta\) opposite the shorter leg. Show that \(\sin\theta\) comes out the same for both.

Solution:

Triangle A: \(\sin\theta = \dfrac{3}{5} = 0.6\)    Triangle B: \(\sin\theta = \dfrac{6}{10} = 0.6\)

Answer: Both give \(\sin\theta = 0.6\), exactly as similar triangles predict. Doubling every side length doubled the numerator and the denominator together — and the 2's canceled. This is not a coincidence about these particular numbers; it is what "similar" always does to a ratio.

Example 2

In a right triangle, an acute angle measures \(32^\circ\) and the hypotenuse is 10 units. Find the lengths of the two legs.

Solution:

Let the opposite leg be \(a\) and the adjacent leg be \(b\). By SOH-CAH:

\(\sin 32^\circ = \dfrac{a}{10} \;\Rightarrow\; a = 10\sin 32^\circ \approx 10(0.5299) \approx 5.30\)

\(\cos 32^\circ = \dfrac{b}{10} \;\Rightarrow\; b = 10\cos 32^\circ \approx 10(0.8480) \approx 8.48\)

Check: the legs should satisfy the Pythagorean theorem: \(5.30^2 + 8.48^2 \approx 28.09 + 71.91 = 100.00 = 10^2\) ✓

Answer: Opposite leg \(\approx 5.30\) units; adjacent leg \(\approx 8.48\) units.

Example 3

A right triangle has legs 7 and 24, with hypotenuse 25 (check: \(7^2 + 24^2 = 49 + 576 = 625 = 25^2\) ✓). Find both acute angles.

Solution:

Let \(\theta\) be the angle opposite the side of length 7. Then \(\sin\theta = \dfrac{7}{25} = 0.28\), so

\(\theta = \sin^{-1}(0.28) \approx 16.26^\circ\)

The two acute angles of a right triangle must sum to \(90^\circ\), so the other angle is \(90^\circ - 16.26^\circ = 73.74^\circ\).

Cross-check: using tangent instead, \(\tan\theta = \dfrac{7}{24} \approx 0.2917\), and \(\tan^{-1}(0.2917) \approx 16.26^\circ\) — the same answer, confirming the two ratios agree.

Answer: The acute angles are approximately \(16.26^\circ\) and \(73.74^\circ\).

Key Fact: Where Tangent Comes From

Tangent is not an independent idea — it is built entirely out of sine and cosine:

\(\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} = \dfrac{\text{opposite}/\text{hypotenuse}}{\text{adjacent}/\text{hypotenuse}} = \dfrac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}} = \tan\theta\)

The hypotenuse divides out of the compound fraction, leaving exactly the opposite-over-adjacent ratio. Keep this identity, \(\tan\theta = \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}\), close at hand — it will resurface constantly once the unit circle extends these ideas beyond right triangles next week.

💻 On Your Calculator: Ratios In, Angles Out (and Back)

6 min

At desmos.com/scientific (the scientific calculator is the fastest tool for single trig evaluations; the full graphing calculator embed below also has a keyboard for this):

  1. Confirm the calculator is in degree mode for this lesson (check yesterday's Common Mistake box if you are unsure how). Type sin(32) and confirm you get \(\approx 0.5299\), matching Example 2.
  2. Now go backward: type sin-1(0.28) (often the "2nd" or "shift" function on the sine key) and confirm you recover \(16.26^\circ\) from Example 3.
  3. Try tan-1(7/24) directly, without simplifying the fraction first — most calculators accept the division inside the function call, and it should return the same \(16.26^\circ\).

▶  Try it yourself: A right triangle has an acute angle of \(58^\circ\) and an adjacent leg of length 12. Find the hypotenuse and the opposite leg. Work it before you peek.
\(\cos 58^\circ = \frac{12}{h} \Rightarrow h = \frac{12}{\cos 58^\circ} \approx \frac{12}{0.5299} \approx 22.65\). Opposite leg: \(\tan 58^\circ = \frac{a}{12} \Rightarrow a = 12\tan 58^\circ \approx 12(1.6003) \approx 19.20\). Check: \(12^2 + 19.20^2 \approx 144 + 368.6 = 512.6\), and \(22.65^2 \approx 513.0\) — matching within rounding.
▶  Try it yourself (honors): Explain, without using a calculator, why \(\sin\theta\) can never be greater than 1 for an acute angle \(\theta\) in a right triangle.
The hypotenuse is always the longest side of a right triangle — it is opposite the largest angle, the right angle. So the opposite leg, whatever it is, can never exceed the hypotenuse in length. Since \(\sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}\) is a proper fraction with numerator no larger than its denominator, the ratio itself can never exceed 1. The same argument bounds \(\cos\theta\) by 1 as well — but places no such ceiling on \(\tan\theta\), since the two legs are not constrained relative to each other the way each leg is constrained relative to the hypotenuse.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Mixing up which leg is "opposite" when the labeled angle changes. In a right triangle with acute angles \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), the leg that is opposite \(\alpha\) is the very same leg that is adjacent to \(\beta\) — a leg's job title depends entirely on which angle you have currently chosen to focus on. Before writing a single ratio, physically re-read the triangle from the vantage point of the specific angle named in the problem: which leg is directly across from it (opposite), and which leg forms one of its two sides along with the hypotenuse (adjacent)? Skipping this re-check is the single most common cause of a sine problem accidentally becoming a cosine problem.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The words "sine" and "cosine" both trace back, indirectly, to a translation accident. Hindu mathematicians used a Sanskrit word, jya, for a half-chord in a circle. Arabic translators rendered the sound as jiba, which has no meaning of its own in Arabic — but resembles the Arabic word jaib, meaning "bay" or "fold of a garment." A later Latin translator, working from the Arabic text, translated jaib literally, giving Latin sinus — "bay" or "curve" — which is exactly the word English inherited as "sine." A misheard word about a half-chord became the name of one of the most-used functions in all of mathematics.

Conclusion

Similar triangles are the quiet reason any of this works at all: a fixed angle locks in a fixed set of ratios, no matter what triangle happens to carry it, so sine, cosine, and tangent are genuinely functions of the angle alone. You can now move fluently between a triangle's sides and its angles in either direction — given an angle, find a side; given two sides, find an angle. Tomorrow those same three ratios leave the geometry classroom and step into the field: elevation, depression, and the surveyor's baseline, where a measured angle and a measured distance become a height or a distance no one could reach with a tape measure alone — and where getting the angle right is no longer just a matter of grade, but of responsibility.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does AA similarity guarantee that \(\sin\theta\) has exactly one value for a given angle \(\theta\), regardless of which right triangle you draw?

2. If you double every side length of a right triangle, what happens to \(\sin\theta\), \(\cos\theta\), and \(\tan\theta\)? Explain why using the ratio itself, not just "because it's true."

3. Explain, in your own words, why \(\tan\theta = \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}\) — what cancels, and why?

4. Why can \(\sin\theta\) and \(\cos\theta\) never exceed 1 for an acute angle, while \(\tan\theta\) has no such ceiling?
