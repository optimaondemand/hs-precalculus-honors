📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
This is Week 19's Focus assignment — the one set this week your teacher grades for method. Every problem below is a version of a job a real survey crew has actually done: finding a height across an obstacle, placing a boat at sea, comparing two sightings from a cliff. The two gold-badged ⭐ METHOD PROBLEMS are the heart of the craft: one complete two-station triangulation with every step labeled, and one sensitivity check that asks what a one-degree instrument error would actually cost. Your teacher reads them as a surveyor's field record — the setup stated, the algebra shown in full, the verdict written as a sentence a real client could read and trust. Give every problem honest work, and give the method problems the full procedure with nothing skipped because it felt obvious.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
A surveyor's angle reading is never perfectly exact. What does it mean to carry the responsibility of that imprecision honestly, rather than simply reporting a number as if it were certain?
📝 PROBLEM SET

Show your work for every problem in neat vertical form — one step per line, equals signs aligned, final answers labeled and rounded sensibly. Draw a labeled diagram (horizontal line of sight marked explicitly) for every problem before writing a single ratio.

1
Angle of elevation: From a point 120 ft from the base of an office building, the angle of elevation to the roofline is \(48^\circ\). Find the building's height, rounded to the nearest tenth of a foot.
2
Angle of depression: From the top of an 85 m cliff, the angle of depression to a boat is \(9^\circ\). Find the horizontal distance from the base of the cliff to the boat, rounded to the nearest tenth of a meter.
⭐ METHOD PROBLEM — your teacher grades this one for method
3
A survey crew cannot reach the base of a radio tower (a highway is in the way). From point \(A\), the angle of elevation to the top of the tower is \(22^\circ\). The crew then walks 175 ft directly toward the tower to point \(B\) and measures the angle of elevation as \(38^\circ\). Complete the full discipline, every step labeled: ① diagram — draw both triangles sharing the tower's height \(h\), labeling the unknown distance \(x\) from \(B\) to the base; ② set up — write the tangent equation from each station; ③ solve — combine the two equations to solve for \(x\), then for \(h\), all algebra shown; ④ check — confirm both equations give the same \(h\); ⑤ verdict — state the tower's height and the distance from \(B\) to its base in a full sentence, exactly as it would appear in a survey report a client could read.
4
Two depressions, one comparison: From the top of a 45 m cliff, an observer sights a boat at an angle of depression of \(15^\circ\) and, farther out, a lighthouse at an angle of depression of \(6^\circ\). Find the horizontal distance from the boat to the lighthouse. (Find each horizontal distance from the base of the cliff first, then subtract.)
5
Explain why: In Problem 3, the angle at \(B\) (\(38^\circ\)) is larger than the angle at \(A\) (\(22^\circ\)). In three or four sentences, explain why the closer observation point must always produce the larger angle of elevation to the same object, and what it would mean if a real field crew's data showed the opposite.
⭐ METHOD PROBLEM — your teacher grades this one for method
6
A crew measures the angle of elevation to the top of a cell tower as \(30^\circ\) from a point 250 ft from its base, and reports the height. ① Compute the reported height using \(30^\circ\), showing full work. ② Suppose the instrument was actually misread by one degree, and the true angle was \(31^\circ\). Recompute the height using \(31^\circ\), full work shown. ③ Find the exact difference between the two computed heights. ④ In a full paragraph (four to six sentences), explain what this difference would mean for a real project — for example, if the tower's height determines a required clearance from a flight path or a property line — and argue for one specific practice a responsible surveyor should follow to guard against an error this size (for instance: taking a repeated reading, using a longer baseline, or stating a margin of error alongside the reported number). Connect your recommendation explicitly to the unit's virtue of responsibility.
7
Honors stretch: From the top of a 100 m cliff, a surveyor sights the base of a distant tower at an angle of depression of \(25^\circ\), and the top of that same tower at an angle of elevation of \(10^\circ\) (the tower is tall enough that its top rises above the cliff's own height). ① Find the horizontal distance from the base of the cliff to the base of the tower. ② Find how far the tower's top rises above the cliff-top's own elevation. ③ Combine your results to find the tower's total height, measured from its own base to its own top. ④ In one sentence, explain why this problem needed two right triangles sharing the same horizontal distance, rather than one.
💡 CHECK YOURSELF WITH DESMOS
After both method problems are done on paper, verify them in Desmos (desmos.com/calculator, degree mode): for problem 3, type x=175tan(22)/(tan(38)-tan(22)) and confirm it matches your hand-computed \(x\), then use it to confirm \(h\). For problem 6, type both height expressions and confirm the difference matches what you computed by hand. The calculator confirms the arithmetic; your written verdict is what makes the number mean something.

Understanding of the material
4 pts		 Method fits the problem exactly; the diagram, ratios, and setup are all correct and consistent with each other. Method is sound with a minor conceptual slip that does not derail the solution. Partial grasp; the approach starts correctly but loses the thread. The method does not match the mathematics being asked. 4 pts
Quality of reasoning
4 pts		 Every step follows from the last; the why is stated, and the check step is actually performed. Reasoning is mostly explicit; one link in the chain is asserted rather than shown. Steps are present but read as recipe-following; explanations restate what without why. Answer appears with little or no supporting reasoning. 4 pts
Presentation & precision
4 pts		 Clean vertical layout, aligned equals signs, a clearly labeled diagram, and a written verdict that reads like a real report. Organized and legible with small lapses in labeling or alignment. Readable but disorderly; the grader must hunt for the thread. Work is scattered or illegible; steps cannot be followed. 4 pts
Intellectual honesty
4 pts		 All work shown faithfully as done; the responsibility paragraph names a real, specific practice rather than a platitude. Work is genuine; one rough patch is smoothed over rather than acknowledged. Some steps appear reverse-engineered from the answer rather than worked. The page misrepresents how the work was actually done. 4 pts
