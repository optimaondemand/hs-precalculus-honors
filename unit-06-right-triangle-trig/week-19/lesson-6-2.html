Coterminal Angles, Arc Length, and Sectors
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to find angles coterminal with a given angle, in both degrees and radians, and identify the one lying in \([0^\circ, 360^\circ)\) or \([0, 2\pi)\).

  • I will be able to compute the arc length subtended by a central angle using \(s = r\theta\).

  • I will be able to compute the area of a circular sector using \(A = \frac{1}{2}r^2\theta\).

  • I will be able to reconstruct, in outline, how a single angle and a single measured distance let Eratosthenes estimate the size of the entire Earth.

📚 VOCABULARY

Coterminal angles: Two angles in standard position that share the same terminal side — they look identical when drawn, even though their measures differ by a whole number of full rotations (\(360^\circ k\) or \(2\pi k\), for any integer \(k\)).

Central angle: An angle whose vertex sits at the center of a circle. Every arc and every sector in this lesson is defined by one.

Arc length: The distance measured along the curved edge of a circle between the two points where a central angle's rays meet the circle — denoted \(s\).

Sector: The pie-slice-shaped region enclosed by a central angle and the arc it cuts off. Its sector area is the amount of the circle's interior that slice covers.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Sometime around 240 BC, the librarian of Alexandria — a scholar named Eratosthenes, who ran what may have been the largest library in the ancient world — heard a curious traveler's report: at noon on the summer solstice, in the city of Syene far to the south, sunlight reached straight down a deep well with no shadow at all. The sun, for one moment, stood directly overhead. Eratosthenes wondered what would happen if he measured the very same moment in his own city. On the solstice, he measured the shadow cast by a vertical stick in Alexandria and found the sun was not directly overhead there — it was off by an angle of about \(7.2^\circ\), which happens to be exactly \(\frac{1}{50}\) of a full circle. Today's lesson gives you the exact tool that made his next move possible.

If the sun's rays are essentially parallel by the time they reach the Earth (they are, because the sun is so far away), then the \(7.2^\circ\) angle Eratosthenes measured in Alexandria is the very same as the central angle, at the Earth's own center, between Syene and Alexandria. And a central angle, together with the arc it subtends, is precisely what today's lesson is about.

Coterminal Angles: Same Terminal Side, Different Story

Rotate \(30^\circ\), and you land somewhere. Rotate \(30^\circ + 360^\circ = 390^\circ\), and you land in the exact same place — you simply went all the way around once more. Angles \(30^\circ\) and \(390^\circ\) are coterminal: same terminal side, different number of trips around the circle.

To find an angle coterminal with a given angle \(\theta\), add or subtract whole-circle rotations: \(\theta + 360^\circ k\) (or \(\theta + 2\pi k\) in radians) for any integer \(k\). To land the answer in the standard window \([0^\circ, 360^\circ)\) or \([0, 2\pi)\), add or subtract just enough full rotations to get there — never a partial one.

Example 1

Find the angle coterminal with \(400^\circ\) in \([0^\circ, 360^\circ)\), and the angle coterminal with \(-100^\circ\) in the same interval.

Solution:

\(400^\circ - 360^\circ = 40^\circ\)   and   \(-100^\circ + 360^\circ = 260^\circ\)

Answer: \(400^\circ\) and \(40^\circ\) are coterminal; \(-100^\circ\) and \(260^\circ\) are coterminal. Subtract a full rotation when the angle overshoots past \(360^\circ\); add one when it is negative.

Example 2

Find the angle coterminal with \(\dfrac{13\pi}{4}\) in \([0, 2\pi)\), and the angle coterminal with \(-\dfrac{\pi}{3}\) in the same interval.

Solution:

\(\dfrac{13\pi}{4} - 2\pi = \dfrac{13\pi}{4} - \dfrac{8\pi}{4} = \dfrac{5\pi}{4}\)   and   \(-\dfrac{\pi}{3} + 2\pi = -\dfrac{\pi}{3} + \dfrac{6\pi}{3} = \dfrac{5\pi}{3}\)

Answer: \(\dfrac{13\pi}{4}\) and \(\dfrac{5\pi}{4}\) are coterminal; \(-\dfrac{\pi}{3}\) and \(\dfrac{5\pi}{3}\) are coterminal. Working in radians is no different in spirit — you are still adding or subtracting whole circles, just measured as \(2\pi\) instead of \(360^\circ\).

Key Facts: Arc Length and Sector Area

For a circle of radius \(r\) and a central angle \(\theta\) measured in radians:

Arc length: \(s = r\theta\)    Sector area: \(A = \dfrac{1}{2}r^2\theta\)

Both formulas demand radians — and now you can see exactly why. An arc length of \(r\theta\) works because a radian was defined as "one radius-length of arc per unit of angle"; multiply that rate by \(\theta\) radians and you get \(\theta\) radius-lengths of arc, i.e. \(r\theta\). Try that reasoning in degrees and it collapses — a "degree" carries no built-in length at all, so a degree-measured angle needs an extra conversion factor bolted on before any length formula will work. This is the radian's real payoff, promised at the end of yesterday's lesson.

Example 3

A circle has radius 10 cm. Find the arc length and sector area for a central angle of \(\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\) radians.

Solution:

\(s = r\theta = 10 \cdot \dfrac{2\pi}{3} = \dfrac{20\pi}{3} \approx 20.94\text{ cm}\)

\(A = \dfrac{1}{2}r^2\theta = \dfrac{1}{2}(100)\dfrac{2\pi}{3} = \dfrac{100\pi}{3} \approx 104.72\text{ cm}^2\)

Answer: Arc length \(\dfrac{20\pi}{3} \approx 20.94\) cm; sector area \(\dfrac{100\pi}{3} \approx 104.72\) cm\(^2\). Keep the exact form first, then round as a courtesy at the end.

Example 4

An arc 15 inches long is cut off by a central angle on a circle of radius 6 inches. Find the angle, in both radians and degrees.

Solution:

Solve \(s = r\theta\) for \(\theta\):

\(\theta = \dfrac{s}{r} = \dfrac{15}{6} = 2.5 \text{ radians}\)

Converting: \(2.5 \times \dfrac{180}{\pi} \approx 143.24^\circ\).

Answer: \(\theta = 2.5\) radians \(\approx 143.24^\circ\). Notice the formula only balances in radians — had you plugged in "\(15 = 6\theta\)" while secretly thinking of \(\theta\) in degrees, the arithmetic would have gone through and handed you a meaningless number.

Example 5 — Eratosthenes Measures the Earth

Eratosthenes measured the sun's angle from vertical in Alexandria as \(7.2^\circ\) — exactly \(\dfrac{1}{50}\) of a full circle — on the same day the sun stood directly overhead in Syene. He had the distance between the two cities measured at about 5,000 stadia (an ancient unit of length). Treating that distance as the arc length between the two cities on the Earth's surface, estimate the Earth's radius and circumference, in stadia.

Solution:

Convert the angle to radians: \(7.2^\circ \times \dfrac{\pi}{180} = \dfrac{\pi}{25}\) rad. Now solve \(s = r\theta\) for the Earth's radius \(r\), using \(s = 5{,}000\) stadia:

\(r = \dfrac{s}{\theta} = \dfrac{5{,}000}{\pi/25} = \dfrac{5{,}000 \times 25}{\pi} = \dfrac{125{,}000}{\pi} \approx 39{,}789 \text{ stadia}\)

Then the circumference: \(C = 2\pi r = 2\pi \left(\dfrac{125{,}000}{\pi}\right) = 250{,}000\) stadia.

Check it a second way, with no algebra at all: since \(7.2^\circ\) is exactly \(\frac{1}{50}\) of \(360^\circ\), the arc from Syene to Alexandria must be \(\frac{1}{50}\) of the entire circumference. So the circumference is simply \(50 \times 5{,}000 = 250{,}000\) stadia — matching the formula exactly.

Answer: Earth's radius \(\approx 39{,}789\) stadia; circumference \(= 250{,}000\) stadia. Historians still debate exactly how long an ancient "stadion" was, which makes converting this figure to modern miles or kilometers uncertain — but the method needs no such conversion to be understood. One measured angle, one measured distance, and the arc-length relationship you learned today: that is the entire calculation. A man in a library, without ever leaving Egypt, sized up the whole planet.

💻 In Desmos: Sliding the Angle, Watching the Arc

7 min

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), build a live sector:

  1. Create a slider for \(\theta\) (radian mode, range 0 to \(2\pi\)) and a slider for \(r\) (range 1 to 15).
  2. Plot the point \((r\cos\theta,\, r\sin\theta)\) — this is where the terminal side meets the circle. Also plot the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\).
  3. Type rθ as an expression and watch its value update live as you drag either slider — that number is exactly the arc length from \((r, 0)\) to your plotted point. Confirm it against a value you compute by hand.
  4. Drag \(\theta\) far past \(2\pi\) — say to \(4\pi + 0.3\). The point keeps landing in the same place every full turn: a moving picture of coterminal angles, and of why \(r\theta\) alone stops being "the arc length actually traveled" once you have gone around more than once.

▶  Try it yourself: Find the angle in \([0, 2\pi)\) coterminal with \(-\dfrac{11\pi}{6}\), and the angle in \([0^\circ, 360^\circ)\) coterminal with \(750^\circ\). Work both before you peek.
\(-\frac{11\pi}{6} + 2\pi = -\frac{11\pi}{6} + \frac{12\pi}{6} = \frac{\pi}{6}\).   \(750^\circ - 360^\circ = 390^\circ\), still too big, so subtract again: \(390^\circ - 360^\circ = 30^\circ\). (Two full rotations were hiding in \(750^\circ\): \(750 = 2(360) + 30\).)
▶  Try it yourself (honors): A sector has area 30 cm\(^2\) and central angle \(\dfrac{5\pi}{6}\). Find the radius.
\(A = \frac{1}{2}r^2\theta \Rightarrow 30 = \frac{1}{2}r^2 \cdot \frac{5\pi}{6} = \frac{5\pi}{12}r^2\). So \(r^2 = \frac{30 \cdot 12}{5\pi} = \frac{360}{5\pi} = \frac{72}{\pi}\), giving \(r = \sqrt{\frac{72}{\pi}} \approx 4.79\) cm.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Plugging a degree measure straight into \(s = r\theta\) or \(A = \frac{1}{2}r^2\theta\). These two formulas are only true when \(\theta\) is in radians — there is no version of them that works directly in degrees. If a problem gives you an angle in degrees, convert it to radians first, every time, before you touch either formula. A student who computes "\(s = r \cdot 45\)" using \(45^\circ\) has not made an arithmetic slip; they have used a formula outside the conditions under which it is true, and the resulting number, however confidently written down, is not an arc length at all.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Eratosthenes did not have GPS, satellites, or even a reliable way to measure the distance between Syene and Alexandria beyond hired pacers counting their steps across the desert — and yet historians credit his method with producing a result impressively close to the true size of the Earth. What made the method trustworthy was not exotic technology; it was the discipline of measuring one angle carefully, measuring one distance carefully, and trusting a relationship — arc length tied to central angle — that is true regardless of how big the circle is. The same discipline is what today's virtue, responsibility, asks of every measurement you take this unit: a small, honestly reported number, handled correctly, can answer an enormous question.

Conclusion

You can now name every angle that shares a terminal side with a given one, and you can turn a single angle and a single radius into an arc length or a sector area — the very tool that let a librarian in Alexandria weigh the whole Earth without leaving his desk. Notice what all of today's work still has in common with yesterday's: every formula, every coterminal-angle hunt, every measurement lived entirely inside a right-triangle-free world of circles and central angles. Tomorrow the circle gets folded back down into the shape you have used since middle school — the right triangle — and the three ratios waiting inside it, sine, cosine, and tangent, become the tools a surveyor actually carries into the field.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Two angles are coterminal exactly when they differ by a whole number of full rotations. Why does that mean there are infinitely many angles coterminal with any given angle?

2. Why must \(\theta\) be in radians for \(s = r\theta\) and \(A = \frac{1}{2}r^2\theta\) to work, and what specifically about the radian's definition makes that true?

3. Eratosthenes needed only one angle and one distance to estimate the size of the Earth. What assumption about sunlight made that single angle stand in for a central angle at the Earth's own core?

4. If a surveyor mis-measures a central angle by a small amount, what happens to the arc-length or sector-area answer as the radius (or the real-world distance involved) grows very large? Why does this make angle measurement a matter of responsibility, not just arithmetic?
