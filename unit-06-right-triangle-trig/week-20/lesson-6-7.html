The Six Trigonometric Functions
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to define tangent, cotangent, secant, and cosecant as ratios and reciprocals built from sine and cosine on the unit circle.

  • I will be able to find the value of any of the six trigonometric functions at an angle, given the coordinates of its terminal point or its sine and cosine.

  • I will be able to identify the domain of each of the six functions — which angles make it undefined, and why that follows directly from division by zero.

📚 VOCABULARY

Tangent (\(\tan\theta\)): The ratio \(\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}\) — on the unit circle, the \(y\)-coordinate divided by the \(x\)-coordinate of the terminal point.

Cotangent (\(\cot\theta\)): The reciprocal of tangent, \(\dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\).

Secant (\(\sec\theta\)): The reciprocal of cosine, \(\dfrac{1}{\cos\theta}\). Note the pairing: secant goes with cosine, not sine.

Cosecant (\(\csc\theta\)): The reciprocal of sine, \(\dfrac{1}{\sin\theta}\). By the same rule, cosecant goes with sine.

Domain: The complete set of input values (here, angles) for which a function produces an output. Today, four of the six functions have gaps in their domain; two never do.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Hipparchus's chord table needed only one function to do all its astronomical work; everything else could be built from it by arithmetic. That is still true today. Sine and cosine are the two primitive trigonometric functions — the two coordinates that actually define a point on the unit circle. The remaining four functions in this lesson's title are not new geometric ideas; they are four different arithmetic combinations of the same two coordinates, given their own names because each combination turns out to be exactly the quantity some later problem needs.

The Whole Family, Built from Two Coordinates

Let \(P(\theta) = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) be the terminal point, as in Lesson 6-5. Define:

\(\tan\theta = \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} \qquad \cot\theta = \dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta} = \dfrac{1}{\tan\theta}\)

\(\sec\theta = \dfrac{1}{\cos\theta} \qquad \csc\theta = \dfrac{1}{\sin\theta}\)

Notice the naming logic, which resolves most confusion on sight: "co-" always attaches to the function built from the other coordinate. Cosine is built from \(x\); cotangent and cosecant are the reciprocal-flavored functions that pair with sine's partner, cosine, in their definitions — secant pairs with cosine, cosecant pairs with sine, and the "co-" simply marks each function's counterpart across the sine/cosine divide.

Example 1

The terminal point of angle \(\theta\) is \(\left(-\dfrac{3}{5}, \dfrac{4}{5}\right)\). Find all six trigonometric function values at \(\theta\).

Solution:

Read \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\) directly from the coordinates, then build the rest:

\(\cos\theta = -\dfrac{3}{5}, \quad \sin\theta = \dfrac{4}{5}\)

\(\tan\theta = \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} = \dfrac{4/5}{-3/5} = -\dfrac{4}{3}, \qquad \cot\theta = \dfrac{1}{\tan\theta} = -\dfrac{3}{4}\)

\(\sec\theta = \dfrac{1}{\cos\theta} = -\dfrac{5}{3}, \qquad \csc\theta = \dfrac{1}{\sin\theta} = \dfrac{5}{4}\)

Answer: \(\sin\theta = \frac{4}{5}\), \(\cos\theta = -\frac{3}{5}\), \(\tan\theta = -\frac{4}{3}\), \(\cot\theta = -\frac{3}{4}\), \(\sec\theta = -\frac{5}{3}\), \(\csc\theta = \frac{5}{4}\). All six built from just two numbers.

Example 2

Using Lesson 6-6's derived values, find all six function values at \(\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{3}\).

Solution:

Recall \(\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\), \(\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{1}{2}\). Build the rest:

\(\tan\dfrac{\pi}{3} = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}/2}{1/2} = \sqrt{3}, \qquad \cot\dfrac{\pi}{3} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\)

\(\sec\dfrac{\pi}{3} = \dfrac{1}{1/2} = 2, \qquad \csc\dfrac{\pi}{3} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}/2} = \dfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}} = \dfrac{2\sqrt{3}}{3}\)

Answer: \(\sin\frac{\pi}{3}=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\), \(\cos\frac{\pi}{3}=\frac{1}{2}\), \(\tan\frac{\pi}{3}=\sqrt{3}\), \(\cot\frac{\pi}{3}=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\), \(\sec\frac{\pi}{3}=2\), \(\csc\frac{\pi}{3}=\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{3}\). One derivation from 6-6, six exact values today — nothing new was memorized, only combined.

Example 3

Explain why \(\tan\theta\) is undefined at \(\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{2}\), and identify every other angle where it is undefined.

Solution:

\(\tan\theta = \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}\), and at \(\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}\), the terminal point is \((0, 1)\), so \(\cos\frac{\pi}{2} = 0\). Division by zero is undefined, so \(\tan\frac{\pi}{2}\) does not exist.

Geometrically: \(\cos\theta = 0\) exactly when the terminal point sits on the \(y\)-axis — \(\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}\), and every angle coterminal with it or with \(\frac{3\pi}{2}\), i.e. \(\theta = \frac{\pi}{2} + k\pi\) for any integer \(k\).

Answer: \(\tan\theta\) (and, by the same reasoning, \(\sec\theta\), since it also has \(\cos\theta\) in the denominator) is undefined exactly at \(\theta = \frac{\pi}{2} + k\pi\) — wherever the terminal point lands on the \(y\)-axis.

Example 4

Identify every angle where \(\cot\theta\) and \(\csc\theta\) are undefined.

Solution:

Both \(\cot\theta = \frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\) and \(\csc\theta = \frac{1}{\sin\theta}\) have \(\sin\theta\) in the denominator. \(\sin\theta = 0\) exactly when the terminal point sits on the \(x\)-axis — the quadrantal angles \(\theta = 0, \pi, 2\pi, \ldots\), i.e., every integer multiple of \(\pi\).

Answer: \(\cot\theta\) and \(\csc\theta\) are undefined exactly at \(\theta = k\pi\) for any integer \(k\). Meanwhile \(\sin\theta\) and \(\cos\theta\) themselves are never undefined — they are literal coordinates of a point that always exists on the circle, for every real \(\theta\), with nothing to divide by.

Key Fact: Two Functions Are Never Undefined

Sine and cosine are coordinates — they exist for every angle on the circle, with a value always between \(-1\) and \(1\). The other four functions are built by division, and division fails exactly when the divisor is zero:

  • \(\tan\theta\) and \(\sec\theta\) divide by \(\cos\theta\) — undefined at \(\theta = \frac{\pi}{2} + k\pi\) (terminal point on the \(y\)-axis).
  • \(\cot\theta\) and \(\csc\theta\) divide by \(\sin\theta\) — undefined at \(\theta = k\pi\) (terminal point on the \(x\)-axis).

Every gap in every one of these four domains traces back to the same single cause: a coordinate hitting exactly zero.

💻 In Desmos: Watching a Function Break

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), type y = tan(x) and zoom out until you can see several periods. Watch what happens as the graph approaches \(x = \frac{\pi}{2}\) (type x = pi/2 as a second line to mark it): the curve shoots upward on one side and downward on the other, never touching that vertical line. That is the graph's way of drawing "undefined" — a full formal study of these shapes, with the name asymptote, is Unit 7's job; today, simply confirm that the break happens exactly where \(\cos x = 0\), by graphing y = cos(x) on the same screen and watching it cross zero at that very point.

▶  Try it yourself: Find all six function values at \(\theta = \pi\) (terminal point \((-1, 0)\)), noting which are undefined. Work it before you peek.
\(\cos\pi = -1\), \(\sin\pi = 0\). So \(\tan\pi = \frac{0}{-1} = 0\), \(\sec\pi = \frac{1}{-1} = -1\) — both defined, since \(\cos\pi \ne 0\). But \(\cot\pi = \frac{-1}{0}\) and \(\csc\pi = \frac{1}{0}\) are both undefined, since \(\sin\pi = 0\).
▶  Try it yourself: Using Lesson 6-6, find \(\sec\frac{\pi}{4}\) and \(\csc\frac{\pi}{4}\).
\(\cos\frac{\pi}{4} = \sin\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\), so \(\sec\frac{\pi}{4} = \csc\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}/2} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{2}} = \sqrt{2}\) — equal to each other, exactly because sine and cosine are equal at this one angle.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Pairing secant with sine and cosecant with cosine — exactly backward. The "co-" prefix names each function's counterpart, not its own coordinate: secant is built from cosine (\(\sec\theta = \frac{1}{\cos\theta}\)), and cosecant is built from sine (\(\csc\theta = \frac{1}{\sin\theta}\)). The safest habit is to never memorize the pairing directly — always rebuild secant and cosecant from sine and cosine on the spot, the way every worked example above did. A second common slip: writing \(\cot\theta = \frac{1}{\cos\theta}\) by confusing it with secant. Cotangent is the reciprocal of tangent, \(\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\) — a full ratio, not a single reciprocal.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The word tangent comes from the Latin tangens, "touching." A tangent line to a circle touches it at exactly one point without crossing it — and if you draw a vertical line tangent to the unit circle at \((1,0)\), the segment of that tangent line cut off by the angle's terminal side, extended, has length exactly \(\tan\theta\). The name is a literal geometric description, not an arbitrary label: tangent is the length you measure along a line that touches the circle.

Conclusion

Six functions, two coordinates: everything else was division. Tangent and cotangent are sine and cosine in ratio; secant and cosecant are their reciprocals. Four of the six functions carry gaps in their domain wherever a denominator hits zero — sine and cosine alone are defined everywhere, because they are the coordinates themselves, never a quotient. Tomorrow's lesson uses the circle's own mirror symmetry to reach angles you have not yet been handed a triangle for — expressing the trig values of \(\pi - x\), \(\pi + x\), and \(2\pi - x\) entirely in terms of the trig values of \(x\), extending Lesson 6-6's three special angles across the whole circle without deriving a single new triangle.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why are tangent, cotangent, secant, and cosecant defined as ratios or reciprocals of sine and cosine, rather than as independent ideas requiring their own new geometry?

2. Why is \(\tan\theta\) undefined exactly where \(\cos\theta = 0\) — what does that tell you about where the terminal point has landed?

3. Sine and cosine are the only two of the six functions with no domain restriction at all. What property of a coordinate, as opposed to a quotient, guarantees that?

4. If you know only \(\sin\theta\) and \(\cos\theta\) at some angle, how many of the six function values can you compute without ever consulting a triangle again?
