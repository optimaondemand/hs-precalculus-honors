Symmetry on the Circle: \(\pi - x\), \(\pi + x\), \(2\pi - x\)
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to locate the terminal points of \(\pi - x\), \(\pi + x\), and \(2\pi - x\) as reflections of the terminal point of \(x\) across an axis or through the origin.

  • I will be able to express \(\sin\) and \(\cos\) of \(\pi - x\), \(\pi + x\), and \(2\pi - x\) entirely in terms of \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\), using symmetry rather than a new triangle.

  • I will be able to combine today's three symmetries with Lesson 6-6's three special angles to find exact trigonometric values anywhere on the unit circle.

📚 VOCABULARY

Even function: A function \(f\) satisfying \(f(-x) = f(x)\) for every \(x\) — its graph is a mirror image across the \(y\)-axis. Today's lesson proves cosine is even.

Odd function: A function \(f\) satisfying \(f(-x) = -f(x)\) for every \(x\) — its graph has \(180°\)-rotational symmetry about the origin. Today's lesson proves sine is odd.

Reflection: A transformation that flips every point of a figure across a line (or through a point), producing its mirror image. Every identity today is one specific reflection of the unit circle, written in algebra.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Lesson 6-6 handed you exact values at three angles, all sitting quietly in Quadrant I. That looks like a small inheritance. It is not: the unit circle carries three simple symmetries, and each one turns a single Quadrant I fact into three more facts elsewhere on the circle, for free — no new triangle, no new derivation, only a reflection. This is the geometer's version of responsible economy: derive a fact once, honestly, and let symmetry deliver it wherever the circle repeats itself.

Three Reflections of the Unit Circle

Let \(x\) be any angle with terminal point \((\cos x, \sin x)\). Each of the following angles reflects that same point across a different axis of symmetry:

  • \(\pi - x\) reflects the point across the \(y\)-axis: \((\cos x, \sin x) \to (-\cos x, \sin x)\). The \(y\)-coordinate (sine) is untouched; only the \(x\)-coordinate (cosine) flips sign.
  • \(\pi + x\) reflects the point through the origin (a \(180°\) rotation): \((\cos x, \sin x) \to (-\cos x, -\sin x)\). Both coordinates flip sign.
  • \(2\pi - x\) reflects the point across the \(x\)-axis: \((\cos x, \sin x) \to (\cos x, -\sin x)\). The \(x\)-coordinate (cosine) is untouched; only the \(y\)-coordinate (sine) flips sign. (Since \(2\pi - x\) and \(-x\) share the same terminal point by periodicity, this is the same reflection that makes \(\cos(-x) = \cos x\) and \(\sin(-x) = -\sin x\).)

Written as identities:

\(\cos(\pi - x) = -\cos x \qquad \sin(\pi - x) = \sin x\)

\(\cos(\pi + x) = -\cos x \qquad \sin(\pi + x) = -\sin x\)

\(\cos(2\pi - x) = \cos x \qquad \sin(2\pi - x) = -\sin x\)

Example 1

Find the exact values of \(\cos\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\) and \(\sin\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\) using symmetry and Lesson 6-6.

Solution:

Recognize \(\dfrac{2\pi}{3} = \pi - \dfrac{\pi}{3}\), so this is the \(\pi - x\) reflection with \(x = \frac{\pi}{3}\):

\(\cos\dfrac{2\pi}{3} = -\cos\dfrac{\pi}{3} = -\dfrac{1}{2}, \qquad \sin\dfrac{2\pi}{3} = \sin\dfrac{\pi}{3} = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\)

Answer: \(\cos\frac{2\pi}{3} = -\frac{1}{2}\), \(\sin\frac{2\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\). Check with the Pythagorean identity: \(\frac{1}{4} + \frac{3}{4} = 1\) ✓.

Example 2

Find the exact values of \(\sin\dfrac{7\pi}{6}\) and \(\cos\dfrac{7\pi}{6}\).

Solution:

Recognize \(\dfrac{7\pi}{6} = \pi + \dfrac{\pi}{6}\), the \(\pi + x\) reflection with \(x = \frac{\pi}{6}\):

\(\sin\dfrac{7\pi}{6} = -\sin\dfrac{\pi}{6} = -\dfrac{1}{2}, \qquad \cos\dfrac{7\pi}{6} = -\cos\dfrac{\pi}{6} = -\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\)

Answer: \(\sin\frac{7\pi}{6} = -\frac{1}{2}\), \(\cos\frac{7\pi}{6} = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\) — both negative, exactly as expected since \(\frac{7\pi}{6}\) lands in Quadrant III, where both coordinates are negative.

Example 3

Find the exact values of \(\cos\dfrac{11\pi}{6}\) and \(\sin\dfrac{11\pi}{6}\).

Solution:

Recognize \(\dfrac{11\pi}{6} = 2\pi - \dfrac{\pi}{6}\), the \(2\pi - x\) reflection with \(x = \frac{\pi}{6}\):

\(\cos\dfrac{11\pi}{6} = \cos\dfrac{\pi}{6} = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \qquad \sin\dfrac{11\pi}{6} = -\sin\dfrac{\pi}{6} = -\dfrac{1}{2}\)

Answer: \(\cos\frac{11\pi}{6} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\), \(\sin\frac{11\pi}{6} = -\frac{1}{2}\) — Quadrant IV, cosine positive and sine negative, exactly as the reflection across the \(x\)-axis predicts.

Example 4

Prove that cosine is an even function and sine is an odd function, using the unit circle.

Solution:

The angle \(-x\) reflects the terminal point of \(x\) across the \(x\)-axis, exactly like \(2\pi - x\) above (they share a terminal point by periodicity): \((\cos x, \sin x) \to (\cos x, -\sin x)\). Reading the coordinates directly:

\(\cos(-x) = \cos x \qquad \sin(-x) = -\sin x\)

Answer: \(\cos(-x) = \cos x\) is exactly the definition of an even function; \(\sin(-x) = -\sin x\) is exactly the definition of an odd function. Both follow from one reflection, with no new geometry beyond what \(2\pi - x\) already established.

Example 5

Find all six trigonometric function values at \(\theta = \dfrac{5\pi}{4}\).

Solution:

\(\dfrac{5\pi}{4} = \pi + \dfrac{\pi}{4}\), the \(\pi + x\) reflection with \(x = \frac{\pi}{4}\):

\(\cos\dfrac{5\pi}{4} = -\cos\dfrac{\pi}{4} = -\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \qquad \sin\dfrac{5\pi}{4} = -\sin\dfrac{\pi}{4} = -\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\)

Now build the remaining four functions as in Lesson 6-7:

\(\tan\dfrac{5\pi}{4} = \dfrac{-\sqrt{2}/2}{-\sqrt{2}/2} = 1, \qquad \cot\dfrac{5\pi}{4} = 1, \qquad \sec\dfrac{5\pi}{4} = -\sqrt{2}, \qquad \csc\dfrac{5\pi}{4} = -\sqrt{2}\)

Answer: All six values recovered from one symmetry and one Lesson 6-6 fact — \(\sin\frac{5\pi}{4} = \cos\frac{5\pi}{4} = -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\), \(\tan\frac{5\pi}{4} = \cot\frac{5\pi}{4} = 1\), \(\sec\frac{5\pi}{4} = \csc\frac{5\pi}{4} = -\sqrt{2}\).

Key Fact: Three Values Become Twelve

Lesson 6-6 derived exact sine and cosine at \(\frac{\pi}{6}\), \(\frac{\pi}{4}\), and \(\frac{\pi}{3}\) — all in Quadrant I. Today's three symmetries each place a reflected copy of every one of those angles into a different quadrant:

  • \(\pi - x\) sends a Quadrant I angle into Quadrant II.
  • \(\pi + x\) sends it into Quadrant III.
  • \(2\pi - x\) sends it into Quadrant IV.

Three special angles, four quadrants each: twelve non-quadrantal angles on the circle now have fully known exact values, all traceable to two triangles and three reflections — no additional derivation required. (One number still floats free of the pattern: the sign of each result always matches the target quadrant, which is exactly why checking the quadrant is never optional.)

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Applying the same sign rule to every symmetry. The three reflections are genuinely different: \(\pi - x\) flips only cosine's sign, \(\pi + x\) flips both, and \(2\pi - x\) flips only sine's sign. Writing \(\sin(\pi - x) = -\sin x\) — borrowing the \(\pi+x\) rule by mistake — is the single most common error here, and it is easy to catch: \(\pi - x\) for an acute \(x\) always lands in Quadrant II, where sine is positive, so \(\sin(\pi-x)\) can never come out negative when \(\sin x\) is positive. Before trusting any sign, name the target quadrant first and check that your answer's signs actually match it.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Today's three reflections are a small preview of a much larger idea. Two centuries after Hipparchus, the astronomer Ptolemy proved a general theorem about chords in a circle inscribed inside a quadrilateral — a result now called Ptolemy's theorem — from which the full sum and difference formulas for sine and cosine can be derived. Those formulas, arriving in Unit 7, will make today's symmetries look like special cases of one master identity. For now, the circle's mirror symmetry alone is doing honest, sufficient work.

Conclusion

Three reflections, one Quadrant I inheritance from Lesson 6-6, and the exact values on the unit circle multiplied fourfold — twelve non-quadrantal angles now fully known, each traced back to a triangle you built yourself. You also caught cosine and sine's oldest habits along the way: cosine is even, sine is odd, both provable from the single reflection across the \(x\)-axis. This closes Week 20's arc: from coordinates (6-5), to derived special values (6-6), to the full family of six functions (6-7), to symmetry across the whole circle (6-8). Next week gathers all of it into one systematic method — reference angles — for evaluating any of the six functions at any angle whatsoever, on the way to building the complete unit circle from memory of only the first quadrant.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does reflecting a unit-circle point across the \(y\)-axis change the sign of cosine but leave sine untouched — and what identity does that reflection draw directly?

2. Why are \(\pi - x\) and \(\pi + x\) genuinely different symmetries, even though both send a Quadrant I angle somewhere else on the circle?

3. Why must \(\cos(2\pi - x) = \cos x\) hold exactly, and how does that fact make cosine an even function?

4. Using only Lesson 6-6's three special angles and today's three symmetries, how many exact trigonometric values can you now compute on the full circle without deriving a single new triangle?
