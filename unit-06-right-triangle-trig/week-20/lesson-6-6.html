Special Angles and Special Triangles

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to derive the exact side lengths of the 45-45-90 and 30-60-90 triangles from the Pythagorean theorem and a bisected equilateral triangle, rather than memorizing them.

  • I will be able to derive the exact values of sine, cosine, and tangent at \(\frac{\pi}{6}\), \(\frac{\pi}{4}\), and \(\frac{\pi}{3}\) from those two triangles.

  • I will be able to verify a derived special-angle value against the unit circle's identity \(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1\), catching an arithmetic slip before it becomes a memorized error.

📚 VOCABULARY

45-45-90 triangle: An isosceles right triangle. Its two legs are equal, and its hypotenuse is always \(\sqrt{2}\) times a leg.

30-60-90 triangle: Half of an equilateral triangle, split along an altitude. Its three sides always stand in the ratio \(1 : \sqrt{3} : 2\).

Exact value: A trigonometric value expressed as a precise number — \(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\), not \(0.866\). Exact values carry no rounding error into later work.

Rationalize the denominator: Rewriting a fraction with an irrational denominator, such as \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\), so the irrational part sits on top instead: \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\). Both name the same number; the second is the form this course reports.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Hipparchus computed his table of chords the hard way: angle by angle, by careful geometric construction, because no shortcut existed. You have a shortcut he lacked, and it comes from a much older source — Euclid's Elements, three centuries before Hipparchus, had already proven everything needed about equilateral triangles, isosceles triangles, and the Pythagorean theorem. Combine those three old facts correctly, and six of Hipparchus's table entries fall out in a few lines, exact, with no measurement and no table at all. That is today's craft: not looking up \(\sin\frac{\pi}{6}\), but building the triangle that forces it to equal what it equals.

Triangle One: 45-45-90, Cut from a Square

Take a square of side 1 and slice it along a diagonal. Each half is an isosceles right triangle: two legs of length 1, meeting at a right angle, with acute angles of \(45°\) each (the diagonal bisects the square's right angle by symmetry). The Pythagorean theorem gives the hypotenuse:

\(c = \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}\)

So the 45-45-90 triangle always has sides in the ratio \(1 : 1 : \sqrt{2}\), regardless of size. Reading off \(\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}\) and \(\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}\) against this triangle:

\(\sin\dfrac{\pi}{4} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \qquad \cos\dfrac{\pi}{4} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \qquad \tan\dfrac{\pi}{4} = \dfrac{1}{1} = 1\)

Triangle Two: 30-60-90, Cut from an Equilateral Triangle

Take an equilateral triangle \(ABC\) with every side of length 2 and every angle \(60°\). Drop the altitude from vertex \(A\) to the midpoint \(M\) of side \(BC\). Because the triangle is equilateral, this altitude is also a median and an angle bisector: it splits \(BC\) into two segments of length 1, and it splits the \(60°\) angle at \(A\) into two \(30°\) angles.

Look at the right triangle \(ABM\): the right angle sits at \(M\), the angle at \(B\) is the original \(60°\), and the angle at \(A\) is the new \(30°\). Its hypotenuse \(AB\) is a full side of the original triangle, length 2; its short leg \(BM\) is half a side, length 1. The Pythagorean theorem gives the altitude:

\(AM = \sqrt{2^2 - 1^2} = \sqrt{3}\)

So the 30-60-90 triangle always has sides in the ratio \(1 : \sqrt{3} : 2\) — the side opposite \(30°\) is the shortest (1), the side opposite \(60°\) is \(\sqrt{3}\), and the hypotenuse opposite the right angle is 2. Reading off the ratios for both acute angles:

\(\sin\dfrac{\pi}{6} = \dfrac{1}{2}, \quad \cos\dfrac{\pi}{6} = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \quad \tan\dfrac{\pi}{6} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\)

\(\sin\dfrac{\pi}{3} = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \quad \cos\dfrac{\pi}{3} = \dfrac{1}{2}, \quad \tan\dfrac{\pi}{3} = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{1} = \sqrt{3}\)

Example 1

Verify that the point \(\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{6}, \sin\frac{\pi}{6}\right) = \left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right)\) actually lies on the unit circle.

Solution:

Check \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\), the standard Lesson 6-5 verification:

\(\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)^2 + \left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^2 = \dfrac{3}{4} + \dfrac{1}{4} = 1\)

Answer: Confirmed — the derived point satisfies the identity exactly. This is not a coincidence: the 30-60-90 triangle's hypotenuse was built to equal 2, and dividing every side by 2 to reach the unit circle (radius 1) is precisely what produces \(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\) and \(\frac{1}{2}\) as coordinates.

Example 2

A ramp makes an angle of \(\dfrac{\pi}{4}\) with the ground and reaches a height of 3 meters. Find the horizontal distance it covers and the length of the ramp itself.

Solution:

In a 45-45-90 triangle, the two legs are equal. Since the vertical leg (height) is 3 m, the horizontal leg is also 3 m. For the hypotenuse, use the fixed ratio \(1:1:\sqrt{2}\), scaled by the actual leg length of 3:

\(\text{ramp length} = 3\sqrt{2} \text{ m} \approx 4.24 \text{ m}\)

Answer: Horizontal distance \(= 3\) m; ramp length \(= 3\sqrt{2} \approx 4.24\) m. No new trigonometry was needed here at all — only the fixed side ratio of the 45-45-90 triangle, scaled to fit.

Example 3

Find the exact value of \(\tan\dfrac{\pi}{3} + \sec\dfrac{\pi}{6}\), where \(\sec\theta = \dfrac{1}{\cos\theta}\).

Solution:

From the 30-60-90 derivation above: \(\tan\frac{\pi}{3} = \sqrt{3}\) and \(\cos\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\), so \(\sec\frac{\pi}{6} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}/2} = \dfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}} = \dfrac{2\sqrt{3}}{3}\).

\(\sqrt{3} + \dfrac{2\sqrt{3}}{3} = \dfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{3} + \dfrac{2\sqrt{3}}{3} = \dfrac{5\sqrt{3}}{3}\)

Answer: \(\dfrac{5\sqrt{3}}{3}\). Exact throughout — no decimal ever entered the computation, and none is needed until the very end, if at all.

Example 4

Without a calculator, decide which is larger: \(\sin\dfrac{\pi}{3}\) or \(\cos\dfrac{\pi}{3}\). Justify from the triangle, not a decimal.

Solution:

In the 30-60-90 triangle, the \(60°\) angle sits opposite the longer leg (length \(\sqrt{3}\)) and adjacent to the shorter leg (length 1), both measured against the same hypotenuse of 2. So \(\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\) (built from the longer leg) must exceed \(\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{1}{2}\) (built from the shorter leg), since \(\sqrt{3} > 1\).

Answer: \(\sin\frac{\pi}{3} > \cos\frac{\pi}{3}\), because \(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} > \frac{1}{2}\) — a comparison read directly off which leg is longer, with no decimal approximation required.

Key Fact: The Hexagon Was There First

The 30-60-90 triangle is not an isolated trick — it is one-sixth of a very old shape. Inscribe a regular hexagon in a circle of radius \(r\): connecting the center to each of the six vertices divides the hexagon into six equilateral triangles, each with two sides equal to the radius \(r\) and central angle exactly \(60°\). Bisect any one of those six triangles, and the 30-60-90 triangle appears again — this time built directly out of the circle itself. Ancient astronomers used inscribed hexagons and squares as their first, most reliable chord measurements for exactly this reason: the hexagon's side equals the radius, no measurement required, only proof.

This is why \(\frac{\pi}{6}\) (\(30°\)), \(\frac{\pi}{4}\) (\(45°\)), and \(\frac{\pi}{3}\) (\(60°\)) are called the special angles: they are exactly the angles a hexagon, a square, and an equilateral triangle hand you for free when inscribed in a circle.

▶  Try it yourself: Derive \(\cot\frac{\pi}{4}\), where \(\cot\theta = \frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\), directly from the 45-45-90 triangle. Work it before you peek.
Both legs of the 45-45-90 triangle equal 1, so \(\cot\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{\cos(\pi/4)}{\sin(\pi/4)} = \frac{\sqrt{2}/2}{\sqrt{2}/2} = 1\) — the same value as \(\tan\frac{\pi}{4}\), because the two legs being equal makes tangent and cotangent equal at this one angle.
▶  Try it yourself: A 30-60-90 triangle has hypotenuse 10. Find both legs.
The base ratio \(1:\sqrt{3}:2\) scales by a factor of 5 to reach hypotenuse 10 (since \(2 \times 5 = 10\)). Short leg \(= 1 \times 5 = 5\); long leg \(= \sqrt{3} \times 5 = 5\sqrt{3} \approx 8.66\).
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Swapping \(\sin\frac{\pi}{6}\) and \(\cos\frac{\pi}{6}\) — or, worse, swapping them for \(\frac{\pi}{3}\) too, and ending up unable to tell which value goes with which angle at all. The fix is not a mnemonic; it is the triangle itself: at the smaller angle \(\frac{\pi}{6}\), the opposite leg is the shorter one (length 1), so \(\sin\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{1}{2}\) is the smaller of the pair. At the larger angle \(\frac{\pi}{3}\), the opposite leg is the longer one (\(\sqrt{3}\)), so \(\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\) is the larger. A bigger angle in a right triangle always sits opposite a longer side — that single geometric fact, not a memorized list, tells you which value is which every time. A second, sneakier slip: leaving \(\tan\frac{\pi}{6}\) as \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\) and calling it done. This course reports rationalized denominators: \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\).
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
A regular hexagon's side length exactly equals the radius of its circumscribed circle — a fact Euclid proves in Book IV of the Elements, and one ancient builders and astronomers leaned on constantly, since it meant a compass alone (no protractor) could step off six equal chords all the way around a circle. Hipparchus's earliest chord tables likely began exactly there: the hexagon's free \(60°\), the square's free \(90°\), and careful bisection filling in everything between.

Conclusion

Two cuts — a square along its diagonal, an equilateral triangle along its altitude — handed you six exact values by proof rather than by table. \(\frac{\pi}{6}\), \(\frac{\pi}{4}\), and \(\frac{\pi}{3}\) are special precisely because a hexagon, a square, and an equilateral triangle deliver them at no cost when inscribed in a circle. You checked every derived value against \(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1\) — the same discipline that will keep you honest for the rest of the unit. Tomorrow those two ratios, sine and cosine, get four companions: tangent, cotangent, secant, and cosecant, all built from the same triangle, none requiring a single new derivation.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does bisecting an equilateral triangle produce a 30-60-90 triangle, and how does that single bisection hand you six exact trig values at once?

2. What is gained by deriving these values from a triangle rather than memorizing a table — the very question Hipparchus's era could not yet avoid answering by hand?

3. How does the identity \(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1\) let you catch an arithmetic mistake in a derivation before it hardens into a memorized error?

4. The 30-60-90 and 45-45-90 triangles are the only two "special" right triangles in this course. What is special about them, geometrically, that no other right triangle shares?
