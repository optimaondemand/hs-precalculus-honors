Recursive Definitions and Factorial Notation
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to write the terms of a sequence defined by a recursive formula, whether it starts from one seed term or two.

  • I will be able to translate between recursive and explicit definitions of the same sequence and verify that both produce identical terms.

  • I will be able to evaluate and simplify expressions written in factorial notation, including quotients of factorials.

📚 VOCABULARY

Recursive formula: A formula that defines each term of a sequence using one or more of the terms that come before it. It must always state the initial term or terms.

Initial term (seed): The starting value(s) a recursive formula declares outright, from which every later term grows.

Fibonacci sequence: The sequence \(1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, \ldots\), in which each term is the sum of the two preceding terms.

Factorial: For a positive integer \(n\), the product of the integers from \(n\) down to 1, written \(n!\). By definition, \(0! = 1\).

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In the last lesson we promised you rabbits. In Liber Abaci (1202), Fibonacci posed a puzzle: begin with one newborn pair of rabbits; each month, every pair old enough to breed produces a new pair. How many pairs are there after a year? Counting month by month gives the list

\(1, \; 1, \; 2, \; 3, \; 5, \; 8, \; 13, \; 21, \; 34, \; \ldots\)

Look for an explicit formula the way you learned in Lesson 1-1 and you will hunt a long time — there is no obvious rule that computes term 9 from the number 9. But say how the list grows and the whole thing collapses into one sentence: each term is the sum of the two before it. That is a different kind of rule. Instead of computing a term from its position, it computes a term from its predecessors — the sequence stands on its own shoulders. Nature seems fond of this kind of growth: the spiral chambers of a nautilus shell, the arrangement of pinecone scales, and the petal counts of many flowers all echo Fibonacci’s numbers, because each new stage of growth is built from the stages already there.

Rules of this kind are called recursive — from the Latin recurrere, “to run back.” To find a term, you run back to the terms before it. This lesson teaches you to read, write, and use recursive definitions — and then introduces factorial notation, a piece of shorthand that is itself recursive at heart and that you will use for the rest of this course.

Recursive Formulas

A recursive formula has two required parts:

  • The initial term or terms (the seeds), stated outright: \(a_1 = 5\), or for Fibonacci, \(a_1 = 1\) and \(a_2 = 1\).
  • A rule defining \(a_n\) from preceding terms: for example, \(a_n = a_{n-1} + a_{n-2}\).

The Fibonacci sequence in full recursive dress:

\(\Large a_1 = 1, \quad a_2 = 1, \quad a_n = a_{n-1} + a_{n-2} \; \text{ for } n \ge 3\)

The seeds are not decoration — they are half the definition. The rule “add the two previous terms” with seeds \(2\) and \(1\) produces \(2, 1, 3, 4, 7, 11, \ldots\) (a different famous sequence, the Lucas numbers). Same engine, different starting fuel, different sequence.

A rule that reaches back only one term needs only one seed; a rule like Fibonacci’s that reaches back two terms needs two.

Example 1

Write the first five terms of the sequence defined by

\(\Large a_1 = 5, \quad a_n = 2a_{n-1} - 3\)

Solution:

The first term is handed to us. Every later term feeds on the one before it, so we must work in order — no skipping ahead:

\(a_1 = 5\)

\(a_2 = 2a_1 - 3 = 2(5) - 3 = 7\)

\(a_3 = 2a_2 - 3 = 2(7) - 3 = 11\)

\(a_4 = 2a_3 - 3 = 2(11) - 3 = 19\)

\(a_5 = 2a_4 - 3 = 2(19) - 3 = 35\)

Answer: The first five terms are \(5, \; 7, \; 11, \; 19, \; 35\).

Notice what the recursion costs you: to know \(a_5\) you had to earn \(a_2\), \(a_3\), and \(a_4\) first. There is no leaping to term 50 — only the patient climb. That is the trade-off between the two kinds of definition, and we will weigh it carefully below.

Example 2

Write the first six terms of the sequence defined by

\(\Large a_1 = 1, \quad a_2 = 3, \quad a_n = a_{n-1} + a_{n-2} \; \text{ for } n \ge 3\)

Solution:

Two seeds are given because the rule reaches back two places. Each new term is the sum of the two most recent:

\(a_1 = 1\)

\(a_2 = 3\)

\(a_3 = a_2 + a_1 = 3 + 1 = 4\)

\(a_4 = a_3 + a_2 = 4 + 3 = 7\)

\(a_5 = a_4 + a_3 = 7 + 4 = 11\)

\(a_6 = a_5 + a_4 = 11 + 7 = 18\)

Answer: The first six terms are \(1, \; 3, \; 4, \; 7, \; 11, \; 18\).

Key Fact: Two Languages, One Sequence

Many sequences can be described both ways, and each language has its strength:

Explicit — \(a_n\) from the position \(n\).
Strength: random access. Term 500 is one substitution away.		 Recursive — \(a_n\) from preceding terms.
Strength: natural description. Growth processes (populations, balances, rabbit pairs) are usually easiest to state as “next = rule applied to current.”

Translating between the two languages is a core skill of this unit. Next week you will see that the two great families of sequences — arithmetic and geometric — each speak both languages fluently.

Example 3

The sequence defined by \(a_1 = 4\) and \(a_n = a_{n-1} + 3\) adds 3 at every step. Find an explicit formula for the same sequence, and verify that the two definitions agree.

Solution:

Unroll a few terms to see the structure: \(4, \; 7, \; 10, \; 13, \; \ldots\) By the time we reach \(a_n\), the rule has added 3 exactly \(n - 1\) times to the seed:

\(a_n = 4 + 3(n-1) = 3n + 1\)

Verify against the recursive definition:

\(a_1 = 3(1) + 1 = 4\) ✓ (matches the seed)

\(a_2 = 3(2) + 1 = 7\) ✓ and \(a_3 = 3(3) + 1 = 10\) ✓ (each 3 more than the last, as the rule demands)

Answer: \(a_n = 3n + 1\).

The reasoning move — “the rule has acted \(n-1\) times by term \(n\)” — is the bridge between the two languages, and it is worth owning rather than memorizing.

Example 4

Write a recursive definition for the sequence given explicitly by \(a_n = 5 \cdot 2^{\,n-1}\).

Solution:

List the first few terms: \(5, \; 10, \; 20, \; 40, \; \ldots\) Each term is double the one before it. The seed is \(a_1 = 5\), and the rule is “multiply by 2”:

\(\Large a_1 = 5, \quad a_n = 2a_{n-1}\)

Check: the recursion gives \(a_2 = 2(5) = 10\) and the explicit formula gives \(a_2 = 5 \cdot 2^1 = 10\) ✓.

Answer: \(a_1 = 5, \; a_n = 2a_{n-1}\).

📊 Try It in Excel: Teaching a Spreadsheet to Recurse 15 min

A spreadsheet is a recursion machine: a formula that refers to the cell above it is exactly a rule that refers to \(a_{n-1}\). Open a blank workbook in Excel and build Example 1:

  1. In column A, type 1 in cell A1 and 2 in A2. Select both cells and drag the fill handle (the small square at the corner of the selection) down to A10. Column A now holds the positions \(n\).
  2. In cell B1, type the seed: 5.
  3. In cell B2, type the rule: =2*B1-3 and press Enter.
  4. Click B2 and drag its fill handle down to B10. Excel rewrites the rule for each row — B3 becomes =2*B2-3, and so on. Read down the column: \(5, 7, 11, 19, 35, \ldots\) — your Example 1 terms, computed by the same patient climb you did by hand.
  5. Now build Fibonacci with two seeds: type 1 in C1 and 1 in C2, then in C3 type =C1+C2 and fill down to C10. There are the rabbits.

Try changing a seed — make C1 equal 2 and C2 equal 1 — and watch every term downstream change instantly. The spreadsheet makes visible what the definition says: in a recursive sequence, everything grows from the seeds. Nothing to submit here; this tool returns in Lesson 1-4, where you will model real situations with it.

Factorial Notation

Some formulas call for the product of all the positive integers up to \(n\). This happens so often — especially in counting, probability, and (later) calculus — that it earned its own symbol. \(n\) factorial, written \(n!\), means

\(\Large n! = n \,(n-1)(n-2) \cdots (2)(1)\)

For example, \(4! = 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 24\). The definition is recursive at heart: each factorial is built from the one before it,

\(\Large n! = n \cdot (n-1)!\)

with the special case

\(\Large 0! = 1\)

Why must \(0! = 1\)? Apply the recursive rule at \(n = 1\): it says \(1! = 1 \cdot 0!\). Since \(1! = 1\), we are forced to accept \(0! = 1\) — not as a convenience, but as the only value consistent with the pattern. (It also matches common sense in counting: there is exactly one way to arrange zero objects — do nothing.)

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Factorials grow faster than any exponential — faster than doubling, faster than tripling, faster than any fixed ratio at all, because the factor you multiply by keeps growing. A deck of 52 cards can be ordered in \(52!\) ways, a number with 68 digits — more orderings than there are atoms in the Earth. Shuffle a deck honestly and it is nearly certain that its exact order has never occurred before in human history.

Example 5

Evaluate or simplify each expression.   ① \(6!\)   ② \(\dfrac{8!}{6!}\)   ③ \(\dfrac{(n+1)!}{(n-1)!}\)

Solution:

① Multiply down from 6:

\(6! = 6 \cdot 5 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 720\)

② Resist the urge to compute both factorials in full. Instead, unwrap \(8!\) just far enough to reveal a copy of \(6!\), then cancel:

\(\dfrac{8!}{6!} = \dfrac{8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6!}{6!} = 8 \cdot 7 = 56\)

③ The same unwrapping works with symbols. Since \((n+1)! = (n+1) \cdot n \cdot (n-1)!\),

\(\dfrac{(n+1)!}{(n-1)!} = \dfrac{(n+1)\, n \,(n-1)!}{(n-1)!} = n(n+1)\)

Answer: ① \(720\)   ② \(56\)   ③ \(n(n+1)\)

The cancellation move in ② and ③ is the one you will use constantly: a larger factorial always contains every smaller factorial as a factor. That single fact turns monstrous quotients into small products.

Example 6

Write the first five terms of the sequence defined by

\(\Large a_n = \dfrac{n!}{2^n}\)

Solution:

Substitute and simplify each fraction fully:

\(a_1 = \dfrac{1!}{2^1} = \dfrac{1}{2}\)

\(a_2 = \dfrac{2!}{2^2} = \dfrac{2}{4} = \dfrac{1}{2}\)

\(a_3 = \dfrac{3!}{2^3} = \dfrac{6}{8} = \dfrac{3}{4}\)

\(a_4 = \dfrac{4!}{2^4} = \dfrac{24}{16} = \dfrac{3}{2}\)

\(a_5 = \dfrac{5!}{2^5} = \dfrac{120}{32} = \dfrac{15}{4}\)

Answer: The first five terms are \(\dfrac{1}{2}, \; \dfrac{1}{2}, \; \dfrac{3}{4}, \; \dfrac{3}{2}, \; \dfrac{15}{4}\).

Watch the story in those terms: the sequence hesitates at first — the doubling in the denominator holds its own for two terms — and then the factorial pulls away for good. By \(a_5\) the factorial is winning decisively, exactly as the Did You Know? box predicts it must.

▶  Try it yourself: Write the first four terms of \(a_1 = 10, \; a_n = a_{n-1} - 6\). Work it on paper before you peek.
\(a_1 = 10\),   \(a_2 = 10 - 6 = 4\),   \(a_3 = 4 - 6 = -2\),   \(a_4 = -2 - 6 = -8\). The first four terms are \(10, 4, -2, -8\). (Could you write this one explicitly? The rule acts \(n-1\) times: \(a_n = 10 - 6(n-1) = 16 - 6n\). Check: \(a_3 = 16 - 18 = -2\) ✓.)
▶  Try it yourself: Simplify \(\dfrac{9!}{7! \; 2!}\)
Unwrap \(9!\) far enough to expose \(7!\):   \(\dfrac{9!}{7! \; 2!} = \dfrac{9 \cdot 8 \cdot 7!}{7! \cdot 2} = \dfrac{72}{2} = 36\).
⚠️ WHERE AI HELPS — AND WHERE IT DOESN'T
Here is a good supervised-tutor exercise: ask an AI to explain why \(0! = 1\), and then grade its answer against this lesson. A strong explanation appeals to the recursive definition (the rule \(1! = 1 \cdot 0!\) forces it) or to counting (one way to arrange nothing). A weak one just says “mathematicians define it that way for convenience” — true, but it dodges the why. An AI can produce either answer depending on how it is asked. Judging which explanation actually explains — that is the mathematician’s job, and this week it is yours.

Conclusion

A recursive formula defines a sequence the way a climb defines a summit: seeds first, then one rule applied again and again, each term standing on the terms before it. You learned to read both one-seed and two-seed recursions, to translate between recursive and explicit definitions — checking that both languages tell the same story — and to wield factorial notation, whose great cancellation trick will serve you from now through calculus. Next lesson, the patient climbing pays off: sequences with a constant step, the arithmetic family, where the two languages meet in their simplest and most useful form.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. What must a recursive formula always state, and why does the rule alone fail to determine a sequence?

2. When is a recursive description more natural than an explicit one — and what does the recursive form cost you?

3. Why is \(0! = 1\) forced by the definition of factorial rather than chosen for convenience?

4. How can you verify that a recursive definition and an explicit formula describe the same sequence?
