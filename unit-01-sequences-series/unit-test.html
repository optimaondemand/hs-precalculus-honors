📋 BEFORE YOU BEGIN

This test gathers the whole three-week arc into one sitting: sequences and their two languages, the arithmetic and geometric families, real-world models, and the sums — finite and infinite — that the families make possible. It has three parts: Part A (fluency), Part B (reasoning — write these as arguments), and Part C (one synthesis problem drawing the unit together).

Ground rules. Set aside a quiet block of about 90 minutes and complete the test in one honest sitting. Work alone: no classmates, no AI tools, no solution websites — this is the one place where the mathematics must be entirely yours. A calculator may be used for arithmetic only (evaluating powers and quotients); every setup, formula, and step of reasoning must appear on your page. Lesson pages and notes stay closed — the formulas this test needs are the ones you have derived yourself twice over.

Write the honor line at the top of your first page and sign it: “I completed this test on my own, without help from people, notes, or AI tools.” Intellectual honesty is a quarter of the rubric — and the whole point of the virtue.

📝 PART A — FLUENCY (Lessons 1-1 through 1-9)

Neat vertical work for every problem — one step per line, equals signs aligned, exact values, labeled answers.

1
Write the first four terms of the sequence \(a_n = n^2 - 3n\), and state, with a reason, whether the sequence is arithmetic, geometric, or neither.
2
Write the first five terms of the sequence defined recursively by \(a_1 = 3, \;\; a_n = 2a_{n-1} - 1\).
3
Evaluate \(\dfrac{9!}{6! \; 3!}\) without a calculator, showing the factorial cancellation.
4
An arithmetic sequence has \(a_6 = 25\) and \(a_{14} = 57\). Find \(d\) and \(a_1\), write a simplified explicit formula, and use it to find \(a_{40}\). Verify your formula against both given terms.
5
Evaluate \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{30} (5k - 2)\).
6
Find the 9th term of the geometric sequence \(2, \; -6, \; 18, \; -54, \; \ldots\) (Predict its sign before computing, and confirm your prediction.)
7
Evaluate \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{6} 4 \cdot 3^{\,k-1}\).
8
For each series, decide whether it converges; find the exact sum of each one that does:   ① \(50 + 30 + 18 + \cdots\)   ② \(4 + 6 + 9 + 13.5 + \cdots\)   ③ \(0.\overline{5}\) written as a series (give the fraction).
📝 PART B — REASONING (write these as arguments)

These problems are graded primarily on the quality of your reasoning. Full sentences where explanation is asked; every claim earned, not asserted.

9
Derive one of the unit’s two sum formulas, your choice — the arithmetic \(S_n = \frac{n}{2}(a_1 + a_n)\) by Gauss’s pairing, or the geometric \(S_n = \frac{a_1(1-r^n)}{1-r}\) by multiply-and-subtract. Present it as you would to a classmate who has never seen it: every line written, the key cancellation explained in a sentence, and (for the geometric) the restriction on \(r\) tied to the exact step that requires it.
10
An engineer’s contract starts at $48,000 with a $1,500 raise each year. Define your variables in words, then find: ① her salary in year 8; ② her total earnings across the eight years. Label which answer is a term and which is a series, and explain in one sentence how you can tell, from the wording of a question alone, which of the two is being asked for.
11
A student claims: “The series \(7 + 7(0.9) + 7(0.9)^2 + \cdots\) can’t have a finite sum — the terms shrink so slowly that adding forever must blow past any bound.” Settle the claim: state and check the convergence condition, compute the exact sum, and explain in two or three sentences what the student’s intuition missed — what has to be true of the terms’ rate of shrinking, and what the partial sums actually do.
📝 PART C — SYNTHESIS (the unit in one problem)
12
Two sequences start at the same value: the arithmetic \(a_n = 5 + 3(n-1)\) and the geometric \(b_n = 5 \cdot (1.5)^{\,n-1}\).
① Compute \(a_{10}\) and \(b_{10}\), and the sums \(S_{10}\) of each sequence’s first ten terms (correct formula for each family).
② Describe the graphs of the two sequences — what shape each set of plotted points follows, and what feature of each formula produces that shape.
③ In a short closing paragraph (3–5 sentences), explain why the geometric sequence must eventually overtake the arithmetic one no matter how the numbers are changed (any \(d\), any \(r > 1\)) — and name the one further question about geometric series that this unit answered which has no arithmetic counterpart at all.
📊 GRADING RUBRIC

The whole test is graded against the standing four-criterion rubric, each criterion scored out of 10. Part A supplies most of the evidence for understanding and precision; Parts B and C supply most of the evidence for reasoning.

Criteria What excellent looks like Pts
Understanding of the material The right tool reaches every problem: families identified correctly, formulas matched to their families, licenses (like \(|r| < 1\)) checked before use, notation exact. 10 pts
Quality of reasoning The derivation in Part B reads as a real argument; explanations say why; verifications are actually performed; the synthesis paragraph reasons about all cases, not one example. 10 pts
Presentation & precision Clean vertical work throughout, aligned equals signs, exact values, labeled answers with units, pages in order — forty points of work a stranger could follow. 10 pts
Intellectual honesty Honor line signed and honored; work shown as actually done; any problem you could not finish carries an honest note of where and why you stopped — which earns respect here, never punishment. 10 pts
Total: 40 pts
