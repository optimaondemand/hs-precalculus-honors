Modeling with Arithmetic Sequences
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to model real-world quantities that change by a fixed amount — salaries, depreciating equipment, seating — with an arithmetic sequence, choosing the indexing that fits the situation.

  • I will be able to solve for an unknown term, an unknown position, or an unknown difference inside a real context, and interpret the answer in that context’s own units.

  • I will be able to verify a proposed sequence model — mine, a spreadsheet’s, or an AI’s — against known data before trusting its predictions.

📚 VOCABULARY

Mathematical model: A precise mathematical description — here, a sequence — standing in for a real situation, so that computing with the model answers questions about the situation.

Initial value: The starting quantity of a model. Depending on the situation it is most naturally labeled \(a_1\) (the first payment, the first row) or \(a_0\) (the value at time zero, before anything has happened).

Straight-line depreciation: Loss of value by a fixed dollar amount per year — an arithmetic sequence with negative common difference.

Domain of a model: The set of positions for which the model honestly describes reality. Every model has one; part of modeling is naming it.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Yesterday you mastered the machinery of arithmetic sequences. Today we point that machinery at the world. The arithmetic family shows up wherever something changes by the same fixed amount at regular intervals: a salary that grows by the same raise each year, a truck that loses the same value each year, a theater whose rows each gain the same number of seats, a savings balance fed by the same deposit each month. Whenever you hear “the same amount, every time,” you should hear the click of the arithmetic family locking into place — and reach for \(a_n = a_1 + (n-1)d\).

But modeling asks one question that pure pattern-work never did: what do the symbols mean? Before you compute anything, you must decide what \(n\) counts, what \(a_n\) measures, and where the counting starts. Most modeling errors are not algebra errors — they are meaning errors, committed in the first line and carried faithfully to the last. So we begin every model the same disciplined way: define your variables in words before you touch them with algebra.

Building an Arithmetic Model

Four steps, every time:

  • Name the quantities. Say in words what \(n\) counts and what \(a_n\) measures, with units. Decide whether the natural first index is \(n = 1\) (the first row, the first paycheck) or \(n = 0\) (the value before any time has passed).
  • Identify \(d\) and the initial value. The fixed change per step is \(d\); the starting quantity is \(a_1\) (or \(a_0\)).
  • Write the formula. Starting at \(a_1\): \(a_n = a_1 + (n-1)d\). Starting at \(a_0\): \(a_n = a_0 + n\,d\) — because by time \(n\), the change has happened \(n\) times.
  • Answer the question in context. A model’s final answer is a sentence with units, not a bare number.

Notice that the two formulas differ exactly where the counting starts. Neither is “the real one” — each is correct for its own choice of starting index. The discipline is to choose once, say so, and stay consistent.

Example 1

A structural engineer takes a position with a starting salary of $47,500 and a guaranteed raise of $2,200 at the end of each year. Write a model for her salary, and find her salary in her 12th year.

Solution:

Define the variables: let \(n\) count the year of employment (so \(n = 1\) is her first year), and let \(a_n\) be her salary that year, in dollars. Her first-year salary is the initial value and each year adds the same raise:

\(a_1 = 47{,}500, \quad d = 2{,}200\)

\(a_n = 47{,}500 + 2{,}200\,(n - 1)\)

Her 12th year is one substitution away — by year 12 she has received \(12 - 1 = 11\) raises:

\(a_{12} = 47{,}500 + 2{,}200 \cdot 11\)

\(a_{12} = 47{,}500 + 24{,}200 = 71{,}700\)

Answer: \(a_n = 47{,}500 + 2{,}200(n-1)\); in her 12th year her salary is $71,700.

Pause on the 11. She is in her 12th year but has been raised only 11 times — the fence-post count from Lesson 1-3, now with a paycheck attached. Getting that count right is exactly the difference between a correct model and a plausible-looking wrong one.

Example 2

A landscaping company buys a truck for $38,000. For tax purposes it depreciates the truck by $3,500 per year (straight-line). Write a model for the truck’s value \(n\) years after purchase, and determine in which year the value first falls below $10,000.

Solution:

Define the variables: here the natural starting point is the moment of purchase, before any time passes — so let \(v_n\) be the truck’s value in dollars \(n\) years after purchase, with \(v_0 = 38{,}000\). Each year subtracts the same amount, so \(d = -3{,}500\), and since the change has happened \(n\) times by year \(n\):

\(v_n = 38{,}000 - 3{,}500\,n\)

We want the smallest whole number \(n\) with \(v_n < 10{,}000\):

\(38{,}000 - 3{,}500\,n < 10{,}000\)

\(-3{,}500\,n < -28{,}000\)

\(n > 8\)

(Dividing by the negative number \(-3{,}500\) flips the inequality.) The smallest whole number greater than 8 is \(n = 9\). And the boundary case is worth a look: \(v_8 = 38{,}000 - 28{,}000 = 10{,}000\) exactly — at eight years the truck is worth $10,000 on the nose, which is not below it. Check year nine: \(v_9 = 38{,}000 - 31{,}500 = 6{,}500\) ✓.

Answer: \(v_n = 38{,}000 - 3{,}500n\); the value first falls below $10,000 in year 9.

Two honors habits on display: we chose the \(a_0\) convention because “value at time zero” is the natural anchor, and we tested the boundary instead of assuming. Strict inequalities repay that care.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
The most common modeling error in this lesson is an indexing mismatch: writing \(a_n = a_1 + (n-1)d\) but treating \(n\) as “years after the start,” or writing \(a_n = a_0 + nd\) but calling the starting value \(a_1\). Both formulas are correct; mixing them is not. Anchor yourself with one test: plug in the starting index and confirm you get the starting value. If \(n = 1\) is your first year, \(a_1\) must return the starting salary, untouched.

Example 3

A concert hall’s center section has 22 seats in row 1, and each row behind it has 2 more seats than the row in front. The section’s last row has 70 seats. How many rows does the section have?

Solution:

Define the variables: let \(a_n\) be the number of seats in row \(n\), so \(a_1 = 22\) and \(d = 2\):

\(a_n = 22 + 2(n-1) = 2n + 20\)

This time the unknown is not the term but the position. Set the formula equal to 70 and solve for \(n\):

\(2n + 20 = 70\)

\(2n = 50\)

\(n = 25\)

Answer: The section has 25 rows.

One family of formulas, three kinds of questions so far: unknown term (Example 1), unknown position under an inequality (Example 2), unknown position under an equation (Example 3). The model does not change — only which letter you solve for. (How many seats does the whole 25-row section hold? That is a genuinely different kind of question — a sum — and it is exactly where Lesson 1-5 begins.)

📊 Try It in Excel: A Model You Can Interrogate 15 min

A spreadsheet turns a model into something you can question. Build Example 2’s truck:

  1. In A1 type Year, in B1 type Value. In A2 type 0, in A3 type =A2+1, and fill column A down to year 12.
  2. In B2 type the initial value: 38000. In B3 type the recursive rule: =B2-3500, and fill down. There is the depreciation, year by year — the same patient climb (downhill, this time) from Lesson 1-2.
  3. In C2 type the explicit model: =38000-3500*A2 and fill down. Columns B and C should agree in every row — the recursive and explicit languages, telling one story. If they disagree anywhere, one of your formulas has an indexing slip: find it.
  4. Select columns A and B and insert a scatter chart. The points fall on a straight line sloping down — the linear function behind the arithmetic family, drawn by your own data.

Now interrogate the model: in what year does the value hit zero? Read it off the sheet, then confirm by algebra (\(38{,}000 - 3{,}500n = 0\) gives \(n \approx 10.9\), so during year 11). Should the company keep depreciating the truck after that — can it be worth negative dollars? Every model has a domain where it tells the truth; this one ends when the value reaches zero. Naming that boundary is part of the model.

⚠️ AI CHECKPOINT: GRADE THE MACHINE

A student asked an AI to model Example 1’s salary, and it answered, fluently and confidently:

“Since the salary starts at $47,500 and increases by $2,200 per year, the salary in year \(n\) is given by \(a_n = 47{,}500 + 2{,}200n\). For example, in year 12 the salary is \(a_{12} = 47{,}500 + 26{,}400 = \$73{,}900\).”

Every sentence sounds right. Test it the way you test any formula: at the anchor. Set \(n = 1\): the AI’s formula gives \(47{,}500 + 2{,}200 = \$49{,}700\) for her first-year salary — but her first-year salary is $47,500. The formula pays her a raise she has not yet earned; every prediction it makes is one full step too high. The AI made the exact off-by-one error flagged in this lesson’s Common Mistake box, and it made it in perfect prose.

This is the standing lesson of AI-assisted mathematics: fluency is not correctness. An AI’s answer is a proposal, and the anchor test — thirty seconds of substitution — is how a mathematician audits a proposal. In this week’s assignment, you will run this audit yourself on another AI-produced model.

▶  Try it yourself: A gym charges a $79 enrollment fee plus $42 per month. Write a model for the total amount paid after \(n\) months of membership, and find the total after 18 months. Work it on paper before you peek.
Let \(t_n\) be the total paid after \(n\) months. At \(n = 0\) (enrollment day) the total is \(t_0 = 79\), and each month adds 42: \(t_n = 79 + 42n\). After 18 months: \(t_{18} = 79 + 756 = \$835\). (Anchor check: \(t_0 = 79\) ✓ — the fee alone, before any monthly charges. This is an \(a_0\)-style model; the change has happened \(n\) times after \(n\) months.)

Conclusion

Modeling with arithmetic sequences is a discipline of meaning: name what \(n\) counts and what \(a_n\) measures, anchor the starting index, identify the fixed change, and only then compute. You solved for unknown terms, unknown positions, and boundary cases; you built a model in Excel and interrogated it about its own domain of honesty; and you audited an AI’s confident, fluent, wrong formula with a thirty-second anchor test. Tomorrow the unit’s central question changes shape: not what is the \(n\)th term? but what do all the terms add up to? A nine-year-old in Brunswick once answered that question faster than his schoolmaster believed possible — and his trick is the heart of Lesson 1-5.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. What signal in a real situation tells you an arithmetic model fits — and what signal would tell you it doesn’t?

2. When is \(a_0\) the natural starting index, when is \(a_1\), and what single test confirms your formula matches your choice?

3. A model answered a question with \(n = 8\) exactly at the boundary. Why does “below $10,000” demand more care than “at most $10,000”?

4. What does it mean for a model to have a domain of honesty, and who is responsible for naming it?
