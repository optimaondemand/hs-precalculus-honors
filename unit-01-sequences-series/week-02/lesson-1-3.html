🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to determine whether a sequence is arithmetic and, when it is, identify its common difference.

  • I will be able to write both the explicit and the recursive form of an arithmetic sequence, and translate between them.

  • I will be able to find any specified term of an arithmetic sequence — including when I am given two scattered terms instead of the first one.

📚 VOCABULARY

Arithmetic sequence: A sequence in which each term is obtained from the one before it by adding the same fixed number.

Common difference: That fixed number, written \(d\). It is found by subtracting any term from the term that follows it: \(d = a_{n} - a_{n-1}\).

Explicit form (arithmetic): \(a_n = a_1 + (n-1)d\) — any term computed directly from its position.

Recursive form (arithmetic): \(a_1\) given, \(a_n = a_{n-1} + d\) — each term built from its predecessor.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Last week you met sequences in the wild — alternating ones, factorial ones, sequences that stand on their own shoulders. This week we study the two great families of sequences, the ones that appear so often in nature, finance, and physics that they have earned names of their own. The first family is the simplest possible kind of growth: add the same amount, every time.

Humanity has been solving problems about this kind of sequence for a very long time. The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus, an Egyptian scroll copied by the scribe Ahmes around 1550 BC, asks its reader to divide ten measures of barley among ten men so that each man receives a fixed amount more than the man before him. Equal steps, evenly climbing — a payroll problem, thirty-five centuries old. The scribes had no algebra as we know it, yet they solved it. By the end of this lesson you will be able to solve it too, and with far better tools.

A sequence that climbs (or descends) by equal steps is called arithmetic. Consider:

\(5, \; 12, \; 19, \; 26, \; 33, \; \ldots\)

Each term is 7 more than the last. That single number — the size of the step — is the sequence’s signature. We call it the common difference and write it \(d\).

Arithmetic Sequences and the Common Difference

A sequence is arithmetic if the difference between consecutive terms is constant:

\(\Large d = a_n - a_{n-1} \; \text{ for every } n\)

  • If \(d > 0\), the sequence increases by \(d\) at every step.
  • If \(d < 0\), the sequence decreases — the “step” is a step down.
  • To test whether a sequence is arithmetic, subtract every pair of consecutive terms, not just the first pair. One constant difference is a coincidence; all of them constant is a family membership.

In recursive language, an arithmetic sequence is the simplest recursion there is:

\(\Large a_1 \text{ given}, \quad a_n = a_{n-1} + d\)

Example 1

Determine whether each sequence is arithmetic. If it is, state the common difference.

① \(4, \; 11, \; 18, \; 25, \; 32, \; \ldots\)   ② \(3, \; 6, \; 12, \; 24, \; \ldots\)   ③ \(40, \; 33, \; 26, \; 19, \; \ldots\)

Solution:

① Subtract consecutive terms, every pair:

\(11 - 4 = 7, \quad 18 - 11 = 7, \quad 25 - 18 = 7, \quad 32 - 25 = 7\)

The difference is constant, so the sequence is arithmetic with \(d = 7\).

② Check the differences: \(6 - 3 = 3\), but \(12 - 6 = 6\). The differences are not constant, so this sequence is not arithmetic. (Look again, though: each term is double the last. A constant ratio instead of a constant difference — that is the second great family, waiting for you in Lesson 1-6.)

③ \(33 - 40 = -7\), \(26 - 33 = -7\), \(19 - 26 = -7\). Constant difference again — arithmetic with \(d = -7\). A negative common difference simply means the equal steps go downhill.

Answer: ① arithmetic, \(d = 7\)   ② not arithmetic   ③ arithmetic, \(d = -7\).

Example 2

Write the first five terms of the arithmetic sequence with \(a_1 = 7\) and \(d = -4\).

Solution:

Start at the seed and add \(-4\) at every step:

\(a_1 = 7\)

\(a_2 = 7 + (-4) = 3\)

\(a_3 = 3 + (-4) = -1\)

\(a_4 = -1 + (-4) = -5\)

\(a_5 = -5 + (-4) = -9\)

Answer: The first five terms are \(7, \; 3, \; -1, \; -5, \; -9\).

Now for the payoff you earned in Lesson 1-2. Building term by term is honest work, but you learned there that a rule applied over and over can be compressed: by the time you reach term \(n\), the rule “add \(d\)” has acted exactly \(n - 1\) times on the seed. That one observation hands us the explicit formula for the entire family.

Key Fact: The Explicit Form of an Arithmetic Sequence

\(\Large a_n = a_1 + (n-1)\,d\)

Read it as a story: start at \(a_1\), then take \(n-1\) steps of size \(d\). The \(n - 1\) is not a decoration to memorize — it counts the steps between term 1 and term \(n\), and there are always one fewer steps than terms. (Five fence posts, four gaps between them.)

Distribute the \(d\) and regroup: \(a_n = dn + (a_1 - d)\). This is a linear function of \(n\) — slope \(d\), evaluated only at the counting numbers. An arithmetic sequence is a line, sampled one integer at a time. When you plot its terms, they march along a straight path; the common difference is the slope.

Example 3

Find the 50th term of the arithmetic sequence \(2, \; 9, \; 16, \; 23, \; \ldots\)

Solution:

First identify the ingredients: \(a_1 = 2\), and \(d = 9 - 2 = 7\) (confirm: \(16 - 9 = 7\) ✓). Now the explicit formula does in one line what fifty patient additions would do slowly:

\(a_{50} = a_1 + (50 - 1)\,d\)

\(a_{50} = 2 + 49 \cdot 7\)

\(a_{50} = 2 + 343 = 345\)

Answer: \(a_{50} = 345\).

Forty-nine steps of size 7, taken from a starting point of 2. The formula is the fifty-line climb, folded into a single expression — perseverance, banked.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Writing \(a_n = a_1 + n\,d\) — multiplying by \(n\) instead of \(n - 1\) — is the single most common arithmetic-sequence error, and it silently shifts every answer by one full step. Catch it the same way you catch every formula error: test at \(n = 1\). The correct formula gives \(a_1 = a_1 + 0 \cdot d = a_1\) ✓. The wrong one gives \(a_1 = a_1 + d\) — already off before you've begun.

Example 4

An arithmetic sequence has \(a_3 = 14\) and \(a_8 = 29\). Find an explicit formula for \(a_n\).

Solution:

We are not given \(a_1\) or \(d\) — but the two given terms hold both, if we reason about steps. Getting from term 3 to term 8 takes \(8 - 3 = 5\) steps, and the total climb over those steps is \(29 - 14 = 15\). Five equal steps covering a rise of 15 means each step is:

\(d = \dfrac{29 - 14}{8 - 3} = \dfrac{15}{5} = 3\)

Now walk backward from \(a_3\) to find the first term: \(a_3 = a_1 + 2d\), so

\(14 = a_1 + 2(3)\)

\(a_1 = 14 - 6 = 8\)

Assemble the explicit formula and simplify:

\(a_n = 8 + (n-1) \cdot 3 = 3n + 5\)

Verify against both given terms — a formula must answer to all its evidence: \(a_3 = 3(3) + 5 = 14\) ✓ and \(a_8 = 3(8) + 5 = 29\) ✓.

Answer: \(a_n = 3n + 5\).

Notice what the calculation \(d = \frac{29-14}{8-3}\) really is: rise over run. Finding the common difference from two scattered terms is exactly finding the slope of a line from two points — the same mathematics wearing sequence clothing.

  2. Line 2: type n1 = [1, ..., 10] (Desmos expands this to the list 1 through 10).
  3. Line 3: type (n1, f(n1)) to plot the ten terms as points.

Watch what the sign of the slope does to the march of the points. Every arithmetic sequence lives on a line; \(d\) is its slope, and the “zeroth term” \(a_1 - d\) is its intercept.

Example 5

Write a recursive formula for the arithmetic sequence of Example 4, and explain when you would prefer it to the explicit form.

Solution:

The recursive form needs only the seed and the step:

\(\Large a_1 = 8, \quad a_n = a_{n-1} + 3\)

Answer: \(a_1 = 8, \; a_n = a_{n-1} + 3\). The recursive form is the natural one when a situation unfolds step by step — a balance growing by a fixed deposit each month, a row of seats gaining two seats per row. The explicit form \(a_n = 3n + 5\) is the one you want for random access: term 500 in one substitution. Fluent mathematicians carry both and translate freely — that is precisely the two-languages skill from Lesson 1-2, now specialized to the arithmetic family.

▶  Try it yourself: Find the 31st term of the arithmetic sequence \(100, \; 94, \; 88, \; 82, \; \ldots\) Work it on paper before you peek.
Here \(a_1 = 100\) and \(d = 94 - 100 = -6\). Then \(a_{31} = 100 + (31-1)(-6) = 100 - 180 = -80\). Thirty steps down of size 6 — the sequence crosses zero along the way (between which two terms? Solve \(100 - 6(n-1) = 0\) to find out: between \(a_{17} = 4\) and \(a_{18} = -2\)).
▶  Try it yourself: An arithmetic sequence has \(a_5 = 22\) and \(a_{12} = 57\). Find \(d\), \(a_1\), and an explicit formula.
From term 5 to term 12 is \(12 - 5 = 7\) steps climbing \(57 - 22 = 35\), so \(d = \frac{35}{7} = 5\). Then \(a_5 = a_1 + 4d\) gives \(22 = a_1 + 20\), so \(a_1 = 2\), and \(a_n = 2 + (n-1)\cdot 5 = 5n - 3\). Check both: \(a_5 = 25 - 3 = 22\) ✓, \(a_{12} = 60 - 3 = 57\) ✓.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The word arithmetic in “arithmetic sequence” comes from the Greek arithmos, “number,” and the family earned the name because each interior term is the arithmetic mean — the ordinary average — of its two neighbors. Test it on \(2, 9, 16\): the average of 2 and 16 is \(\frac{2+16}{2} = 9\), the term sitting between them. Equal steps and balanced averages are the same fact seen from two directions.

Conclusion

An arithmetic sequence is growth by equal steps: one number \(d\) governs the whole march. You learned to detect the family by checking differences, to speak both of its languages — the recursive \(a_n = a_{n-1} + d\) and the explicit \(a_n = a_1 + (n-1)d\) — and to recover the entire sequence from just two scattered terms, the way a surveyor recovers a line from two stakes. And beneath it all sits one unifying picture: an arithmetic sequence is a linear function sampled at the counting numbers, its common difference the slope. In the next lesson we put this family to work on real quantities — salaries, depreciating trucks, theater seating — where the question is not “what is the pattern?” but “what does the pattern cost?”

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. What single piece of information, together with a starting term, determines an entire arithmetic sequence — and why?

2. Why does the explicit formula multiply \(d\) by \(n - 1\) rather than \(n\), and how can you always catch the error if you forget?

3. In what precise sense is an arithmetic sequence a linear function, and what plays the role of the slope?

4. Given two scattered terms of an arithmetic sequence, how do you recover the whole sequence — and what familiar calculation is hiding in your method?
