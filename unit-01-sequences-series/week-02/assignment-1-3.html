📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
This set drills the whole arithmetic toolkit from Lesson 1-3: detecting the family, writing terms, building explicit and recursive formulas, and recovering a sequence from two scattered terms. Work every problem by hand, showing your reasoning — not just answers. This is a practice set: it is scored for honest completion, and worked solutions will be released after the due date so you can correct your own work line by line, finding exactly where any slip happened.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
If one number — the common difference — governs an arithmetic sequence's entire march, how little information do you actually need to know everything about it?
📝 PROBLEM SET

Show your work for every problem in neat vertical form — one step per line, equals signs aligned, final answers labeled. Fractions stay exact; do not convert to decimals.

1
Determine whether each sequence is arithmetic; if it is, state \(d\):   ① \(6, \; 13, \; 20, \; 27, \; \ldots\)   ② \(1, \; 4, \; 9, \; 16, \; \ldots\)   ③ \(\dfrac{5}{2}, \; 2, \; \dfrac{3}{2}, \; 1, \; \ldots\)
2
Write the first five terms of the arithmetic sequence with \(a_1 = -3\) and \(d = 6\).
3
Find the 40th term of the arithmetic sequence \(11, \; 18, \; 25, \; 32, \; \ldots\)
4
For the sequence \(a_n = 82 - 9n\): write its first three terms, state its common difference, and give its recursive form (seed and rule).
5
An arithmetic sequence has \(a_4 = 19\) and \(a_{10} = 43\). Find \(d\), find \(a_1\), and write a simplified explicit formula. Verify your formula against both given terms.
6
Is \(-215\) a term of the arithmetic sequence \(40, \; 33, \; 26, \; 19, \; \ldots\)? Answer yes or no, and justify with algebra: set up the equation a term number must satisfy and interpret what its solution tells you.
7
Explain why: The explicit form of an arithmetic sequence can be written \(a_n = dn + (a_1 - d)\). Explain, in two or three sentences, why plotting the terms of any arithmetic sequence always produces points that lie on a single straight line, and say exactly what feature of the sequence the line's slope represents.
8
Honors stretch: The interior terms of an arithmetic sequence are each the average of their two neighbors. Prove this in general: show algebraically that for any arithmetic sequence, \(a_n = \dfrac{a_{n-1} + a_{n+1}}{2}\) for every interior term. Then explain why the converse is also believable: if every interior term of a sequence is the average of its neighbors, why must the steps all be equal?
💡 CHECK YOURSELF WITH DESMOS
After solving on paper, verify problems 4 and 5 in Desmos (desmos.com/calculator) using the point-plotting method from the lesson: define your formula as \(f(n)\), plot \((n_1, f(n_1))\) for \(n_1 = [1, ..., 10]\), and confirm the points pass through the given terms. The calculator confirms; your algebra convinces.
