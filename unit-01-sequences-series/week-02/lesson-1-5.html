🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to read and write summation (sigma) notation: identify the index, the limits, and the summand, and expand or evaluate the sum.

  • I will be able to derive the finite arithmetic series formula using Gauss’s pairing method — and explain why the derivation works, not just recite the result.

  • I will be able to apply the formula \(S_n = \frac{n}{2}(a_1 + a_n)\) to sums written in any form — lists, sigma notation, or real-world accumulation problems.

📚 VOCABULARY

Series: The sum of the terms of a sequence. The sequence is the list; the series is the total.

Partial sum: The sum of the first \(n\) terms of a sequence, written \(S_n\).

Summation (sigma) notation: Compact notation for a sum, built on the capital Greek letter \(\Sigma\) (sigma, the Greek “S,” for sum).

Index of summation: The counting variable (often \(k\)) that steps through the terms being added.

Lower and upper limits: The starting and ending values of the index — where the count begins and where it stops.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Around 1786, in a schoolroom in Brunswick, a teacher named Büttner set his class a long, quiet task: add every whole number from 1 to 100. Within moments, a nine-year-old named Carl Friedrich Gauss laid his slate on the desk: 5050. While his classmates began the hundred-step climb, Gauss had seen something structural. Pair the first number with the last: \(1 + 100 = 101\). Pair the second with the second-to-last: \(2 + 99 = 101\). Every pair — and there are exactly 50 of them — sums to the same 101:

\(50 \times 101 = 5050\)

Gauss went on to become perhaps the greatest mathematician who ever lived, and this schoolroom story survives because it captures his lifelong signature: where others compute, look for structure. Yesterday you asked “what is the \(n\)th term?” Today the question is “what do the terms add up to?” — how many seats in the whole theater, how much saved after two years, how much barley in all ten shares. A sum of sequence terms is called a series, and before we can compute series efficiently, we need a notation compact enough to hold them.

Summation Notation: The Anatomy of \(\Sigma\)

Mathematicians compress “add these terms” into one symbol:

\(\Large \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k = a_1 + a_2 + a_3 + \cdots + a_n\)

  • \(\Sigma\) — the capital Greek sigma, the Greek letter S, standing for sum.
  • \(k = 1\) (below) — the index of summation and its lower limit: start counting at \(k = 1\).
  • \(n\) (above) — the upper limit: stop after \(k = n\).
  • \(a_k\) (beside) — the summand: the expression to evaluate at each value of \(k\), each result joining the sum.

Read \(\sum_{k=1}^{5}(2k+1)\) aloud as: “the sum, as \(k\) runs from 1 to 5, of \(2k + 1\).” The index is a private counter — the letter \(k\) (or \(i\), or \(j\)) never appears in the final answer. It does the counting and retires.

Example 1

Expand and evaluate \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{5} (2k + 1)\).

Solution:

Let \(k\) take each value from 1 to 5, evaluate the summand at each, and add:

\(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{5} (2k+1) = \underbrace{3}_{k=1} + \underbrace{5}_{k=2} + \underbrace{7}_{k=3} + \underbrace{9}_{k=4} + \underbrace{11}_{k=5}\)

\(= 35\)

Answer: \(35\).

Notice the terms \(3, 5, 7, 9, 11\): an arithmetic sequence with \(d = 2\). A linear summand always produces an arithmetic sequence of terms — keep that connection in view; it is about to matter.

Example 2

Write the sum \(6 + 10 + 14 + 18 + \cdots + 82\) in summation notation.

Solution:

First find the general term of the sequence being added. It is arithmetic with \(a_1 = 6\) and \(d = 4\), so by Lesson 1-3:

\(a_k = 6 + 4(k-1) = 4k + 2\)

Next find where the count stops: solve \(4k + 2 = 82\), giving \(4k = 80\), so \(k = 20\). The sum runs from \(k = 1\) to \(k = 20\):

\(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{20} (4k + 2)\)

Check the ends: at \(k = 1\) the summand gives \(6\) ✓; at \(k = 20\) it gives \(82\) ✓.

Answer: \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{20} (4k+2)\). (Sigma expressions are not unique — \(\sum_{k=0}^{19}(4k+6)\) names the same sum. What must never change is the list of numbers actually added.)

Now the centerpiece. Twenty terms is too many to add by brute force with any joy — but Gauss showed us we do not have to. His pairing trick is not a curiosity; it is a proof, and it works on every arithmetic series at once. Watch it in slow motion.

Deriving the Formula: Gauss, Generalized

Let \(S_n\) be the sum of the first \(n\) terms of any arithmetic sequence. Write the sum twice — once forward, once backward — and add the two rows column by column:

\(S_n = a_1 + (a_1 + d) + (a_1 + 2d) + \cdots + a_n\)

\(S_n = a_n + (a_n - d) + (a_n - 2d) + \cdots + a_1\)

In every column, the deviations cancel: \(+d\) meets \(-d\), \(+2d\) meets \(-2d\). Each of the \(n\) columns sums to exactly \(a_1 + a_n\):

\(2S_n = n\,(a_1 + a_n)\)

\(\Large S_n = \dfrac{n}{2}\,(a_1 + a_n)\)

Read it as Gauss did: (number of terms) times (average of the first and last), because in an arithmetic series the first-and-last average is the average of every balanced pair. For \(1 + 2 + \cdots + 100\): \(S_{100} = \frac{100}{2}(1 + 100) = 50 \cdot 101 = 5050\). The slate, vindicated.

When the last term isn’t handy, substitute \(a_n = a_1 + (n-1)d\) to get the second form: \(S_n = \frac{n}{2}\left[\,2a_1 + (n-1)d\,\right]\). Same formula, different wardrobe — use whichever matches the information you hold.

Example 3

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{20} (4k + 2)\) — the twenty-term sum from Example 2.

Solution:

The terms are arithmetic, so the formula applies. We know all three ingredients: \(n = 20\), \(a_1 = 6\), \(a_{20} = 82\):

\(S_{20} = \dfrac{20}{2}\,(6 + 82)\)

\(S_{20} = 10 \cdot 88 = 880\)

Answer: \(880\).

Twenty terms, one multiplication. Sanity-check it Gauss’s way: ten pairs (\(6+82\), \(10+78\), …), each summing to 88 — \(10 \times 88 = 880\) ✓.

Example 4

The concert hall from Lesson 1-4 has 25 rows: 22 seats in row 1, each row gaining 2 seats, ending at 70 seats in row 25. How many seats does the section hold in all?

Solution:

Yesterday’s question was about one row; today’s is about the total — a finite arithmetic series with \(n = 25\), \(a_1 = 22\), \(a_{25} = 70\):

\(S_{25} = \dfrac{25}{2}\,(22 + 70)\)

\(S_{25} = \dfrac{25}{2} \cdot 92 = 25 \cdot 46 = 1150\)

Answer: The section holds 1,150 seats.

Note the arithmetic hygiene: rather than wrestle \(\frac{25}{2} = 12.5\), we halved the even factor 92 first. Exact arithmetic prefers the cleanest path — and the formula always offers one, since either \(n\) or \(a_1 + a_n\) must be even. (Why must it? Think about pairing an odd number of terms: the middle term is its own partner, and it equals the average \(\frac{a_1+a_n}{2}\) exactly.)

Example 5

A student saves $250 in the first month and increases the deposit by $25 each month. How much has she saved in total after two full years?

Solution:

Define the variables: let \(a_n\) be the deposit in month \(n\), in dollars: \(a_1 = 250\), \(d = 25\), and two years means \(n = 24\) deposits. The question asks for the total, \(S_{24}\). First find the final deposit:

\(a_{24} = 250 + 25\,(24 - 1) = 250 + 575 = 825\)

Then the series formula:

\(S_{24} = \dfrac{24}{2}\,(250 + 825)\)

\(S_{24} = 12 \cdot 1075 = 12{,}900\)

Answer: After two years she has saved $12,900.

Twenty-four months of small, growing faithfulness, and the total is five figures. This is the unit’s virtue made arithmetic: perseverance is not one heroic act but a long series of ordinary ones — and the series formula is how you see, in advance, what the ordinary acts amount to.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
The series formula \(S_n = \frac{n}{2}(a_1 + a_n)\) works only for arithmetic sequences — its derivation depends on the balanced pairs, and the pairs balance only when the steps are equal. Before you reach for it, confirm the terms have a common difference. (Next week’s geometric series will demand a completely different trick.) Also: know the difference between the questions “find \(a_{24}\)” and “find \(S_{24}\)” — the last deposit and the total saved are answers to different questions, and mixing them up is the classic word-problem stumble.

📊 Try It in Excel: Watching a Series Accumulate 10 min

A series is a sequence of sums — and a spreadsheet shows you both at once. Build Example 5:

  1. Column A: months 1 through 24. Column B: the deposits — type 250 in B1 and =B1+25 in B2, fill down.
  2. Column C: the running total. In C1 type =B1. In C2 type =C1+B2 — “everything so far, plus this month” — and fill down. Column C is the sequence of partial sums \(S_1, S_2, \ldots, S_{24}\).
  3. Read C24: it should say 12900, agreeing with the formula. If it doesn’t, audit your seed cells.
  4. Chart columns B and C together (scatter). The deposits march in a straight line — but the running total curves upward. Adding a linearly growing quantity produces quadratic growth. You met this shape in Week 1’s triangular numbers; here it is again, wearing dollars.

The partial-sum column is a tool you will use again in Lesson 1-9, where the question becomes: what happens to \(S_n\) when the terms never stop coming?

⚠️ WHERE AI HELPS — AND WHERE IT DOESN'T
Sigma notation rewards drill, and an AI tutor is an excellent drill partner: ask it for “five sigma-notation sums with arithmetic summands — give me the notation only, and keep the totals hidden.” Evaluate each one twice — once by the formula, once by Gauss-style pairing — and only then ask the AI for its totals. If you and the AI disagree, do not assume the machine is right: expand the first three terms by hand and find out whose sum is telling the truth. Being the auditor, not the audited, is the skill.
▶  Try it yourself: Evaluate \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{40} (3k + 2)\). Work it on paper before you peek.
The terms are arithmetic. First term: \(3(1) + 2 = 5\). Last term: \(3(40) + 2 = 122\). Then \(S_{40} = \frac{40}{2}(5 + 122) = 20 \cdot 127 = 2540\).
▶  Try it yourself: Find the sum of the first 30 even numbers, \(2 + 4 + 6 + \cdots + 60\), two ways — by the formula, and by Gauss-style pairing.
Formula: \(S_{30} = \frac{30}{2}(2 + 60) = 15 \cdot 62 = 930\). Pairing: \(2 + 60 = 62\), \(4 + 58 = 62\), … — fifteen pairs of 62, so \(15 \times 62 = 930\) ✓. (Elegant bonus: the sum of the first \(n\) even numbers is always \(n(n+1)\) — here \(30 \cdot 31 = 930\). Can you see why, using the factored form?)
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The sums \(1, 3, 6, 10, 15, \ldots\) — the partial sums of \(1 + 2 + 3 + \cdots\) — are called triangular numbers, because each can be stacked as a triangle of dots. The Pythagoreans revered them twenty-five centuries ago; their tetractys, the ten-dot triangle of \(1+2+3+4\), was practically a sacred emblem. Gauss’s formula \(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\) computes them all — a nine-year-old’s slate trick answering a question the ancients asked with pebbles in the sand.

Conclusion

A series is a sequence asked a deeper question: not where are you at step \(n\), but what have all the steps amounted to? You learned to read and write sigma notation — index, limits, summand — and you did something more important than learn a formula: you derived one, by Gauss’s forward-and-backward pairing, and saw exactly why \(S_n = \frac{n}{2}(a_1 + a_n)\) must be true for every arithmetic series ever written. Then you spent the formula on theaters, savings plans, and forty-term sums that fold into one multiplication. Tomorrow we return to the sequence family you glimpsed in Example 1 of Lesson 1-3 — the one that grows by a constant ratio — and the week after, we ask this same what-does-it-total question about sums that never end.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. What are the three working parts of sigma notation, and why does the index never appear in the final answer?

2. Why does writing an arithmetic sum forward and backward and adding the columns prove the formula — what exactly cancels, and why?

3. In what sense is \(S_n = \frac{n}{2}(a_1 + a_n)\) the statement “number of terms times average term”?

4. Why does the arithmetic series formula fail for a sequence with unequal steps — where does the derivation break?
