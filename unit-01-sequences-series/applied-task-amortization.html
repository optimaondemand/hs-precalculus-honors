The Price of Patience: Amortization
Excel Modeling Task · 10 pts
📋 THE SCENARIO

You are buying your first car: $25,000, financed at 6% annual interest (0.5% per month). The dealer offers two loans — 60 months at $483.32 per month, or 36 months at $760.55 per month. The 60-month payment feels lighter. Whether it is lighter is a question this unit has equipped you to answer exactly.

A loan is Unit 1 run in reverse: each month the balance grows by 0.5% (a geometric step — Lesson 1-7), then shrinks by your payment (an arithmetic step — Lesson 1-4). The recursive column you have built four times in Excel — seed, rule, fill down — is the entire machinery. Banks call the resulting table an amortization schedule. You will build one, interrogate it, and grade an AI’s financial intuition against it.

🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
What does patience cost when you are the borrower — and what does it pay when you are the saver?
📝 THE TASK

Part 1 — Build the 60-month schedule. In a new Excel workbook, on a sheet named Loan60, build these columns for months 0 through 60:

  • Month (0, 1, 2, …, 60) — month 0 is the day you drive off, balance $25,000.
  • Interest this month = previous balance × 0.005.
  • Principal this month = payment − interest this month. (Put the payment, 483.32, in its own labeled cell at the top and reference it — e.g., $B$1 — so one edit changes the whole model.)
  • New balance = previous balance − principal this month. Fill everything down to month 60.

Model check: the month-60 balance should land within a dollar or two of $0 (the leftover pennies are rounding — real banks adjust the final payment). If it doesn’t, audit your first two rows by hand the way you audited every model this unit. Then add a SUM cell totaling the interest column, and a chart of balance vs. month.

Part 2 — The 36-month rival. Copy the sheet, name it Loan36, change the payment cell to 760.55, and read the table down to month 36 (the rows beyond it will go meaningless once the balance crosses zero — note where, and why the model’s domain of honesty ends there). Total its interest column. Then answer on a sheet named Answers, in labeled cells with full sentences:

  • Total interest paid on each loan, and the dollar difference. (Sanity check: the 60-month total should land near $4,000; if you are wildly off, audit.)
  • Look at month 1 on each sheet: how much of each payment was interest and how much was principal? Write one sentence on why early payments are the most interest-heavy — connect it to the balance the 0.5% acts on.
  • The verdict: what does the “lighter” 60-month loan actually cost for the privilege of smaller payments?

Part 3 — Grade the machine. Before computing anything further, ask your AI tutor to predict: “On a $25,000, 60-month car loan at 6% APR with payments of $483.32, suppose I make one extra $483.32 payment every December. Predict — without calculating — roughly how many months earlier the loan is paid off, and roughly how much interest is saved.” Record its prediction verbatim on your Answers sheet. Then build the truth: copy Loan60 to a sheet named ExtraPayment, and in every 12th month add one extra payment to that month’s principal. Read off the actual payoff month and actual interest saved. Finish with two or three sentences grading the AI’s prediction: was it close, cautious, or confidently wrong — and what does your spreadsheet know that it didn’t?

Honors stretch (optional): Find, by experimenting with the payment cell on a copy of Loan60, the monthly payment that would pay the loan off in exactly 48 months. Report the payment and how you hunted it down.
