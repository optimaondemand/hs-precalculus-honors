Infinite Geometric Series and Convergence
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to determine whether an infinite geometric series converges or diverges, using the condition \(|r| < 1\), and explain what convergence means in terms of partial sums.

  • I will be able to compute the sum of a convergent infinite geometric series with \(S = \dfrac{a_1}{1 - r}\), and explain where that formula comes from.

  • I will be able to apply infinite series to resolve Zeno’s dichotomy and to convert repeating decimals into exact fractions.

📚 VOCABULARY

Infinite series: The sum of all the terms of an infinite sequence, written \(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} a_k\).

Converge: An infinite series converges if its partial sums \(S_n\) approach a single finite value as \(n\) grows without bound. That value is called the sum of the series.

Diverge: An infinite series diverges if its partial sums do not approach any finite value — they grow without bound, or refuse to settle.

Limit: The value that a quantity approaches. The sum of a convergent infinite series is the limit of its partial sums — your first working encounter with the idea at the heart of calculus.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Twenty-four centuries ago, the philosopher Zeno of Elea argued that you cannot walk across a room. Before crossing the room, you must first cross half of it. Then half of what remains. Then half of that remainder — and so on, forever. Since the halves never run out, you have infinitely many crossings to perform, and no one — said Zeno — can complete infinitely many acts. Therefore motion is impossible. Aristotle wrestled with the argument; so did every serious thinker for two millennia. The paradox refuses to be waved away, because each individual claim in it is true: there really are infinitely many stages, and none of them may be skipped.

Write the stages as mathematics — the room has length 1 — and Zeno’s walk becomes an infinite geometric series:

\(\dfrac{1}{2} + \dfrac{1}{4} + \dfrac{1}{8} + \dfrac{1}{16} + \cdots\)

The question underneath the paradox is precise: can infinitely many positive numbers add up to something finite? Yesterday’s Example 3 all but answered it — the partial sums of \(40 + 20 + 10 + \cdots\) crept toward 80, halving their shortfall at every step. Today we finish the thought, name the idea (convergence), find the formula, and walk out of Zeno’s room.

Convergence: What Happens to \(r^n\) Decides Everything

Yesterday’s finite formula holds the whole story. For any geometric series,

\(S_n = \dfrac{a_1(1 - r^{\,n})}{1 - r}\)

Now let \(n\) grow without bound and watch the only moving part: the term \(r^{\,n}\).

  • If \(|r| < 1\) — a proper fraction, positive or negative — then \(r^{\,n}\) shrinks toward 0 as \(n\) grows: \(\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{10} \approx 0.001\), \(\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{20} \approx 0.000001\). The formula’s \((1 - r^{\,n})\) settles toward 1, and the partial sums settle toward a single value. The series converges.
  • If \(|r| \ge 1\), then \(r^{\,n}\) refuses to fade — it grows without bound (\(|r| > 1\)), stays put (\(r = 1\)), or oscillates (\(r = -1\)). The partial sums never settle. The series diverges, and it has no sum to speak of.

When \(|r| < 1\), replacing \(r^{\,n}\) by the 0 it approaches gives the infinite-series formula:

\(\Large S = \dfrac{a_1}{1 - r}, \qquad |r| < 1\)

Test it on yesterday’s cliffhanger: \(40 + 20 + 10 + \cdots\) has \(a_1 = 40\), \(r = \frac{1}{2}\), so \(S = \frac{40}{1/2} = 80\) — exactly the value the partial sums were stalking. The restriction \(|r| < 1\) is not fine print. It is the entire theory: it marks the boundary between series that settle and series that run away.

Example 1

Resolve Zeno: evaluate \(\dfrac{1}{2} + \dfrac{1}{4} + \dfrac{1}{8} + \dfrac{1}{16} + \cdots\)

Solution:

This is an infinite geometric series with \(a_1 = \frac{1}{2}\) and \(r = \frac{1}{2}\). Since \(|r| = \frac{1}{2} < 1\), it converges, and

\(S = \dfrac{a_1}{1 - r} = \dfrac{\tfrac{1}{2}}{1 - \tfrac{1}{2}} = \dfrac{\tfrac{1}{2}}{\tfrac{1}{2}} = 1\)

Answer: The series sums to exactly 1 — one whole room.

Sit with what just happened. Infinitely many positive distances, none of them skippable, total precisely the length of the room — no more, no less. Zeno was right that the stages are infinite in number; he was wrong to conclude they are infinite in total. The stages shrink fast enough that all infinitely many of them fit inside a finite length — and, if you walk at a steady pace, inside a finite time. The paradox dissolves not by dismissing the infinite but by computing it. It took mathematics two thousand years to earn this one line of algebra; you should feel the full weight of writing it.

Example 2

Determine whether each series converges; if it does, find its sum.

① \(24 + 12 + 6 + 3 + \cdots\)   ② \(9 - 3 + 1 - \dfrac{1}{3} + \cdots\)   ③ \(2 + 6 + 18 + 54 + \cdots\)

Solution:

① \(a_1 = 24\), \(r = \frac{12}{24} = \frac{1}{2}\). Since \(\left|\frac{1}{2}\right| < 1\): converges.

\(S = \dfrac{24}{1 - \tfrac{1}{2}} = \dfrac{24}{\tfrac{1}{2}} = 48\)

② \(a_1 = 9\), \(r = \frac{-3}{9} = -\frac{1}{3}\). The ratio is negative, but what matters is its size: \(\left|-\frac{1}{3}\right| = \frac{1}{3} < 1\): converges. Mind the sign in the denominator — subtracting a negative:

\(S = \dfrac{9}{1 - \left(-\tfrac{1}{3}\right)} = \dfrac{9}{\tfrac{4}{3}} = \dfrac{27}{4}\)

The partial sums of an alternating series close in on the limit from both sides — overshoot, undershoot, overshoot — like a pendulum settling.

③ \(a_1 = 2\), \(r = 3\). Since \(|3| \ge 1\): diverges. The terms themselves grow without bound; the partial sums chase them. This series has no sum, and no formula may be applied to it.

Answer: ① converges to 48   ② converges to \(\dfrac{27}{4}\)   ③ diverges.

⚠️ AI CHECKPOINT: THE FORMULA THAT DIDN'T CHECK ITS OWN LICENSE

A student asked an AI for the sum of \(2 + 6 + 18 + 54 + \cdots\), and it responded:

“Using the infinite geometric series formula with \(a_1 = 2\) and \(r = 3\): \(S = \dfrac{a_1}{1-r} = \dfrac{2}{1-3} = \dfrac{2}{-2} = -1\). The sum of the series is \(-1\).”

The algebra is flawless — every substitution correct, every simplification clean. And the conclusion is absurd on its face: an ever-growing sum of positive numbers cannot total negative one. The error sits in the very first move: the formula \(S = \frac{a_1}{1-r}\) carries a license, \(|r| < 1\), and this series’ ratio of 3 voids it. Applied outside its license, a formula produces confident nonsense — and an AI, which pattern-matches formulas fluently, is especially prone to exactly this failure.

Two habits guard you. Check the license before applying any formula — the hypotheses are part of the theorem. And interrogate answers for plausibility — a negative sum of positive terms should trip an alarm no matter who computed it, you, a spreadsheet, or a machine. This week’s Focus assignment hands you another AI claim to audit; hold it to today’s standard.

Example 3

Express the repeating decimal \(0.\overline{7} = 0.7777\ldots\) as an exact fraction.

Solution:

Unfold the decimal by place value — it is an infinite geometric series in disguise:

\(0.7777\ldots = \dfrac{7}{10} + \dfrac{7}{100} + \dfrac{7}{1000} + \cdots\)

Here \(a_1 = \frac{7}{10}\) and \(r = \frac{1}{10}\) (each term is the last, one decimal place further out). Since \(\left|\frac{1}{10}\right| < 1\):

\(S = \dfrac{\tfrac{7}{10}}{1 - \tfrac{1}{10}} = \dfrac{\tfrac{7}{10}}{\tfrac{9}{10}} = \dfrac{7}{9}\)

Answer: \(0.\overline{7} = \dfrac{7}{9}\). Check by division: \(7 \div 9 = 0.777\ldots\) ✓

Every repeating decimal you have ever met is a convergent geometric series — that is why repeating decimals are always rational numbers. The infinite tail of 7s is not approximately \(\frac{7}{9}\); it is \(\frac{7}{9}\), exactly, by the same reasoning that walked us across Zeno’s room.

Example 4

Express \(0.\overline{36} = 0.363636\ldots\) as an exact fraction in lowest terms.

Solution:

The repeating block is two digits long, so the series steps by hundredths:

\(0.363636\ldots = \dfrac{36}{100} + \dfrac{36}{10000} + \dfrac{36}{1000000} + \cdots\)

with \(a_1 = \frac{36}{100}\) and \(r = \frac{1}{100}\). Since \(\left|\frac{1}{100}\right| < 1\):

\(S = \dfrac{\tfrac{36}{100}}{1 - \tfrac{1}{100}} = \dfrac{\tfrac{36}{100}}{\tfrac{99}{100}} = \dfrac{36}{99} = \dfrac{4}{11}\)

Answer: \(0.\overline{36} = \dfrac{4}{11}\). Check: \(4 \div 11 = 0.3636\ldots\) ✓

The pattern generalizes beautifully: a repeating block of digits \(B\) of length \(\ell\) contributes \(\frac{B}{10^\ell - 1}\) — the block over as many 9s as it has digits. \(0.\overline{7} = \frac{7}{9}\), \(0.\overline{36} = \frac{36}{99}\), \(0.\overline{123} = \frac{123}{999}\). One theorem, wearing a party trick’s clothes.

💻 On Your Calculator: Watching Convergence Happen 15 min

Convergence is a claim about partial sums — so plot them. Open Desmos at desmos.com/calculator (or the window below):

  1. Line 1: S(n) = 24(1 − 0.5^n)/(1 − 0.5) — yesterday’s finite formula for Example 2’s first series, as a function of \(n\).
  2. Line 2: n1 = [1, ..., 20]. Line 3: (n1, S(n1)) — twenty partial sums, plotted.
  3. Line 4: y = 48 — the claimed sum, drawn as a horizontal line.
  4. Watch the points rise steeply, then flatten, pressing against the line without crossing it. Zoom in near \(y = 48\) again and again — the gap never reaches zero for any finite \(n\), but it dies faster than any zoom can chase. That is convergence, seen.

Now break it on purpose: change the 0.5 to 1.1 and re-plot (adjust the window). The points climb without ceiling — divergence, seen. Then try −1/3 with \(a_1 = 9\) and the line \(y = 27/4\): the pendulum settling from both sides. One last experiment: ratio −1. The partial sums hop between two values forever, settling on neither — divergence without escape, the strangest case in the family.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Do not confuse “the terms approach zero” with “the series converges to zero” — Example 1’s terms die away, but the series sums to 1, not 0. The terms are the steps; the series is the distance covered. And a warning for the road to calculus: terms shrinking to zero is necessary for convergence but not always sufficient — for geometric series the test \(|r| < 1\) settles everything, but stranger series await where the terms fade and the sum still runs away.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Archimedes computed the area under a parabola in the third century BC by summing the infinite geometric series \(1 + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{16} + \cdots = \frac{4}{3}\) — two millennia before calculus existed, and two centuries after Zeno insisted such infinities were unthinkable. He called his approach the method of exhaustion: exhaust the region with infinitely many shrinking triangles, then total them. Today’s formula dispatches his series in one line — but he did it first, with nothing but geometry and nerve.

Conclusion

This unit began with a list of numbers and a promise: describe the rule, and the infinite fits in the palm of your hand. Today the promise was kept in the strongest sense. An infinite geometric series converges exactly when \(|r| < 1\) — when \(r^n\) dies away, taking the finite formula’s only moving part with it — and then the whole endless sum collapses to \(S = \frac{a_1}{1-r}\). With that one line you dissolved a paradox that stood for two thousand years, revealed every repeating decimal as a rational number in disguise, and watched partial sums press against their limit in Desmos — your first true encounter with the limit, the idea on which all of calculus is built. The unit’s virtue was perseverance, and its deepest lesson is this: term by term, step by step, the patient march of small things arrives somewhere exact. Review well; you have built everything you need.
