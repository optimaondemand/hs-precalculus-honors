Modeling with Geometric Sequences
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to translate a percent change — growth or decay — into a common ratio, and build a geometric model from it.

  • I will be able to model compound interest, population growth, rebound height, and percent depreciation with geometric sequences, choosing the indexing that fits the situation.

  • I will be able to distinguish situations calling for an arithmetic model from those calling for a geometric one — and defend the choice.

📚 VOCABULARY

Growth factor: The common ratio of a growing percent-change model: \(r = 1 + \) (rate as a decimal). A 3% increase means \(r = 1.03\).

Decay factor: The common ratio of a shrinking percent-change model: \(r = 1 - \) (rate as a decimal). A 15% loss means \(r = 0.85\).

Compound interest: Interest computed on the current balance — including previously earned interest — so the balance grows geometrically.

Principal: The starting amount of money in an interest problem; the model’s initial value.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Lesson 1-4 modeled the world that changes by a fixed amount: the $2,200 raise, the $3,500 depreciation. But listen to how the world more often talks: prices rise 3 percent; the account earns 4 percent; the car loses 15 percent of its value each year. A percent is not an amount — it is a proportion of whatever is currently there. A 3% raise on a large salary is more dollars than a 3% raise on a small one. Change described by percent is multiplication in disguise, and multiplication at every step is precisely the geometric family’s signature.

The whole art of this lesson is one translation: a percent change becomes a common ratio. Grow by 3% and each new value is 103% of the old one — multiply by \(1.03\). Lose 15% and each new value is 85% of the old one — multiply by \(0.85\). Master that translation and every compound-interest table, population projection, and bouncing ball in this lesson opens to the formula you already own: \(a_n = a_1 r^{\,n-1}\) — or, when counting from time zero, \(a_n = a_0 r^{\,n}\).

From Percent to Ratio

To build a geometric model from a percent change:

  • Name the quantities, as always: what \(n\) counts, what \(a_n\) measures, where the count starts. Percent models almost always start naturally at time zero, so the \(a_0\) convention — \(a_n = a_0\,r^n\) — is usually the honest choice: by time \(n\), the multiplication has happened \(n\) times.
  • Convert the percent to a factor: growth of \(p\%\) gives \(r = 1 + \frac{p}{100}\); decay of \(p\%\) gives \(r = 1 - \frac{p}{100}\).
  • Anchor-test the model: at \(n = 0\) it must return the starting value; at \(n = 1\), the starting value times the factor, once.

The commonest translation error is multiplying by the rate instead of the factor — by \(0.03\) instead of \(1.03\). Multiplying by \(0.03\) doesn’t grow an account 3%; it obliterates 97% of it. The factor keeps the whole (the 1) and adds the change (the 0.03). Say it in words each time: “each year the town keeps all of itself and adds 3%.”

Example 1

A town of 12,000 people grows by 3% each year. Write a model for the population \(n\) years from now, and project the population in 10 years.

Solution:

Define the variables: let \(p_n\) be the population \(n\) years from now, with \(p_0 = 12{,}000\). Growth of 3% means each year’s population is 103% of the last: \(r = 1.03\). By year \(n\) the multiplication has happened \(n\) times:

\(p_n = 12{,}000\,(1.03)^n\)

For 10 years out:

\(p_{10} = 12{,}000\,(1.03)^{10}\)

\(p_{10} = 12{,}000\,(1.34392\ldots) \approx 16{,}127\)

Answer: \(p_n = 12{,}000(1.03)^n\); in 10 years the model projects about 16,100 people.

Two honest notes. First, the rounding: populations are whole people, and a model’s fourth digit is not a promise — reporting “about 16,100” respects what the model actually knows. Second, the surprise: 3% per year does not add 30% in ten years but about 34.4%, because each year’s growth compounds on the last. That extra 4.4% is the difference between the two families.

Example 2

A grandparent deposits $2,000 into an account earning 4% interest, compounded annually, on the day a child is born. No other deposits are made. What is the balance when the child turns 18?

Solution:

Define the variables: let \(b_n\) be the balance, in dollars, on the child’s \(n\)th birthday, so \(b_0 = 2{,}000\) (the principal) and \(r = 1.04\):

\(b_n = 2{,}000\,(1.04)^n\)

\(b_{18} = 2{,}000\,(1.04)^{18}\)

\(b_{18} = 2{,}000\,(2.02582\ldots) \approx 4{,}051.63\)

Answer: \(b_{18} \approx \$4{,}051.63\) — the money has slightly more than doubled.

Compare this with simple interest — 4% of the original $2,000, or $80, added each year. That is an arithmetic model, and after 18 years it yields \(2{,}000 + 18 \cdot 80 = \$3{,}440\). Compounding beat it by more than $600, because compound interest pays interest on the interest: each year’s 4% acts on a larger base. Banks, biology, and bouncing balls all keep their books geometrically; only the contract with fixed payments keeps them arithmetically.

💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Bankers estimate doubling with the Rule of 72: money at \(p\%\) annual interest doubles in roughly \(72 / p\) years. At 4%, that predicts \(72/4 = 18\) years — and Example 2’s exact computation landed at 2.026 times the principal at year 18, almost exactly a doubling. The rule is centuries old; Luca Pacioli, the Franciscan friar who taught mathematics to Leonardo da Vinci, recorded it in 1494. Why 72? The full answer needs logarithms — and is waiting for you in Unit 3.

Example 3

A ball is dropped from 8 feet. Each bounce rebounds to 60% of the height it fell from. Write a model for the rebound height after the \(n\)th bounce, and find the height after the 5th bounce.

Solution:

Define the variables: let \(h_n\) be the rebound height in feet after bounce \(n\). The drop height is \(h_0 = 8\) (before any bounce), and each bounce multiplies by the decay factor \(r = 0.6\):

\(h_n = 8\,(0.6)^n\)

\(h_5 = 8\,(0.6)^5 = 8\,(0.07776) = 0.62208\)

Answer: \(h_n = 8(0.6)^n\); after the fifth bounce the ball rises only about 0.62 feet — roughly seven and a half inches.

Five bounces erased more than 92% of the original height, yet no bounce ever rebounds to zero — each is still 60% of something positive. A geometric model with \(0 < r < 1\) dies away without ever quite dying. Whether the total distance such a ball travels is finite or infinite is a genuinely deep question — and it is exactly where this unit is headed in Lesson 1-9.

Example 4

A car bought for $24,000 loses 15% of its value each year. Model its value, find its worth after 6 years, and contrast this model with the straight-line depreciation of Lesson 1-4.

Solution:

Define the variables: let \(v_n\) be the value in dollars \(n\) years after purchase: \(v_0 = 24{,}000\), decay factor \(r = 1 - 0.15 = 0.85\):

\(v_n = 24{,}000\,(0.85)^n\)

\(v_6 = 24{,}000\,(0.85)^6 = 24{,}000\,(0.37715\ldots) \approx 9{,}052\)

Answer: \(v_n = 24{,}000(0.85)^n\); after 6 years the car is worth about $9,050.

Now the contrast. Straight-line depreciation subtracts the same dollars every year and eventually drives the value to zero and past it — you had to cut that model off at the boundary of honesty. Percent depreciation subtracts 15% of a shrinking value: big losses early (year one costs $3,600), gentler losses late (year six costs about $1,600). The geometric model never goes negative on its own — used cars lose proportion, not fixed dollars, and the marketplace agrees with the mathematics. Choosing between the families is not taste; it is a claim about how the world changes, and the data get the final vote.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Three traps, in descending order of damage. ① Multiplying by the rate instead of the factor (0.15 instead of 0.85 for a 15% loss). ② Treating percent change as fixed change — “loses 15% per year” is not “loses $3,600 per year,” except in year one. ③ Indexing drift — percent models usually anchor at \(a_0\), so the exponent is \(n\), not \(n-1\); if you instead let \(a_1\) be the starting value, the exponent must be \(n-1\). Whichever convention you choose, run the anchor test before predicting anything.

📊 Try It in Excel: Compounding, Watched Closely 15 min

Build Example 2’s account and put simple interest beside it, so the gap becomes visible:

  1. Column A: years 0 through 18. In B1 (year 0) type 2000; in B2 type =B1*1.04 and fill down. Column B is compound interest — the recursive geometric climb.
  2. In C1 type 2000; in C2 type =C1+80 and fill down. Column C is simple interest — the arithmetic alternative.
  3. In D1 type the explicit model =2000*1.04^A1 and fill down. Columns B and D must agree to the penny in every row; if not, hunt the indexing slip.
  4. Chart columns B and C together. For the first several years the two lines nearly touch — then the curve peels away from the line and never looks back. Read off the year the gap first exceeds $100 (it is earlier than most people guess).

Then flip the sign of the story: rebuild column B with =B1*0.85 and the $24,000 car. The same machinery runs growth and decay; only the factor’s side of 1 changes. Keep this workbook — the unit’s applied task (The Price of Patience) extends exactly this construction to a real loan.

⚠️ WHERE AI HELPS — AND WHERE IT DOESN'T
Before you compute, make your AI tutor predict: ask it, “A car loses 15% of its value each year. Without calculating, will it lose more dollars in year 1 or year 6 — and roughly what fraction of its value remains after 6 years?” Then check both claims against your Example 4 work (the true fraction is about 38%). A good AI explains the shrinking base; a sloppy one may reason as if the loss were fixed dollars, or estimate \(6 \times 15\% = 90\%\) lost. Prediction first, computation second, comparison always — that supervision loop is how a mathematician uses a fluent assistant, and you will run it again, formally, in the unit’s applied task.
▶  Try it yourself: A colony of 500 bacteria doubles every hour. Write a model for the count after \(n\) hours, and find the count after 12 hours. Work it on paper before you peek.
Let \(c_n\) be the count after \(n\) hours: \(c_0 = 500\), \(r = 2\), so \(c_n = 500 \cdot 2^n\). After 12 hours: \(c_{12} = 500 \cdot 4096 = 2{,}048{,}000\) — over two million, from five hundred, in half a day. (Doubling is a 100% growth rate: \(r = 1 + 1.00 = 2\). The percent-to-factor translation covers even the dramatic cases.)

Conclusion

Percent change is multiplication wearing everyday clothes, and the translation \(r = 1 \pm \frac{p}{100}\) turns any percent story — interest, population, rebound, depreciation — into a geometric model you already know how to drive. You saw compounding beat simple interest by paying interest on interest; you saw percent decay shrink a car’s value steeply then gently, where straight-line depreciation marched blindly past zero; and you practiced supervising an AI’s predictions against your own computation. One question from today refuses to close: the bouncing ball’s heights never reach zero, yet keep shrinking forever. Tomorrow we learn to add up a geometric family — finitely first — and the day after, we follow the bounces all the way down.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does a percent change translate to the factor \(1 \pm \frac{p}{100}\) rather than to \(\frac{p}{100}\) itself?

2. What, precisely, does compound interest compound — and why does that guarantee it outruns simple interest?

3. How do straight-line and percent depreciation disagree about a car’s future, and what would real-world data look like under each?

4. When you must choose between an arithmetic and a geometric model for a situation, what question about the situation decides it?
