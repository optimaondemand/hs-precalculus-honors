🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to derive the finite geometric series formula by the multiply-and-subtract method — and explain why the method collapses the sum.

  • I will be able to apply \(S_n = \dfrac{a_1(1 - r^n)}{1 - r}\) to sums given as lists or in sigma notation.

  • I will be able to solve accumulation problems — including annuity-style savings plans — by recognizing them as geometric series.

📚 VOCABULARY

Geometric series: The sum of the terms of a geometric sequence.

Finite geometric series formula: \(S_n = \dfrac{a_1(1 - r^n)}{1 - r}\), valid for \(r \neq 1\).

Annuity: A sequence of equal payments made at regular intervals into an interest-bearing account. Its accumulated value is a geometric series in disguise.

Telescoping cancellation: The mass cancellation that occurs when two long expressions are subtracted and all but a few terms annihilate in pairs — the engine of today’s derivation.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Return one last time to the vizier’s chessboard. We computed what the last square holds — \(2^{63}\) grains. But the king owes every square: \(1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + \cdots + 2^{63}\), a sixty-four-term geometric sum. Can Gauss’s trick from Lesson 1-5 rescue us? Try it: write the sum forward and backward, add columns — and the columns all come out different. Gauss’s pairing lived on the equal steps of the arithmetic family; a geometric series has no equal steps to balance. A new family needs a new trick.

The new trick is worthy of the family: since the sequence is built by multiplication, multiply the whole sum by the ratio — and watch it nearly become itself. That near-coincidence, engineered into a subtraction, collapses sixty-four terms into a fraction. It is one of the most-reused derivations in mathematics: you will meet it again in Unit 3 with mortgages and in calculus with power series. Learn the move, not just the formula.

Deriving the Formula: Multiply and Subtract

Let \(S_n\) be the sum of the first \(n\) terms of a geometric sequence. Write it out, then multiply every term by \(r\):

\(S_n = a_1 + a_1 r + a_1 r^2 + \cdots + a_1 r^{\,n-1}\)

\(r\,S_n = \phantom{a_1 + {}} a_1 r + a_1 r^2 + \cdots + a_1 r^{\,n-1} + a_1 r^{\,n}\)

Multiplying by \(r\) pushed every term one position to the right: the two rows share everything except the first term of the top row and the last term of the bottom. Subtract the second row from the first, and the shared middle annihilates:

\(S_n - r\,S_n = a_1 - a_1 r^{\,n}\)

Factor both sides — \(S_n(1 - r)\) on the left, \(a_1(1 - r^{\,n})\) on the right — and divide by \(1 - r\):

\(\Large S_n = \dfrac{a_1\,(1 - r^{\,n})}{1 - r}, \quad r \neq 1\)

The exclusion \(r \neq 1\) is not pedantry: dividing by \(1 - r\) is illegal when \(r = 1\). And no harm done — a “geometric” sequence with ratio 1 repeats one constant value, and its sum is simply \(n \cdot a_1\). Every restriction on a formula marks the exact step of the derivation that fails without it. When you see a restriction, ask: which line breaks? Here, this one.

Example 1

Find the sum of the first 10 terms of the geometric series \(3 + 6 + 12 + 24 + \cdots\)

Solution:

Identify the ingredients: \(a_1 = 3\), \(r = 2\), \(n = 10\):

\(S_{10} = \dfrac{3\,(1 - 2^{10})}{1 - 2}\)

\(S_{10} = \dfrac{3\,(1 - 1024)}{-1} = \dfrac{3\,(-1023)}{-1}\)

\(S_{10} = 3 \cdot 1023 = 3069\)

Answer: \(S_{10} = 3069\).

Keep the signs honest: with \(r > 1\), both \(1 - r^n\) and \(1 - r\) come out negative, and the two negatives cancel to give the positive sum they must. Notice also a curiosity worth remembering: \(3069 = 3 \cdot 1023\), and \(1023 = 2^{10} - 1\) is one less than the next term’s coefficient. In any doubling series, the sum of everything so far is one term’s worth less than the next term alone — which is why the vizier’s 64th square out-owes the other sixty-three squares combined.

Example 2

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{8} 5 \cdot 3^{\,k-1}\).

Solution:

Read the ingredients straight off the notation: the summand is the explicit form of a geometric sequence with \(a_1 = 5\) (at \(k=1\)) and \(r = 3\), summed over \(n = 8\) terms:

\(S_8 = \dfrac{5\,(1 - 3^8)}{1 - 3}\)

\(S_8 = \dfrac{5\,(1 - 6561)}{-2} = \dfrac{5\,(-6560)}{-2}\)

\(S_8 = 5 \cdot 3280 = 16{,}400\)

Answer: \(16{,}400\).

Example 3

Find the sum of the first 8 terms of the series \(40 + 20 + 10 + 5 + \cdots\), exactly.

Solution:

Here \(a_1 = 40\), \(r = \frac{1}{2}\), \(n = 8\). A fractional ratio changes nothing about the method — only the arithmetic asks for more care:

\(S_8 = \dfrac{40\left(1 - \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{8}\right)}{1 - \tfrac{1}{2}}\)

\(S_8 = \dfrac{40\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{256}\right)}{\tfrac{1}{2}} = 80 \cdot \dfrac{255}{256}\)

\(S_8 = \dfrac{20{,}400}{256} = \dfrac{1275}{16} = 79.6875\)

Answer: \(S_8 = \dfrac{1275}{16} = 79.6875\).

Look hard at that number. Eight terms of this shrinking series total just under 80 — and the factored form shows why: the sum is exactly \(80 \cdot \frac{255}{256}\), which is 80 short by one part in 256. Add more terms and the shortfall halves each time: \(S_9\) misses 80 by \(\frac{1}{512}\) of it, \(S_{10}\) by \(\frac{1}{1024}\). The partial sums are creeping up on 80 like a bounce dying away. Whether they ever arrive — and what “arrive” could even mean for an endless sum — is precisely tomorrow’s lesson. Today, hold the observation.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
The exponent in the formula is \(n\) — the number of terms — not \(n - 1\), and not the largest exponent you can see in the series. In \(5 + 5\cdot3 + \cdots + 5 \cdot 3^{7}\) there are eight terms (exponents 0 through 7 — fence posts again), so the formula uses \(3^8\). Counting the terms wrongly is the dominant error with this formula; count exponents from 0, or convert to sigma notation and read \(n\) off the limits.

Example 4

On the last day of every month, a student deposits $100 into an account earning 0.5% interest per month (6% annual, compounded monthly). What is the account worth immediately after the 12th deposit — and how much of that is interest?

Solution:

The trap is to treat this like Lesson 1-7’s single deposit — but there are twelve deposits, each with its own growing time. Track each deposit separately at the moment after deposit 12:

  • The 12th deposit was just made: it has grown for 0 months — worth \(100\).
  • The 11th deposit has compounded once — worth \(100(1.005)\).
  • The 10th deposit has compounded twice — worth \(100(1.005)^2\).
  • … and the 1st deposit has compounded eleven times — worth \(100(1.005)^{11}\).

The total is therefore

\(S = 100 + 100(1.005) + 100(1.005)^2 + \cdots + 100(1.005)^{11}\)

— a finite geometric series with \(a_1 = 100\), \(r = 1.005\), and \(n = 12\) terms. The formula (with \(r > 1\), both parentheses negative, signs canceling as in Example 1):

\(S_{12} = \dfrac{100\,(1 - 1.005^{12})}{1 - 1.005} = \dfrac{100\,(1.005^{12} - 1)}{0.005}\)

\(S_{12} = \dfrac{100\,(0.061678\ldots)}{0.005} \approx 1233.56\)

Answer: The account holds about $1,233.56. The student deposited \(12 \times \$100 = \$1{,}200\), so about $33.56 is interest.

Thirty-three dollars may look like modest magic — but this structure, the annuity, is how retirements are actually built: not one heroic deposit but a long geometric series of ordinary ones, each quietly compounding for however long it has. Run this same structure for thirty years instead of one and the interest dwarfs the deposits. That computation is exactly what you will do in Excel in this unit’s applied task, The Price of Patience — where the series runs in reverse, paying down a loan.

📊 Try It in Excel: The Annuity, Audited 15 min

Example 4 claimed each deposit could be tracked separately and totaled with one formula. Make the spreadsheet vouch for it:

  1. Column A: months 1 through 12. In B1 type 100 (the first month’s balance: one fresh deposit).
  2. In B2 type =B1*1.005+100 — “last month’s balance grows half a percent, then a new $100 arrives” — and fill down to B12. This is the recursion an account actually lives.
  3. Read B12: it should say 1233.56 (to the penny), agreeing with the series formula. Two entirely different accountings — deposit-by-deposit powers versus month-by-month recursion — one answer. When two independent methods agree, a mathematician starts to believe.
  4. Now feel the leverage of time: extend the table to 60 months (five years). Deposits: $6,000. Balance: read it off — and notice interest is no longer pocket change. Then try 360 months if you are curious what thirty years does.

Keep this workbook too. The applied task will ask you for exactly this construction with a loan balance that shrinks — same recursion, one sign flipped.

▶  Try it yourself: Find the sum of the first 7 terms of \(2 + 8 + 32 + 128 + \cdots\) Work it on paper before you peek.
Here \(a_1 = 2\), \(r = 4\), \(n = 7\): \(S_7 = \dfrac{2(1 - 4^7)}{1 - 4} = \dfrac{2(1 - 16384)}{-3} = \dfrac{2(-16383)}{-3} = \dfrac{32766}{3} = 10{,}922\).
▶  Try it yourself: Evaluate \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{6} 9\left(\tfrac{1}{3}\right)^{k-1}\), exactly.
\(a_1 = 9\), \(r = \frac{1}{3}\), \(n = 6\): \(S_6 = \dfrac{9\left(1 - \frac{1}{729}\right)}{1 - \frac{1}{3}} = \dfrac{9 \cdot \frac{728}{729}}{\frac{2}{3}} = \dfrac{\frac{728}{81} \cdot 3}{2} = \dfrac{728}{54} \cdot \dfrac{3}{3} = \dfrac{364}{27}\). As a decimal, about 13.48 — sneaking up on \(13.5\), which is \(\dfrac{9}{1 - 1/3}\). (Tomorrow, that denominator will explain itself.)
⚠️ WHERE AI HELPS — AND WHERE IT DOESN'T
Ask your AI tutor to walk through the multiply-and-subtract derivation with different letters — say, for \(T_m = b + b q + \cdots + b q^{\,m-1}\) — and audit it against this lesson line by line: does it shift the rows honestly? does it name the \(q \neq 1\) restriction, and at the right step? A derivation is the one place an AI cannot hide behind a correct final answer, because every line must justify the next. Grading a derivation is harder and more valuable than grading an answer — and it is a skill your Focus assignment will ask of you tomorrow.

Conclusion

The geometric family demanded its own summing trick, and the trick honored the family’s nature: multiply the sum by the ratio, subtract, and let the telescoping cancellation crush \(n\) terms into \(\frac{a_1(1-r^n)}{1-r}\). You practiced it on growing series, shrinking series, sigma notation, and an annuity that revealed twelve deposits as twelve staggered geometric terms. And Example 3 left a thread deliberately hanging: a shrinking series whose partial sums crowd ever closer to 80 without touching it. Tomorrow that thread gets pulled. The question — first asked by a philosopher named Zeno twenty-four centuries ago, to argue that motion itself is impossible — is whether an infinite sum can honestly equal a finite number. The answer is one of the most beautiful in mathematics, and you already hold every tool needed to see it.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does Gauss’s pairing fail on a geometric series, and how does the multiply-and-subtract trick exploit exactly what the pairing lacked?

2. In the subtraction \(S_n - rS_n\), which terms cancel and which survive — and why?

3. Why does the formula exclude \(r = 1\), and which line of the derivation breaks there?

4. Why is an annuity a geometric series rather than a single compounding deposit — what is different about the twelve $100 bills?
