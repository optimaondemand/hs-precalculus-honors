📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
Every problem below is percent change wearing a story — interest, populations, rebounds, depreciation. For each model, follow the discipline of Lessons 1-4 and 1-7: define your variables in words, state your starting index, translate the percent into a factor, and anchor-test before predicting. A calculator is welcome for evaluating powers; the setup and reasoning stay yours. This is a practice set: scored for honest completion, with worked solutions released after the due date for line-by-line self-correction.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
A percent is a proportion of whatever is currently there. What follows — for banks, for towns, for falling balls — from that one sentence?
📝 PROBLEM SET

Show your work for every problem in neat vertical form — one step per line, equals signs aligned, final answers labeled with units. For every model: define variables in words before using them.

1
Translate each percent change into a common ratio (factor):   ① grows 7% per year   ② loses 12% per year   ③ doubles each month   ④ grows 0.5% per month.
2
A city of 85,000 grows 2% per year. Write a model for the population \(n\) years from now, and project the population in 8 years, rounded honestly (say what precision the model deserves and why).
3
$3,500 is deposited at 5% interest, compounded annually, with no further deposits. Find the balance after 10 years — and also compute what simple interest ($175 added each year) would have produced, and state the gap between the two in dollars.
4
A ball dropped from 12 feet rebounds to 75% of each fall. Write a model for the rebound height after bounce \(n\), find the height after the 4th bounce, and determine — by building a short table of bounces, since we do not yet have logarithms — after which bounce the rebound first falls below 2 feet.
5
Audit the claim: A laptop costing $1,800 loses 25% of its value each year. A classmate reasons: “25% a year for 3 years is 75%, so after 3 years it’s worth 25% of $1,800 — that’s $450.” Compute the true 3-year value with a geometric model, state what fraction of its value the laptop actually retains, and explain in a sentence or two exactly where the classmate’s reasoning breaks.
6
Choose the family: For each scenario, state whether an arithmetic or a geometric model fits, give the \(d\) or \(r\), and justify in one sentence:   ① a savings account fed $50 every month   ② a salary raised 2% every year   ③ a reservoir losing 500,000 gallons every week of a drought   ④ a medication whose amount in the bloodstream halves every 6 hours.
7
Honors stretch: Test the Rule of 72 from the lesson at a 6% annual rate. The rule predicts doubling in \(72/6 = 12\) years: compute \((1.06)^{12}\) and report how close to exact doubling it lands. Then test the rule at 12% (prediction: 6 years) and at 1% (prediction: 72 years — you may trust your calculator’s power button). Write two or three sentences on where the rule is most accurate and why a banker would accept this much error from a mental-math tool.
💡 OPTIONAL: CHECK YOURSELF IN EXCEL
After your hand work is done, rebuild problems 3 and 4 as recursive columns in Excel (seed, then =previous*factor, fill down), with the explicit model in the next column. The columns must agree row by row; the year-10 balance and the bounce table should match your page. If they disagree, expand the first two rows by hand and find the slip. Nothing to submit; the hand-written PDF is the assignment.
