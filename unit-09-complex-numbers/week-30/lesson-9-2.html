Distance and Midpoint in the Complex Plane
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to compute the modulus \(|z|\) of a complex number and interpret it as the distance from \(z\) to the origin.

  • I will be able to compute the distance between two complex numbers using \(|z_1-z_2|\).

  • I will be able to compute the midpoint between two complex numbers using \(\dfrac{z_1+z_2}{2}\).

  • I will be able to connect both formulas directly to the Euclidean distance and midpoint formulas from coordinate geometry, and explain why they must agree.

📚 VOCABULARY

Modulus \(|z|\): for \(z=a+bi\), the nonnegative real number \(|z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\) — the distance from \(z\) to the origin in the complex plane.

Distance between two complex numbers: \(|z_1-z_2|\), the modulus of their difference — the straight-line distance between their two points in the complex plane.

Midpoint of two complex numbers: \(\dfrac{z_1+z_2}{2}\), the complex number whose point sits exactly halfway between the points for \(z_1\) and \(z_2\).

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Once Argand and Gauss had given the “imaginary” numbers a home in the plane, a natural question followed almost immediately: if complex numbers are points, does every tool you already own for measuring points — distance, midpoint — still work? The honest answer is that it works exactly as before, because a complex number's plot is, in every geometric respect, an ordinary point in an ordinary plane. What changes is only the notation: instead of writing a point as \((a,b)\) and reaching for the distance formula from coordinate geometry, you can now write the same point as \(a+bi\) and reach for a single absolute-value bar. The Pythagorean theorem does not know or care whether you call its two legs \(a\) and \(b\) or the real and imaginary parts of a complex number; the theorem simply measures the straight line between two points, and today's lesson is exactly that measurement, translated into the language of Unit 9.

Modulus: Distance from the Origin

For a complex number \(z=a+bi\), the modulus \(|z|\) is defined as

\(|z| = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}\)

This is precisely the Pythagorean-theorem distance from the point \((a,b)\) to the origin \((0,0)\): the real part and imaginary part are the two legs of a right triangle, and the modulus is its hypotenuse. \(|z|\) is always a nonnegative real number, even though \(z\) itself may have a negative real part, a negative imaginary part, or both.

Example 1

Find the modulus of \(z=3-4i\).

Solution:

\(|z| = \sqrt{3^2+(-4)^2} = \sqrt{9+16} = \sqrt{25} = 5\)

Answer: \(|3-4i|=5\). The point \((3,-4)\) sits exactly \(5\) units from the origin — the same \(3\text{-}4\text{-}5\) right triangle you have used since Unit 6.

Distance Between Two Complex Numbers

The distance between \(z_1=a+bi\) and \(z_2=c+di\) is the modulus of their difference:

\(|z_1-z_2| = \sqrt{(a-c)^2+(b-d)^2}\)

This is exactly the Euclidean distance formula between the points \((a,b)\) and \((c,d)\), because \(z_1-z_2=(a-c)+(b-d)i\) and taking its modulus applies the Pythagorean theorem to those same two differences.

Example 2

Find the distance between \(z_1=1+2i\) and \(z_2=4+6i\).

Solution:

\(z_1-z_2 = (1-4)+(2-6)i = -3-4i\)

\(|z_1-z_2| = \sqrt{(-3)^2+(-4)^2} = \sqrt{9+16} = \sqrt{25} = 5\)

Answer: The distance is \(5\). Check against the ordinary coordinate-geometry formula on the points \((1,2)\) and \((4,6)\): \(\sqrt{(1-4)^2+(2-6)^2}=\sqrt{9+16}=5\) ✓ — identical, because it is the identical calculation wearing different notation.

Key Fact: Midpoint of Two Complex Numbers

\(\text{midpoint} = \dfrac{z_1+z_2}{2}\)

Averaging the real parts and averaging the imaginary parts separately — which is exactly what adding \(z_1+z_2\) and dividing by \(2\) does — produces the point exactly halfway between them, matching the familiar midpoint formula \(\left(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2},\frac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)\) from coordinate geometry.

Example 3

Find the midpoint of \(z_1=1+2i\) and \(z_2=4+6i\).

Solution:

\(\dfrac{z_1+z_2}{2} = \dfrac{(1+4)+(2+6)i}{2} = \dfrac{5+8i}{2} = 2.5+4i\)

Answer: \(2.5+4i\), the point \((2.5,4)\). Check: the ordinary midpoint of \((1,2)\) and \((4,6)\) is \(\left(\frac{1+4}{2},\frac{2+6}{2}\right)=(2.5,4)\) ✓.

Example 4

Find both the distance and the midpoint between \(z_1=2-3i\) and \(z_2=-4+5i\).

Solution:

\(z_1-z_2 = (2-(-4))+(-3-5)i = 6-8i \qquad |z_1-z_2|=\sqrt{6^2+(-8)^2}=\sqrt{36+64}=\sqrt{100}=10\)

\(\dfrac{z_1+z_2}{2} = \dfrac{(2-4)+(-3+5)i}{2} = \dfrac{-2+2i}{2} = -1+i\)

Answer: Distance \(=10\); midpoint \(=-1+i\). Check against coordinate geometry on \((2,-3)\) and \((-4,5)\): distance \(=\sqrt{(2-(-4))^2+(-3-5)^2}=\sqrt{36+64}=10\) ✓, and midpoint \(=\left(\frac{2-4}{2},\frac{-3+5}{2}\right)=(-1,1)\) ✓.

💻 On Your Calculator: Confirming Distance and Midpoint

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), plot two complex numbers as ordered pairs — for Example 4, type (2, -3) and (-4, 5) — then type distance((2,-3),(-4,5)) directly into the expression list to confirm the distance instantly. For the midpoint, type ((2 + -4)/2, (-3 + 5)/2) as a third point and confirm it plots exactly halfway between the other two, right where your hand computation of \(-1+i\) predicted.

▶  Try it yourself: Find the modulus of \(z=-5+12i\).
\(|z|=\sqrt{(-5)^2+12^2}=\sqrt{25+144}=\sqrt{169}=13\).
▶  Try it yourself: Find the distance and midpoint between \(z_1=-3+i\) and \(z_2=1-3i\).
\(z_1-z_2=(-3-1)+(1-(-3))i=-4+4i\), so distance \(=\sqrt{(-4)^2+4^2}=\sqrt{32}=4\sqrt2\). Midpoint \(=\dfrac{(-3+1)+(1-3)i}{2}=\dfrac{-2-2i}{2}=-1-i\).
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Computing \(|z_1|-|z_2|\) (the difference of the two moduli) instead of \(|z_1-z_2|\) (the modulus of the difference) when asked for the distance between two complex numbers. These are not the same quantity, and confusing them can even produce a negative “distance,” which is a sure sign something has gone wrong. Always subtract the complex numbers first, then take the modulus of that single result.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The word “modulus” comes from the Latin modulus, a small measure — the same root that gives English “module” and “model.” It is a fitting name: the modulus is, quite literally, the measure of how far a complex number sits from the plane's center, the single number that tells you a number's size while leaving its direction to a separate quantity you will meet formally in Lesson 9-4.

Conclusion

Nothing about distance or midpoint required new machinery today — only the recognition that a complex number, once plotted, is a point like any other, and the Pythagorean theorem never asked what you decided to call its coordinates. The modulus you computed today as “distance from the origin” is about to earn a second job: in Lesson 9-4, that same number becomes the \(r\) in a complex number's polar form, doing for the complex plane exactly what \(r\) already does for the polar plane from Unit 8.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why must the formula for distance between two complex numbers reduce exactly to the Euclidean distance formula from coordinate geometry?

2. Why is the modulus \(|z|\) always nonnegative, no matter the signs of \(z\)'s real and imaginary parts?

3. Why is \(|z_1-z_2|\) the correct way to measure the distance between two complex numbers, while \(|z_1|-|z_2|\) is not?

4. What does it mean that averaging two complex numbers' real parts and averaging their imaginary parts separately produces exactly the point halfway between them?
