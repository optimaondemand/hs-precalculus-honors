The Complex Plane: Arithmetic Seen Geometrically
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to plot a complex number \(a+bi\) as the point \((a,b)\) in the complex plane, and plot its conjugate \(\overline{z}\).

  • I will be able to add, subtract, and conjugate complex numbers algebraically, and describe each operation's geometric effect — a translation for addition and subtraction, a reflection across the real axis for conjugation.

  • I will be able to multiply complex numbers algebraically and explain the result geometrically as a rotation-and-scaling of the plane, verifying the special case of multiplying by \(i\).

  • I will be able to write a precise paragraph, using correct mathematical vocabulary, explaining what multiplication by \(i\) does to a point in the complex plane.

📚 VOCABULARY

Imaginary unit \(i\): the number defined by \(i^2=-1\), so that \(i=\sqrt{-1}\).

Complex number: a number of the form \(a+bi\), where \(a\) and \(b\) are real numbers; \(a\) is called the real part and \(b\) the imaginary part.

Complex plane (Argand plane): a plane in which the complex number \(a+bi\) is plotted as the point \((a,b)\) — the horizontal axis carries the real part, the vertical axis the imaginary part.

Complex conjugate \(\overline{z}\): for \(z=a+bi\), the number \(\overline{z}=a-bi\) — same real part, opposite sign on the imaginary part.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In 1545, the Italian mathematician Girolamo Cardano was working through a problem that every algebra student's formulas ought to have handled without incident: divide \(10\) into two parts whose product is \(40\). Set up as an equation, the two parts turn out to be \(5+\sqrt{-15}\) and \(5-\sqrt{-15}\) — and there sat a square root of a negative number, refusing to resolve into anything Cardano's era considered a real quantity. He pressed through the arithmetic anyway, multiplied the two expressions together by the ordinary rules of algebra, and found that the product genuinely did come out to \(40\), exactly as required, even though the numbers involved seemed to name nothing at all. Cardano himself called the reasoning behind this kind of calculation his “mental tortures,” and confessed he considered the resulting quantities as subtle as they were useless. He had found the arithmetic worked. He had no idea, yet, what the numbers were.

Three decades later, the engineer and mathematician Rafael Bombelli took Cardano's discomfort and pushed straight past it. Working on cubic equations whose real, ordinary, entirely respectable solutions could only be reached by passing through expressions involving square roots of negative numbers, Bombelli made a decision he later described as a “wild thought”: treat these strange quantities as legitimate numbers, invent rules for adding and multiplying them, and simply carry the algebra through to the end. When he did, the impossible square roots canceled out in exactly the way ordinary algebra promised they should, and the real answer that came out the other side was correct every time. Bombelli's wild thought is, in essence, the entire method of this lesson: the arithmetic of these numbers is trustworthy, whether or not anyone can yet say what kind of thing they measure.

For two more centuries, the numbers Bombelli had tamed algebraically still had no home geometrically — and no respect, either. It was Renée Descartes, in the 1600s, who gave them the name that has stuck ever since, and he did not mean it as a compliment: he called them nombres imaginaires, “imaginary numbers,” precisely because he considered them fictions of the imagination rather than genuine quantities. The name survived; Descartes's judgment did not. Working independently in the early 1800s, the Swiss bookkeeper Jean-Robert Argand and the mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss each proposed the same resolution: let the real numbers occupy a horizontal axis, as always, and let the “imaginary” part of a complex number occupy a second axis, running perpendicular to the first. A number that seemed to name nothing turned out to name a point in a plane, as concretely and as visibly as any coordinate pair you have ever plotted. It took the courage of mathematicians willing to keep working with a number their own era dismissed as impossible, and it is exactly that courage this unit asks you to imitate.

The Complex Plane: A New Address for \(a+bi\)

Every complex number \(z=a+bi\) is plotted in the complex plane exactly the way you would plot the ordered pair \((a,b)\): the real part \(a\) sets the horizontal position, and the imaginary part \(b\) sets the vertical position. \(z=3+4i\) sits at the same location a rectangular-coordinates student would call \((3,4)\); the horizontal axis is simply relabeled the real axis and the vertical axis the imaginary axis. Nothing about plotting a complex number is new machinery — what is new is realizing that arithmetic performed on these numbers has a visible, geometric meaning on that same plane.

Example 1

Plot \(z=5-2i\) and its conjugate \(\overline{z}\). Describe the geometric relationship between them.

Solution:

\(z=5-2i\) plots at the point \((5,-2)\). Its conjugate is \(\overline{z}=5-(-2)i=5+2i\), which plots at \((5,2)\). The real part stayed at \(5\); only the sign of the imaginary part flipped.

Answer: \(\overline{z}=5+2i\). Geometrically, \((5,2)\) is the mirror image of \((5,-2)\) across the horizontal axis — taking the conjugate of any complex number reflects its point across the real axis. This is true in general, not just for this example: conjugation never touches the real part, and always negates the imaginary part, which is precisely what a reflection across a horizontal axis does to a coordinate pair.

Addition and Subtraction as Translation

\((a+bi)+(c+di) = (a+c)+(b+d)i \qquad (a+bi)-(c+di) = (a-c)+(b-d)i\)

Adding two complex numbers adds their real parts and adds their imaginary parts separately — exactly the rule for adding vectors component by component. Geometrically, adding a fixed complex number \(w\) to every point in the plane translates the plane by the vector \((\text{Re}(w),\text{Im}(w))\): every point slides the same distance in the same direction. Subtraction is a translation in the reverse direction.

Example 2

Compute \((3+2i)+(4+i)\), and describe the result as a translation.

Solution:

\((3+2i)+(4+i) = (3+4)+(2+1)i = 7+3i\)

Answer: \(7+3i\), the point \((7,3)\). The point \((3,2)\) has been translated by the vector \((4,1)\) — slid \(4\) units right and \(1\) unit up — landing exactly on \((7,3)\).

Example 3

Compute \((5+4i)-(2+3i)\), and describe the result as a translation.

Solution:

\((5+4i)-(2+3i) = (5-2)+(4-3)i = 3+i\)

Answer: \(3+i\), the point \((3,1)\). The point \((5,4)\) has been translated by the vector \((-2,-3)\) — slid \(2\) units left and \(3\) units down — landing exactly on \((3,1)\).

Key Fact: Multiplication as Rotation-and-Scaling

Multiplying two complex numbers follows the ordinary distributive rule, together with \(i^2=-1\):

\((a+bi)(c+di) = ac+adi+bci+bdi^2 = (ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i\)

Geometrically, multiplying by a fixed complex number \(w\) does two things to every point in the plane at once: it scales distances from the origin by a factor of \(|w|\), and it rotates the whole plane about the origin by the angle \(w\) makes with the real axis. Unit 9-4 makes this precise once you have polar form in hand; for now, verify it directly by example.

Example 4

Compute \((1+i)(2+3i)\). Confirm that its distance from the origin equals the product of the two factors' distances from the origin.

Solution:

\((1+i)(2+3i) = 1(2)+1(3i)+i(2)+i(3i) = 2+3i+2i+3i^2 = 2+5i-3 = -1+5i\)

Distance of \(1+i\) from the origin: \(\sqrt{1^2+1^2}=\sqrt2\). Distance of \(2+3i\) from the origin: \(\sqrt{2^2+3^2}=\sqrt{13}\). Distance of the product \(-1+5i\) from the origin: \(\sqrt{(-1)^2+5^2}=\sqrt{26}\).

Answer: \(-1+5i\). Check: \(\sqrt2\cdot\sqrt{13}=\sqrt{26}\) ✓ — the product's distance from the origin is exactly the product of the two factors' distances, precisely the scaling half of the rule.

Example 5

Compute \((3+4i)\cdot i\). Show that this multiplication rotates the point \((3,4)\) by \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise about the origin, without changing its distance from the origin.

Solution:

\((3+4i)\cdot i = 3i+4i^2 = 3i-4 = -4+3i\)

Distance check: \(|3+4i|=\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5\) and \(|-4+3i|=\sqrt{(-4)^2+3^2}=\sqrt{25}=5\) — the distance from the origin is unchanged, so this multiplication is a pure rotation with no scaling. Rotating the point \((3,4)\) by \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise about the origin sends \((x,y)\) to \((-y,x)\), which is \((-4,3)\) — exactly the point \(-4+3i\) plots at.

Answer: \(-4+3i\). Multiplying any complex number by \(i\) rotates its point \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise about the origin while leaving its distance from the origin exactly the same — a rotation with no scaling at all, since \(|i|=1\).

Key Fact: The Powers of \(i\) Cycle with Period 4

\(i^1=i \qquad i^2=-1 \qquad i^3=-i \qquad i^4=1 \qquad\) then the pattern repeats: \(i^5=i,\ i^6=-1,\ \ldots\)

Because multiplying by \(i\) is a \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise rotation with no scaling, each successive power of \(i\) simply rotates the previous point another quarter turn about the origin: \(i^1=i\) sits at \((0,1)\), a quarter turn further is \(i^2=-1\) at \((-1,0)\), another quarter turn is \(i^3=-i\) at \((0,-1)\), and a fourth quarter turn returns all the way to \(i^4=1\) at \((1,0)\) — back exactly where a full \(360^\circ\) turn should land. From there, the same four points repeat forever.

💻 On Your Calculator: Plotting and Checking Complex Arithmetic

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), plot any complex number \(a+bi\) simply by typing the ordered pair (a, b). Plot \(3+4i\) as (3, 4) and its conjugate as (3, -4), and confirm by eye that the two points sit mirrored across the horizontal axis, exactly as Example 1 predicted. Then check Example 5's rotation directly: plot (3, 4) and (-4, 3) together, and use Desmos's distance tool (or type distance((3,4),(0,0)) and distance((-4,3),(0,0))) to confirm both points are exactly \(5\) units from the origin, even though multiplying by \(i\) moved the point to an entirely new location.

▶  Try it yourself: Compute \((6-3i)+(-2+5i)\) and describe the translation.
\((6-3i)+(-2+5i) = (6-2)+(-3+5)i = 4+2i\). The point \((6,-3)\) is translated by \((-2,5)\) — \(2\) units left, \(5\) units up — landing on \((4,2)\).
▶  Try it yourself: Compute \((2-i)\cdot i\), and state which direction it rotates \((2,-1)\).
\((2-i)\cdot i = 2i-i^2 = 2i+1 = 1+2i\). The point \((2,-1)\) rotates to \((-(-1),2)=(1,2)\) — a \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise turn about the origin, matching the general rule for multiplying by \(i\).
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Believing the conjugate flips both coordinates, as if it reflected the point through the origin. It does not. Conjugation reflects across the real axis only — the real part is left completely alone, and only the imaginary part changes sign. \(\overline{5-2i}=5+2i\), not \(-5+2i\). Reflecting through the origin (negating both parts) is a different operation entirely, equivalent to multiplying by \(-1\).
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Descartes coined “imaginary number” as a term of dismissal — and it is a small irony of mathematical history that his skeptical label is the one every textbook, including this one, still uses today. The numbers themselves were vindicated long ago; only the name stuck around as a reminder of how long it took the mathematical community to trust what the arithmetic had been telling it all along.

Conclusion

Four operations, four geometric pictures: addition and subtraction slide the plane, conjugation reflects it across the real axis, and multiplication turns and stretches it about the origin — with multiplication by \(i\) alone giving a pure quarter-turn. None of this required abandoning ordinary algebra; it required only trusting Bombelli's wild thought that the algebra of these numbers was sound, and then noticing, as Argand and Gauss did, that the algebra was quietly describing motion in a plane the whole time. Tomorrow that same plane gets a ruler: you will measure exactly how far apart two complex numbers sit, and exactly what point lies halfway between them.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does it make sense that conjugation reflects a point across the real axis specifically, rather than across the imaginary axis or through the origin?

2. What did Bombelli's “wild thought” actually require of him — and why is trusting a consistent algebra before you fully understand what it describes a form of mathematical courage rather than recklessness?

3. Why does multiplying by \(i\) change a point's direction from the origin but never its distance from the origin?

4. How does watching the powers of \(i\) trace the same four points over and over help explain why \(i^{4}=1\) rather than some new, fifth kind of number?
