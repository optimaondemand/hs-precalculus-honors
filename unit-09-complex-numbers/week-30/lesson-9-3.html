Solving Problems with Complex Numbers
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to solve a quadratic equation with a negative discriminant, expressing the solutions in \(a+bi\) form.

  • I will be able to verify a complex solution by substituting it back into the original equation, rather than trusting the quadratic formula blindly.

  • I will be able to locate a pair of complex solutions as points in the complex plane and describe their symmetry.

  • I will be able to use an AI tool to generate additional complex-solution quadratics and independently verify its work, rather than accepting it on trust.

📚 VOCABULARY

Discriminant: for \(ax^2+bx+c=0\), the quantity \(b^2-4ac\) under the radical in the quadratic formula; its sign determines the type of solutions the equation has.

Complex solution (complex root): a solution to an equation that is a complex number \(a+bi\) with \(b\ne0\) — one that does not correspond to any point on the real number line.

Conjugate pair: two complex solutions of the form \(a+bi\) and \(a-bi\), which appear together whenever a polynomial equation with real-number coefficients has a non-real solution.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Recall the number that started this unit: \(5+\sqrt{-15}\), the quantity that gave Cardano his “mental tortures” back in Lesson 9-1. That expression came from solving an ordinary-looking algebra problem, and Cardano's discomfort came precisely from not yet having a number system in which \(\sqrt{-15}\) meant anything. You now have that number system. Today's lesson takes the single most common place a negative square root turns up in ordinary algebra — the quadratic formula, applied to an equation whose discriminant happens to be negative — and shows that the “impossible” solutions it produces are not errors, dead ends, or nonsense. They are complex numbers, exactly as legitimate as any other, and they can be checked, trusted, and put to work.

The Discriminant Decides the Kind of Solution

For \(ax^2+bx+c=0\) with real-number coefficients, the quadratic formula gives

\(x = \dfrac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}\)

The quantity \(b^2-4ac\), the discriminant, decides what kind of solutions appear: a positive discriminant gives two distinct real solutions, a discriminant of exactly \(0\) gives one repeated real solution, and a negative discriminant gives two complex solutions, written in \(a+bi\) form by factoring \(i=\sqrt{-1}\) out of the negative square root.

Example 1

Solve \(x^2+9=0\).

Solution:

\(x^2=-9 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x=\pm\sqrt{-9} = \pm\sqrt{9}\cdot\sqrt{-1} = \pm3i\)

Answer: \(x=3i\) or \(x=-3i\). Check: \((3i)^2+9 = 9i^2+9 = -9+9 = 0\) ✓, and \((-3i)^2+9=9i^2+9=-9+9=0\) ✓ — both solutions satisfy the original equation exactly.

Example 2

Solve \(x^2+4x+13=0\) using the quadratic formula, and verify your answer by substitution.

Solution:

\(a=1,\ b=4,\ c=13 \qquad b^2-4ac = 16-52 = -36\)

\(x = \dfrac{-4\pm\sqrt{-36}}{2} = \dfrac{-4\pm6i}{2} = -2\pm3i\)

Verify \(x=-2+3i\) by substitution: \((-2+3i)^2+4(-2+3i)+13\).

\((-2+3i)^2 = 4-12i+9i^2 = 4-12i-9 = -5-12i\)

\(4(-2+3i) = -8+12i\)

\((-5-12i)+(-8+12i)+13 = (-5-8+13)+(-12i+12i) = 0+0i = 0\)

Answer: \(x=-2+3i\) or \(x=-2-3i\), a conjugate pair. The substitution above confirms \(x=-2+3i\) genuinely satisfies the equation — not merely that the formula produced it, but that it checks out under the same algebra you would use to check any real solution.

Example 3

An engineer models the total opposition to current flow in a small AC circuit with the equation \(z^2-6z+34=0\), where \(z\) represents a quantity built from the circuit's resistance and reactance. Solve for \(z\), and verify your answer by substitution.

Solution:

\(a=1,\ b=-6,\ c=34 \qquad b^2-4ac = 36-136 = -100\)

\(z = \dfrac{6\pm\sqrt{-100}}{2} = \dfrac{6\pm10i}{2} = 3\pm5i\)

Verify \(z=3+5i\): \((3+5i)^2-6(3+5i)+34 = (9+30i-25)-(18+30i)+34 = (-16+30i)+(-18-30i)+34 = (-16-18+34)+(30i-30i) = 0\).

Answer: \(z=3+5i\) or \(z=3-5i\). Circuits like this one are exactly why electrical engineers work with complex numbers every day: a quantity called impedance, written \(R+Xi\) for resistance \(R\) and reactance \(X\), behaves algebraically like any other complex number, and equations describing it routinely have complex solutions that are fully meaningful within the model — not errors to be explained away.

Key Fact: Complex Roots Come in Conjugate Pairs

Whenever a polynomial equation has real-number coefficients, its non-real solutions always come in conjugate pairs: if \(a+bi\) is a solution, so is \(a-bi\). You can see why directly from the quadratic formula — the \(\pm\) in front of \(\sqrt{b^2-4ac}\) produces the two solutions \(\frac{-b}{2a}+\frac{\sqrt{4ac-b^2}}{2a}i\) and \(\frac{-b}{2a}-\frac{\sqrt{4ac-b^2}}{2a}i\), which are conjugates of each other by construction. Geometrically, this means the two solutions always plot as reflections of each other across the real axis — exactly Lesson 9-1's conjugate-as-reflection rule, now showing up inside every complex-solution quadratic you will ever solve.

Example 4

Plot the two solutions to Example 2, \(x=-2+3i\) and \(x=-2-3i\), and describe their geometric relationship.

Solution:

\(-2+3i\) plots at \((-2,3)\); \(-2-3i\) plots at \((-2,-3)\).

Answer: The two points share the same real part and have opposite imaginary parts, so they are mirror images of each other across the real axis — precisely what it means for two numbers to be conjugates, and precisely what the Key Fact above guarantees for any real-coefficient quadratic with a negative discriminant.

🤖 AI-ENHANCED: SUPERVISE THE TOOL
Ask your AI tutor to generate two more quadratic equations with complex solutions, similar in style to Example 2 or Example 3. Then, for each one, solve it yourself with the quadratic formula and verify the solution by substituting it back into the original equation, exactly as this lesson's worked examples did — do not simply trust that the AI's equation or its stated solutions are correct. If your substitution does not come out to zero, work back through both your algebra and the AI's to find where the mismatch happened. An AI tutor is useful for generating practice material quickly; it is your own substitution check, not the AI's say-so, that tells you whether an answer is actually right.

💻 On Your Calculator: Seeing Why the Discriminant Matters

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), graph \(y=x^2+4x+13\) from Example 2. Notice that the parabola never crosses the \(x\)-axis at all — it stays entirely above it, with its vertex sitting at \(y=9\). That is exactly what a negative discriminant means graphically: no real \(x\)-intercepts, because the equation's real-valued graph never touches zero. The solutions \(-2\pm3i\) are real and correct as complex numbers; they are simply not visible on a graph that only plots real inputs and real outputs.

▶  Try it yourself: Solve \(x^2+2x+5=0\), and verify one solution by substitution.
\(b^2-4ac=4-20=-16\), so \(x=\dfrac{-2\pm\sqrt{-16}}{2}=\dfrac{-2\pm4i}{2}=-1\pm2i\). Check \(x=-1+2i\): \((-1+2i)^2+2(-1+2i)+5 = (1-4i-4)+(-2+4i)+5 = (-3-4i)+(-2+4i)+5 = 0\) ✓.
▶  Try it yourself: Without solving, state whether \(x^2-5x+11=0\) has real or complex solutions, and why.
\(b^2-4ac = 25-44 = -19 < 0\), so the equation has two complex solutions, a conjugate pair — the discriminant's sign alone answers this without solving the equation at all.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Writing \(\sqrt{-36}\) as \(6i\) without first factoring, or worse, treating \(\sqrt{-36}\) as if the ordinary rule \(\sqrt{a}\cdot\sqrt{b}=\sqrt{ab}\) still applies to negative numbers under the root. That product rule for radicals is proven only for nonnegative \(a\) and \(b\); applying it carelessly to two negative radicands can produce a sign error. The reliable method is always to factor out \(\sqrt{-1}=i\) first — \(\sqrt{-36}=\sqrt{36}\cdot\sqrt{-1}=6i\) — and then simplify the remaining real radical normally.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Cardano's original 1545 problem — dividing \(10\) into two parts with product \(40\) — leads to exactly this lesson's kind of equation: \(x(10-x)=40\) rearranges to \(x^2-10x+40=0\), whose discriminant is \(100-160=-60\), giving the complex solutions \(5\pm i\sqrt{15}\) that so troubled him. Four hundred and eighty years later, the method for handling it is a routine part of a precalculus course — a fair measure of how far mathematical courage, once exercised, becomes ordinary competence for everyone who follows.

Conclusion

A negative discriminant does not mean a quadratic has no solutions; it means the quadratic's solutions are complex, and today you confirmed — by direct substitution, the same standard you would apply to any real solution — that those complex solutions are genuinely correct. The conjugate-pair pattern you saw in every example is not a coincidence of these particular equations; it is a guaranteed feature of any real-coefficient polynomial, and it is the same reflection-across-the-real-axis relationship you met geometrically in Lesson 9-1. Tomorrow, the modulus you have already been computing gets a partner — an angle — and together they give every complex number a second, equally useful address: its polar form.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does a negative discriminant guarantee that a quadratic's solutions are complex rather than real?

2. Why is substituting a claimed complex solution back into the original equation a more trustworthy check than simply re-deriving it with the quadratic formula a second time?

3. Why must the two non-real solutions of a real-coefficient quadratic always be conjugates of each other?

4. What responsibility do you take on when you use an AI tool to generate practice problems, and why does that responsibility not disappear just because the AI produced a confident-looking answer?
