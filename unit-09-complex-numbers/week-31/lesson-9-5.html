Trigonometric Form: Multiplying and Dividing
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to rewrite a complex number in trigonometric form, and rewrite a trigonometric-form complex number back in rectangular form.

  • I will be able to derive the product and quotient rules for trigonometric-form complex numbers from the angle-sum and angle-difference identities.

  • I will be able to multiply and divide complex numbers in trigonometric form by multiplying (or dividing) their moduli and adding (or subtracting) their arguments.

  • I will be able to verify a trigonometric-form product or quotient by cross-checking it against direct rectangular multiplication.

📚 VOCABULARY

Trigonometric form (polar form): a complex number written as \(z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\), often abbreviated \(r\,\mathrm{cis}\,\theta\), where \(r\) and \(\theta\) locate the number by distance and direction rather than by real and imaginary parts.

Modulus: \(r=|z|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\), the distance from the origin to the point \(z\) — the exact same quantity Unit 8 called \(r\), now measured for a point that happens to be a complex number.

Argument: \(\theta\), the angle the ray to \(z\) makes with the positive real axis — Unit 8's \(\theta\) under a new name, and just as multi-valued: add any whole multiple of \(2\pi\) and you name the identical point.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

You have already learned to plot a complex number \(a+bi\) as a point \((a,b)\) in a plane — the insight, formalized independently around the turn of the nineteenth century by the Norwegian surveyor Caspar Wessel, the Swiss bookkeeper Jean-Robert Argand, and confirmed with Gauss's own considerable authority, that a number once dismissed as impossible could simply be a point like any other. But a point in a plane, this whole course learned three weeks ago, does not owe its address to only one coordinate system. It can be named by a distance and a direction from a fixed center exactly as readily as by two perpendicular measurements. Today, the plane Unit 8 spent an entire unit exploring turns out to already contain every complex number you have ever written — and the polar dictionary you built there, \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\), \(r^2=x^2+y^2\), \(\tan\theta=\frac{y}{x}\), transfers to the complex plane completely unchanged, because a complex number simply is a point.

A Complex Number's Second Address

Every nonzero complex number \(z=x+yi\) can be rewritten in trigonometric form: \(z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\), where \(r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) is the modulus and \(\theta\) is any argument satisfying \(\cos\theta=\frac{x}{r}\), \(\sin\theta=\frac{y}{r}\). As in Unit 8, you find the reference angle from \(\tan\theta=\frac{y}{x}\) and then place \(\theta\) in the correct quadrant by looking at the actual signs of \(x\) and \(y\) — the formula still cannot see the point; only you, looking at it, can.

Example 1 — Two Numbers, Two Addresses

Write \(z_1=1+i\sqrt3\) and \(z_2=\sqrt3+i\) in trigonometric form.

Solution:

For \(z_1\): \(r_1=\sqrt{1^2+(\sqrt3)^2}=\sqrt{4}=2\). \(\tan\theta_1=\frac{\sqrt3}{1}=\sqrt3\), reference angle \(\frac{\pi}{3}\); since \(x>0\) and \(y>0\) (Quadrant I), \(\theta_1=\frac{\pi}{3}\).

For \(z_2\): \(r_2=\sqrt{(\sqrt3)^2+1^2}=\sqrt4=2\). \(\tan\theta_2=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\), reference angle \(\frac{\pi}{6}\); Quadrant I again, so \(\theta_2=\frac{\pi}{6}\).

Answer: \(z_1=2\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{3}+i\sin\frac{\pi}{3}\right)=2\,\mathrm{cis}\,\frac{\pi}{3}\) and \(z_2=2\,\mathrm{cis}\,\frac{\pi}{6}\). Check: \(2\cos\frac{\pi}{3}=2\left(\frac12\right)=1\) ✓ and \(2\sin\frac{\pi}{3}=2\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)=\sqrt3\) ✓.

Deriving the Product Rule from the Angle-Sum Identities

Multiply two trigonometric-form numbers directly and let Unit 7's own angle-sum identities do the simplifying:

\(r_1\,\mathrm{cis}\,\theta_1\cdot r_2\,\mathrm{cis}\,\theta_2=r_1r_2\big[(\cos\theta_1\cos\theta_2-\sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2)+i(\sin\theta_1\cos\theta_2+\cos\theta_1\sin\theta_2)\big]\)

The real part is exactly the expansion of \(\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)\); the imaginary part is exactly the expansion of \(\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\) — the same two identities Lesson 7-14 built from Ptolemy's theorem. So:

\(r_1\,\mathrm{cis}\,\theta_1\cdot r_2\,\mathrm{cis}\,\theta_2=r_1r_2\,\mathrm{cis}(\theta_1+\theta_2)\)

Multiply the moduli. Add the arguments. The rule is not a new fact about complex numbers to memorize — it is last quarter's trigonometry, arriving here fully paid for.

Example 2 — Multiplying, Two Ways at Once

Multiply \(z_1=1+i\sqrt3\) and \(z_2=\sqrt3+i\) from Example 1 using the trigonometric-form product rule, then verify the result by multiplying the original rectangular forms directly.

Solution:

Trigonometric form: \(z_1z_2=(2\,\mathrm{cis}\,\tfrac{\pi}{3})(2\,\mathrm{cis}\,\tfrac{\pi}{6})=(2\cdot2)\,\mathrm{cis}\left(\tfrac{\pi}{3}+\tfrac{\pi}{6}\right)=4\,\mathrm{cis}\,\tfrac{\pi}{2}=4\left(\cos\tfrac{\pi}{2}+i\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)=4(0+i)=4i\).

Direct rectangular multiplication: \((1+i\sqrt3)(\sqrt3+i)=\sqrt3+i+3i+i^2\sqrt3=\sqrt3+4i-\sqrt3=4i\).

Answer: \(z_1z_2=4i\), by both routes at once. ✓ The two methods do not almost agree — they agree exactly, because they were never two different facts, only two different paths to the same one.

Key Fact: The Quotient Rule

Dividing follows the same derivation in reverse (multiply numerator and denominator by \(\cos\theta_2-i\sin\theta_2\) and simplify with the angle-difference identities):

\(\dfrac{r_1\,\mathrm{cis}\,\theta_1}{r_2\,\mathrm{cis}\,\theta_2}=\dfrac{r_1}{r_2}\,\mathrm{cis}(\theta_1-\theta_2)\)

Divide the moduli. Subtract the arguments. Multiplication adds; division subtracts — the same relationship exponents have always had with each other.

Example 3 — Dividing, Two Ways at Once

Divide \(z_1=1+i\sqrt3\) by \(z_2=\sqrt3+i\) using the quotient rule, then verify by dividing the rectangular forms directly.

Solution:

Trigonometric form: \(\dfrac{z_1}{z_2}=\dfrac{2\,\mathrm{cis}\,\frac{\pi}{3}}{2\,\mathrm{cis}\,\frac{\pi}{6}}=\dfrac{2}{2}\,\mathrm{cis}\left(\tfrac{\pi}{3}-\tfrac{\pi}{6}\right)=1\cdot\mathrm{cis}\,\tfrac{\pi}{6}=\cos\tfrac{\pi}{6}+i\sin\tfrac{\pi}{6}=\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}+\dfrac{1}{2}i\).

Direct rectangular division (multiply by the conjugate of the denominator): \(\dfrac{1+i\sqrt3}{\sqrt3+i}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt3-i}{\sqrt3-i}=\dfrac{(1+i\sqrt3)(\sqrt3-i)}{3+1}=\dfrac{(\sqrt3+3i)-(i+i^2\sqrt3)}{4}=\dfrac{2\sqrt3+2i}{4}=\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}+\dfrac12i\).

Answer: \(\dfrac{z_1}{z_2}=\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}+\dfrac12i\), confirmed by both methods. ✓

💡 CHECK YOURSELF WITH DESMOS
At desmos.com/calculator, type \(z_1\) and \(z_2\) directly as ordered pairs — (1, sqrt(3)) and (sqrt(3), 1) — and plot (1*sqrt(3) - sqrt(3)*1, 1*1 + sqrt(3)*sqrt(3)), which is exactly the rectangular expansion of \(z_1z_2\). Confirm the plotted point lands at \((0,4)\), matching \(4i\) from Example 2.
▶  Try it yourself: Write \(z=-4+4i\) in trigonometric form.
\(r=\sqrt{(-4)^2+4^2}=\sqrt{32}=4\sqrt2\). \(\tan\theta=\frac{4}{-4}=-1\), reference angle \(\frac{\pi}{4}\); since \(x<0,y>0\) (Quadrant II), \(\theta=\pi-\frac{\pi}{4}=\frac{3\pi}{4}\). So \(z=4\sqrt2\,\mathrm{cis}\,\frac{3\pi}{4}\). Check: \(4\sqrt2\cos\frac{3\pi}{4}=4\sqrt2\left(-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)=-4\) ✓.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Swapping which operation goes with which quantity — multiplying the arguments and adding the moduli, backward from the rule. Say it in words before you touch a single number: multiplication makes distances multiply (a modulus of \(2\) times a modulus of \(2\) is a modulus of \(4\), not \(2+2=4\) by coincidence — check a case where they differ, like \(3\) and \(5\): the true modulus is \(15\), not \(8\)) and it makes rotations accumulate by addition (spin \(60^\circ\), then spin another \(30^\circ\), and you have spun a total of \(90^\circ\), not \(60^\circ\times30^\circ\)). Multiply the sizes; add the turns. Reversing that reverses the whole answer.

Conclusion

Trigonometric form did not just give the complex plane a second dictionary — it turned complex multiplication and division into arithmetic on distances and angles, exactly the arithmetic Unit 8 already made second nature. Today's rule handles a single multiplication or division. Tomorrow asks the natural next question: what happens when you multiply a complex number by itself, over and over — and, harder still, what number, multiplied by itself some fixed number of times, gets you back to where you started?

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does multiplying two trigonometric-form complex numbers multiply their moduli but add their arguments, rather than treating both quantities the same way?

2. Which specific identities from Lesson 7-14 make the product-rule derivation work, and why do the angle-difference versions of those same identities produce the quotient rule instead?

3. In Example 2, both the trigonometric-form product rule and direct rectangular multiplication produced exactly \(4i\). Why should you expect exact agreement rather than only approximate agreement?

4. Why does a negative modulus never appear in trigonometric form, when Unit 8 allowed a negative \(r\) in ordinary polar coordinates?
