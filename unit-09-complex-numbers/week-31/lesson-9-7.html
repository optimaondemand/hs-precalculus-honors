🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to move fluently among the rectangular, polar, and trigonometric forms of the same complex number, choosing whichever representation fits the question being asked.

  • I will be able to narrate, in my own words, the historical argument that eventually persuaded mathematicians to accept \(i\) as a genuine number rather than a fiction.

  • I will be able to evaluate an AI system’s worked root-finding solution, identify a planted error, and correct it with my own reasoning.

📚 VOCABULARY

Ars Magna: Girolamo Cardano’s 1545 book, the first to publish general solutions of the cubic and quartic equations — and the place where negative numbers under a square root sign first forced their way into a mathematician’s calculation.

Casus irreducibilis (“the irreducible case”): the situation in which Cardano’s cubic formula demands the square root of a negative number even though the cubic equation itself has three perfectly ordinary real solutions — the exact crack through which imaginary numbers first proved themselves useful.

Imaginary number: René Descartes’s dismissive seventeenth-century label (from his 1637 La Géométrie) for numbers like \(i\), coined as an insult implying they were mere fictions of the mind — a name that outlived the insult behind it by centuries.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In 1545, the Italian physician and mathematician Girolamo Cardano was working through a simple-sounding puzzle in his book Ars Magna: divide \(10\) into two parts whose product is \(40\). Ordinary algebra gives the two parts as \(5+\sqrt{-15}\) and \(5-\sqrt{-15}\) — and there Cardano stopped, staring at a square root of a negative number, a quantity every mathematician of his century agreed simply did not exist. He pressed on anyway, and in a passage often translated along the lines of “dismissing the mental tortures involved,” he multiplied the two expressions together as if they were ordinary numbers. \((5+\sqrt{-15})(5-\sqrt{-15})=25-(-15)=40\) — exactly the answer the problem demanded. The nonsense number had produced a perfectly sensible result, and Cardano, unnerved, called the whole maneuver “as subtle as it is useless” and moved on.

Three decades later, the engineer Rafael Bombelli refused to let the matter drop. Working through cubic equations that Cardano’s own formula could solve only by passing through these forbidden square roots — even for equations whose answers were plainly, uncontroversially real — Bombelli had what he called, in his own words, a “wild thought”: what if you simply computed with these expressions using consistent rules, the way you compute with any other number, and trusted the arithmetic to sort itself out?

Example 1 — Bombelli’s Wild Thought, Tested

The equation \(x^3=15x+4\) has \(x=4\) as an obvious real solution (check: \(4^3=64\) and \(15(4)+4=64\)). But Cardano’s formula for this equation returns \(x=\sqrt[3]{2+11i}+\sqrt[3]{2-11i}\) — a real answer, built entirely out of the square root of a negative number. Verify that Bombelli’s proposed cube roots, \(2+i\) and \(2-i\), actually work.

Solution:

\((2+i)^3=(2+i)^2(2+i)=(4+4i+i^2)(2+i)=(3+4i)(2+i)=6+3i+8i+4i^2=6+11i-4=2+11i\). ✓

By the identical computation with every sign on the imaginary part flipped, \((2-i)^3=2-11i\). ✓

Answer: \(\sqrt[3]{2+11i}+\sqrt[3]{2-11i}=(2+i)+(2-i)=4\) — Cardano’s formula, passed straight through the “impossible” square root of \(-121\), delivers the correct real answer \(4\) anyway. Bombelli’s wild thought worked: these forbidden numbers were not simply nonsense that happened to cancel — they were doing real, trustworthy arithmetic the entire time, whether or not anyone believed in them yet.

Belief came slowly and grudgingly. Descartes himself, in 1637, gave these numbers the name that stuck by way of an insult — imaginary, meaning existing only in the mind, not in reality. It took almost two more centuries, and the independent work of the Norwegian surveyor Caspar Wessel, the Swiss bookkeeper Jean-Robert Argand, and finally the towering authority of Carl Friedrich Gauss, to give these numbers what every other number already had: a place to stand. Plotting \(a+bi\) as the point \((a,b)\) — the move Unit 8 quietly assumed you already knew, and Lessons 9-5 and 9-6 spent the last two days re-describing in polar language — turned “imaginary” from an insult into simply a name nobody bothered to change once its sting had worn off.

One Number, Three Addresses

Consider \(z=-3+3i\).

Form Address Best for…
Rectangular \(-3+3i\) Adding and subtracting complex numbers.
Trigonometric (polar) \(3\sqrt2\,\mathrm{cis}\,135^\circ\) Multiplying, dividing, and raising to powers.
Roots (De Moivre) 4 fourth roots, spaced \(90^\circ\) apart Finding every number that raises to \(z\).

Example 2 — Rectangular to Trigonometric to a Power

Convert \(z=-3+3i\) to trigonometric form, then use De Moivre’s Theorem to compute \(z^4\). Verify by squaring twice directly in rectangular form.

Solution:

Trigonometric form: \(r=\sqrt{(-3)^2+3^2}=\sqrt{18}=3\sqrt2\). \(\tan\theta=\frac{3}{-3}=-1\), reference angle \(45^\circ\); since \(x<0,y>0\) (Quadrant II), \(\theta=180^\circ-45^\circ=135^\circ\). So \(z=3\sqrt2\,\mathrm{cis}\,135^\circ\).

De Moivre: \(z^4=(3\sqrt2)^4\,\mathrm{cis}(4\cdot135^\circ)=(9\cdot4)\,\mathrm{cis}\,540^\circ=324\,\mathrm{cis}\,180^\circ=324(-1+0i)=-324\).

Direct verification: \(z^2=(-3+3i)^2=9-18i+9i^2=9-18i-9=-18i\). \(z^4=(z^2)^2=(-18i)^2=324i^2=-324\).

Answer: \(z^4=-324\), confirmed by both methods. ✓ Trigonometric form did not merely offer an alternative route to this answer — it turned two rounds of rectangular squaring into one multiplication and one addition.

🤖 AI CHECKPOINT — FIND THE PLANTED ERROR

A student asked an AI tutor: “Use De Moivre’s Theorem to find all cube roots of \(-27\).” The AI answered:

“Write \(-27=27\,\mathrm{cis}\,180^\circ\). Then \(r^{1/3}=3\) and \(\theta/3=60^\circ\), so the cube root of \(-27\) is \(3\,\mathrm{cis}\,60^\circ=\dfrac32+\dfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}i\).”

Is the AI's answer correct? Every nonzero complex number has exactly \(n\) distinct \(n\)th roots — find every root of \(-27\) the AI's answer left out, and verify at least one of the missing roots by cubing it back. Then, in a sentence, name the exact step the AI skipped.

▶  See the fix: what did the AI's answer miss?
The AI computed only the \(k=0\) root. A complete answer needs \(z_k=3\,\mathrm{cis}\!\left(\frac{180^\circ+360^\circ k}{3}\right)\) for \(k=0,1,2\): \(z_0=3\,\mathrm{cis}\,60^\circ=\frac32+\frac{3\sqrt3}{2}i\) (the AI's root), \(z_1=3\,\mathrm{cis}\,180^\circ=-3\), and \(z_2=3\,\mathrm{cis}\,300^\circ=\frac32-\frac{3\sqrt3}{2}i\). Checking the AI's own root is not enough — it also has to be checked that no roots are missing. Cubing \(z_1=-3\): \((-3)^3=-27\). ✓ The AI never added \(360^\circ k\) before dividing by \(3\); it treated a three-root problem as if it only had one answer.
✍ IN YOUR OWN WORDS
In \(4\)–\(6\) sentences, tell the story of \(i\)'s legitimacy in your own words: what problem forced Cardano to write down a square root of a negative number in the first place, what did Bombelli's “wild thought” actually propose, and what did Wessel, Argand, and Gauss finally give these numbers that made the word “imaginary” obsolete? Close with one sentence connecting this history to the unit's virtue of courage — whose courage, and courage to do what, specifically?

Conclusion

A number that eighteenth-century mathematicians openly called impossible now has a rectangular form for adding, a trigonometric form for multiplying and dividing, and a complete theory of powers and roots that a real number alone could never have supplied — and every one of those tools traces back to a physician in 1545 who did the arithmetic anyway, and an engineer thirty years later who trusted a wild thought enough to test it. That is the shape courage takes in mathematics: not recklessness, but the willingness to compute honestly with an idea before you are sure it is allowed, and to let the arithmetic itself have the last word. Thursday's test gathers the whole unit — arithmetic, the plane, both forms, De Moivre's powers and roots — into one sitting. And the plane is not finished yet: next unit, magnitude and direction step off the complex plane entirely and into vectors, where a rotation is still a rotation, and courage gives way to a new virtue — service, magnitudes and directions cooperating toward a common resultant.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why did Cardano's own \(5\pm\sqrt{-15}\) computation trouble him even though it produced the correct answer, \(40\), when multiplied out?

2. What specifically made Bombelli's cubic example (Example 1) different from Cardano's — why couldn't Bombelli simply dismiss his own forbidden square root the way Cardano dismissed his?

3. In Example 2, which representation of \(z=-3+3i\) would you reach for to compute \(z^4\), and which would you reach for to compute \(z_1+z_2\) for some second complex number \(z_2\)? Why does no single representation win both jobs?

4. In your own words, what exact step did the AI checkpoint's response skip, and why does that step matter every single time an \(n\)th root is requested, not just for large \(n\)?
