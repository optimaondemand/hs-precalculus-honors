De Moivre’s Theorem and Roots (Honors Extension)
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to apply De Moivre’s Theorem to raise a trigonometric-form complex number to a whole-number power.

  • I will be able to find all \(n\) distinct \(n\)th roots of a complex number using De Moivre’s Theorem.

  • I will be able to explain why the \(n\) roots of a complex number are evenly spaced around a circle, forming the vertices of a regular \(n\)-gon.

  • I will be able to verify a computed root by raising it back to the \(n\)th power and confirming it reproduces the original number.

📚 VOCABULARY

De Moivre’s Theorem: for any positive integer \(n\), \((r\,\mathrm{cis}\,\theta)^n=r^n\,\mathrm{cis}(n\theta)\) — raise the modulus to the \(n\)th power and multiply the argument by \(n\).

\(n\)th root of a complex number: any complex number \(z\) satisfying \(z^n=w\) for a given \(w\). Every nonzero complex number has exactly \(n\) distinct \(n\)th roots — never fewer, never more.

Root index \(k\): the label \(k=0,1,2,\ldots,n-1\) distinguishing the \(n\) roots of \(w=r\,\mathrm{cis}\,\theta\) from one another, given by \(r^{1/n}\,\mathrm{cis}\!\left(\dfrac{\theta+2\pi k}{n}\right)\).

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Abraham de Moivre spent most of his working life in London as a private mathematics tutor, never quite landing the university post his talent deserved, but earning the friendship and admiration of Isaac Newton himself — Newton is said to have answered questions about his own Principia in later years by telling students, “go to Mr. de Moivre; he knows these things better than I do.” De Moivre spent decades studying the trigonometric identities that came from repeatedly multiplying a number by itself, and the pattern that now bears his name is one of the tidiest results in this entire course: it turns raising a complex number to a power — and, in reverse, extracting its roots — into simple arithmetic on a modulus and an argument.

De Moivre’s Theorem

Yesterday’s product rule said multiplying two trigonometric-form numbers multiplies their moduli and adds their arguments. Multiplying a number by itself, \(n\) times in a row, is just that same rule applied \(n-1\) times in succession — the modulus gets multiplied by itself \(n\) times (that is, raised to the \(n\)th power), and the argument gets added to itself \(n\) times (that is, multiplied by \(n\)):

\((r\,\mathrm{cis}\,\theta)^n=r^n\,\mathrm{cis}(n\theta)\)

No new machinery is required — De Moivre’s Theorem is yesterday’s product rule, run against itself.

Example 1 — A Power, Two Ways

Compute \((1+i)^8\) using De Moivre’s Theorem, then verify by repeated squaring in rectangular form.

Solution:

Trigonometric form: \(1+i=\sqrt2\,\mathrm{cis}\,\frac{\pi}{4}\) (since \(r=\sqrt{1^2+1^2}=\sqrt2\) and \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{4}\), Quadrant I). So \((1+i)^8=(\sqrt2)^8\,\mathrm{cis}\!\left(8\cdot\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=16\,\mathrm{cis}(2\pi)=16(\cos2\pi+i\sin2\pi)=16(1+0i)=16\).

Repeated squaring: \((1+i)^2=1+2i+i^2=2i\). \((1+i)^4=(2i)^2=4i^2=-4\). \((1+i)^8=(-4)^2=16\).

Answer: \((1+i)^8=16\), by both routes. ✓ A number with a nonzero imaginary part, raised to the eighth power, landed exactly on the real axis — because \(8\cdot\frac{\pi}{4}=2\pi\), a full trip around the circle.

Key Fact: Finding All \(n\) Roots

Run De Moivre’s Theorem in reverse. Since \(\mathrm{cis}\,\theta\) names the same point as \(\mathrm{cis}(\theta+2\pi k)\) for any integer \(k\), every nonzero \(w=r\,\mathrm{cis}\,\theta\) has exactly \(n\) distinct \(n\)th roots:

\(z_k=r^{1/n}\,\mathrm{cis}\!\left(\dfrac{\theta+2\pi k}{n}\right),\qquad k=0,1,2,\ldots,n-1\)

Every root shares the identical modulus \(r^{1/n}\) — so all \(n\) roots sit on one circle — and consecutive roots differ in argument by exactly \(\dfrac{2\pi}{n}\), spacing them evenly around that circle. Equal radius, equal spacing: the \(n\) roots of any complex number are, geometrically, nothing other than the \(n\) vertices of a regular \(n\)-gon.

Example 2 — All Three Cube Roots of \(-8\)

Find all three cube roots of \(-8\), and verify at least one non-trivial root by cubing it back.

Solution:

Write \(-8=8\,\mathrm{cis}\,\pi\) (\(r=8\), \(\theta=\pi\)). Then \(r^{1/3}=8^{1/3}=2\), and the three roots are \(z_k=2\,\mathrm{cis}\!\left(\dfrac{\pi+2\pi k}{3}\right)\) for \(k=0,1,2\):

\(k=0:\ \theta=\frac{\pi}{3}\ (60^\circ)\), giving \(z_0=2\left(\frac12+i\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)=1+i\sqrt3\).
\(k=1:\ \theta=\pi\ (180^\circ)\), giving \(z_1=2(-1+0i)=-2\).
\(k=2:\ \theta=\frac{5\pi}{3}\ (300^\circ)\), giving \(z_2=2\left(\frac12-i\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)=1-i\sqrt3\).

Verifying \(z_0\) by hand: \((1+i\sqrt3)^2=1+2i\sqrt3+i^2\cdot3=1+2i\sqrt3-3=-2+2i\sqrt3\). Then \((1+i\sqrt3)^3=(-2+2i\sqrt3)(1+i\sqrt3)=-2-2i\sqrt3+2i\sqrt3+2i^2\cdot3=-2-6=-8\). ✓

Answer: The three cube roots of \(-8\) are \(1+i\sqrt3\), \(-2\), and \(1-i\sqrt3\) — all three at distance \(2\) from the origin, at angles \(60^\circ\), \(180^\circ\), and \(300^\circ\), exactly \(120^\circ\) apart: the three vertices of an equilateral triangle inscribed in the circle of radius \(2\).

💡 CHECK YOURSELF WITH DESMOS
At desmos.com/calculator, plot the three points (1, sqrt(3)), (-2, 0), and (1, -sqrt(3)). Desmos will let you draw a circle of radius \(2\) centered at the origin through all three — confirm by eye that they form an equilateral triangle, exactly as the theorem predicts.
▶  Try it yourself: Find both square roots of \(i\).
\(i=1\,\mathrm{cis}\,\frac{\pi}{2}\), so the two roots are \(z_k=1\,\mathrm{cis}\!\left(\frac{\frac{\pi}{2}+2\pi k}{2}\right)\) for \(k=0,1\): \(z_0=\mathrm{cis}\,\frac{\pi}{4}=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}+\frac{\sqrt2}{2}i\), and \(z_1=\mathrm{cis}\,\frac{5\pi}{4}=-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}i\). Check: \(\left(\frac{\sqrt2}{2}+\frac{\sqrt2}{2}i\right)^2=\frac12+2\left(\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)\left(\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)i+\frac12i^2=\frac12+i-\frac12=i\). ✓
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Dividing \(\theta\) by \(n\) once and stopping — reporting a single root when the problem asked for all of them. \(\dfrac{\theta}{n}\) alone only ever recovers \(z_0\), the \(k=0\) case; a nonzero complex number always has exactly \(n\) distinct \(n\)th roots, and the other \(n-1\) come from adding \(2\pi k\) to \(\theta\) before dividing by \(n\), for every \(k\) from \(1\) up to \(n-1\). Skipping that step doesn’t give a wrong answer so much as an incomplete one — and an incomplete answer to “find all the roots” is still a wrong answer.

Conclusion

De Moivre’s Theorem closes the loop this unit opened: a number once ridiculed as impossible not only earned a place in the plane, it earned its own complete theory of powers and roots — roots that a real number alone could never fully supply. (Every positive real number has exactly one positive real square root, but ask for all its complex square roots and you get two; ask for all its complex cube roots and you get three, spaced like the vertices of a triangle.) Tomorrow gathers everything this unit has built — rectangular form, trigonometric form, and today’s powers and roots — into a single capstone, and tells the fuller story of how a number nobody wanted became a number nobody could do without.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does De Moivre’s Theorem raise the modulus to the \(n\)th power but only multiply the argument by \(n\), rather than also raising it to a power?

2. In Example 2, why must all three cube roots of \(-8\) lie on the exact same circle, even though only one of the three is a real number?

3. If a complex number has exactly \(n\) distinct \(n\)th roots, why does a positive real number like \(4\) still seem to have only one square root, \(2\), in ordinary arithmetic?

4. Explain, in your own words, why forgetting to add \(2\pi k\) before dividing by \(n\) produces an incomplete answer rather than a wrong one — what is true about the one root that IS found?
