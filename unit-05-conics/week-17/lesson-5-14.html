Eccentricity: One Family, One Number (Honors Extension)
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to compute the eccentricity of any conic from its equation, and predict a conic's type from its eccentricity alone.

  • I will be able to explain how the focus–directrix property makes circle, ellipse, parabola, and hyperbola one family, sorted by a single number.

  • I will be able to interpret eccentricity as shape — from Earth's nearly circular orbit to a comet's long ellipse — and compute orbital extremes from \(a\) and \(e\).

  • I will be able to derive a conic's equation directly from a focus, a directrix, and an eccentricity, and verify the result by completing the square.

📚 VOCABULARY

Eccentricity: The number \(e\) that measures a conic's shape — for ellipses and hyperbolas, \(e = \frac{c}{a}\), the distance to the focus as a fraction of the distance to the vertex. From Latin ex centro, "out of center": how far the focus has strayed from the middle.

Focus–directrix property: The unifying definition — a conic is the set of points whose distance to a fixed focus is exactly \(e\) times their distance to a fixed line, the directrix.

Directrix (generalized): Until now a private possession of the parabola; today, every non-circular conic gets one (ellipses and hyperbolas get two, one per focus).

Semi-major axis: The distance \(a\) from an ellipse's center to a vertex — in orbital language, the orbit's average scale, partnered with \(e\) to give perihelion \(a(1-e)\) and aphelion \(a(1+e)\).

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

The names you have used all unit are twenty-two centuries old, and they hide today's lesson inside them. When Apollonius of Perga organized the conics around 200 BC, he named them with the Greek of comparison: elleipsis, "falling short"; parabolē, "exact application," a perfect match; hyperbolē, "excess," an overshooting (the same word English borrowed for exaggeration). He was describing a comparison of areas in his own construction — but the names turned out to be prophecy. There is a single number attached to every conic, its eccentricity \(e\), and the whole family sorts itself by exactly Apollonius's trichotomy: falling short of 1, equal to 1, exceeding 1. Eighteen centuries later, Johannes Kepler — the man who coined the word focus (Latin for "hearth") in 1604 — saw what the number meant physically, and argued that the conics form one continuous family: nudge an ellipse's eccentricity upward and it stretches, at \(e = 1\) it breaks open into a parabola, and past 1 it splits into a hyperbola. One family. One number. Today you learn to read it.

The Number and the Trichotomy

For an ellipse or hyperbola with center-to-vertex distance \(a\) and center-to-focus distance \(c\):

\(e = \dfrac{c}{a}\)

For the ellipse, \(c^2 = a^2 - b^2\), so \(c < a\) and \(0 \le e < 1\). For the hyperbola, \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\), so \(c > a\) and \(e > 1\). The parabola takes the boundary value \(e = 1\), and the circle is the ellipse with its foci collapsed to the center: \(c = 0\), \(e = 0\).

  • \(e = 0\) — circle (the focus sits dead center)
  • \(0 < e < 1\) — ellipse (falling short — elleipsis)
  • \(e = 1\) — parabola (the exact boundary — parabolē)
  • \(e > 1\) — hyperbola (excess — hyperbolē)

The deeper unity is the focus–directrix property: every non-circular conic is the set of points \(P\) with \(\dfrac{PF}{Pd} = e\), where \(PF\) is the distance to a focus and \(Pd\) the distance to a fixed line. The parabola you derived as a locus in Lesson 5-3 was the case \(e = 1\), where the two distances match exactly — you have been using this property all along without knowing it was a family trait. (The circle joins the family as the limiting case \(e \to 0\), its directrix retreating to infinity as the focus settles into the center — a limit, and an honest one.)

Example 1

Two ellipses share the same width: \(\dfrac{x^2}{25} + \dfrac{y^2}{9} = 1\) and \(\dfrac{x^2}{25} + \dfrac{y^2}{24} = 1\). Compute each eccentricity and compare the shapes.

Solution:

Both have \(a^2 = 25\), so \(a = 5\). For the first: \(c^2 = a^2 - b^2 = 25 - 9 = 16\), so \(c = 4\) and \(e = \frac{4}{5} = 0.8\). For the second: \(c^2 = 25 - 24 = 1\), so \(c = 1\) and \(e = \frac{1}{5} = 0.2\).

Answer: Same \(a\), utterly different characters. At \(e = 0.8\) the foci sit 80% of the way to the vertices and the ellipse is a drawn-out oval; at \(e = 0.2\) they huddle near the center and the curve is nearly a circle — you would need a ruler to distinguish it from one by eye. Eccentricity measures shape, not size: a dinner plate and a stadium can share \(e\), while these two same-width ellipses do not.

Example 2

Find the eccentricity of the hyperbola \(\dfrac{x^2}{9} - \dfrac{y^2}{16} = 1\), and explain why no hyperbola can have \(e \le 1\).

Solution:

Here \(a^2 = 9\) and \(b^2 = 16\), and the hyperbola's Pythagorean relation is the sum: \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2 = 9 + 16 = 25\), so \(c = 5\) and

\(e = \dfrac{c}{a} = \dfrac{5}{3} \approx 1.67\)

Why must this exceed 1? Because \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\) with \(b^2 > 0\) forces \(c > a\) for every hyperbola ever written — the foci live beyond the vertices by construction. The bound is not a habit of examples; it is a consequence of the definition.

Answer: \(e = \frac{5}{3}\). And the bound works as a free error detector: if a computation ever hands you a "hyperbola" with \(e < 1\) or an "ellipse" with \(e > 1\), the classification itself is telling you an arithmetic crime has occurred upstream. Let it testify.

Example 3

Halley's comet rides an ellipse with semi-major axis \(a = 17.8\) AU and eccentricity \(e = 0.967\), the sun at one focus. Find its closest and farthest distances from the sun.

Solution:

The sun sits at a focus, a distance \(c = ae\) from the center. The closest approach (perihelion) is vertex-to-focus, and the farthest (aphelion) is the opposite vertex to that same focus:

\(\text{perihelion} = a - c = a(1 - e) = 17.8(1 - 0.967) = 17.8(0.033) \approx 0.59 \text{ AU}\)

\(\text{aphelion} = a + c = a(1 + e) = 17.8(1.967) \approx 35.01 \text{ AU}\)

Answer: The comet swings inside Venus's orbit (about 0.59 AU, closer to the sun than Earth ever goes) and then retreats beyond Neptune (about 35 AU) — a sixty-fold swing in distance encoded in the single fact \(e = 0.967\). Compare Earth: \(e = 0.0167\), an orbit so nearly circular that drawn to scale on this page, your eye could not tell. One number separates a home from an odyssey.

Example 4

(Honors derivation.) Find the conic with focus at the origin, directrix \(x = 4\), and eccentricity \(e = \tfrac{1}{2}\) — predicting its type first, then deriving and verifying its equation.

Solution:

Predict: \(e = \tfrac{1}{2} < 1\): the trichotomy promises an ellipse before we lift a pencil.

Derive. A point \(P(x, y)\) belongs exactly when \(PF = \tfrac{1}{2} \, Pd\):

\(\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,|x - 4|\)

\(x^2 + y^2 = \tfrac{1}{4}(x - 4)^2 = \tfrac{1}{4}\left(x^2 - 8x + 16\right)\)

\(4x^2 + 4y^2 = x^2 - 8x + 16 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; 3x^2 + 8x + 4y^2 = 16\)

Now the master key of the unit — complete the square:

\(3\left(x^2 + \tfrac{8}{3}x\right) + 4y^2 = 16 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; 3\left(x + \tfrac{4}{3}\right)^2 - \tfrac{16}{3} + 4y^2 = 16\)

\(3\left(x + \tfrac{4}{3}\right)^2 + 4y^2 = \tfrac{64}{3} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \dfrac{\left(x + \frac{4}{3}\right)^2}{64/9} + \dfrac{y^2}{16/3} = 1\)

Verify against the promise. An ellipse indeed, with \(a^2 = \tfrac{64}{9}\) and \(b^2 = \tfrac{16}{3}\), so \(a = \tfrac{8}{3}\) and \(c^2 = a^2 - b^2 = \tfrac{64}{9} - \tfrac{48}{9} = \tfrac{16}{9}\), giving \(c = \tfrac{4}{3}\). Then \(e = \frac{c}{a} = \frac{4/3}{8/3} = \tfrac{1}{2}\) ✓ — and the center is \(\left(-\tfrac{4}{3}, 0\right)\), so the right focus sits at \(-\tfrac{4}{3} + \tfrac{4}{3} = 0\): the origin, exactly where we posted it ✓.

Answer: \(\dfrac{\left(x + \frac{4}{3}\right)^2}{64/9} + \dfrac{y^2}{16/3} = 1\). One locus condition in, one completed square later, out comes an ellipse wearing precisely the \(e\) and the focus it was ordered with. The focus–directrix definition is not a curiosity — it manufactures conics to specification, and completing the square reads the label.

Reading \(e\) at a Glance

  • Ellipse: as \(e \to 0\) the foci sink into the center and the curve rounds toward a circle; as \(e \to 1\) the foci flee toward the vertices and the ellipse stretches long and thin. Planets live near 0; comets near 1.
  • Parabola: \(e = 1\), always and exactly — every parabola has the same shape, differing only in scale. There is no such thing as a "rounder" parabola, only a closer one.
  • Hyperbola: for a fixed \(a\), larger \(e\) means larger \(b\) and steeper asymptotes — branches that open wider. Just past \(e = 1\), the branches hug the axis, nearly parabolic; at great \(e\) they flatten toward a pair of crossed lines.

Shape, not size. Two conics with equal \(e\) are magnifications of one another — which is why a single number can describe both a whispering gallery and a planetary orbit.

⚠️ AI CHECKPOINT: THE IMPOSSIBLE ECCENTRICITY

A student asked an AI for the eccentricity of the hyperbola \(\dfrac{x^2}{16} - \dfrac{y^2}{9} = 1\). It answered:

“Here a² = 16 and b² = 9, so c² = a² − b² = 16 − 9 = 7, giving c = √7. The eccentricity is e = √7/4 ≈ 0.66.”

You do not need to redo the arithmetic to know this is wrong — the answer convicts itself. The equation is a hyperbola (that minus sign between the squared terms is its signature), and every hyperbola has \(e > 1\); an eccentricity of 0.66 belongs to an ellipse. The machine reached for the ellipse's relation \(c^2 = a^2 - b^2\) when the hyperbola demands the sum: \(c^2 = 16 + 9 = 25\), \(c = 5\), and \(e = \frac{5}{4}\) — safely in excess of 1, as Apollonius's name promised.

This is the lesson's bound working as a smoke detector: the classification limits (\(e < 1\) ellipse, \(e > 1\) hyperbola) cost nothing to check and catch a whole species of formula mix-ups. Before you accept any eccentricity — yours, a book's, or a machine's — ask the one free question: is this number even legal for this curve?

▶  Try it yourself: Compute the eccentricity of \(\frac{x^2}{49} + \frac{y^2}{33} = 1\), and say in one sentence what the number tells you about the shape.
\(a^2 = 49\), \(b^2 = 33\): \(c^2 = 49 - 33 = 16\), so \(c = 4\) and \(e = \frac{4}{7} \approx 0.57\). A middling ellipse — visibly oval (the foci sit a bit past halfway to the vertices), but nowhere near the drawn-out shape of a comet's \(e \approx 0.97\).
▶  Try it yourself: Find the eccentricity of \(\frac{y^2}{4} - \frac{x^2}{21} = 1\); then classify sight-unseen the conics with \(e = 0\), \(e = 0.31\), \(e = 1\), and \(e = 2.4\).
Hyperbola with vertical transverse axis: \(a^2 = 4\), \(b^2 = 21\), so \(c^2 = 4 + 21 = 25\), \(c = 5\), and \(e = \frac{5}{2}\) — legal, since \(2.5 > 1\). Classifications: \(e = 0\) circle; \(e = 0.31\) ellipse (modestly oval); \(e = 1\) parabola, exactly; \(e = 2.4\) hyperbola with wide-opening branches.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Using \(c^2 = a^2 - b^2\) on a hyperbola. The ellipse subtracts because its foci live inside, short of the vertices; the hyperbola adds — \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\) — because its foci live beyond them. Mix the two and your eccentricity lands on the wrong side of 1, claiming an impossible curve. The remedy is the free check from the AI box: an ellipse's \(e\) must fall short of 1 and a hyperbola's must exceed it — Apollonius put the rule in the names, so let the name audit the number every time.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Kepler found the ellipse the hard way. Working through Tycho Brahe's observations of Mars — eccentricity just 0.093, an orbit your eye would call a circle — he found the best circular model missed by a mere eight minutes of arc, about a quarter of the full moon's width. A lesser astronomer would have shrugged. Kepler trusted the data over the two-thousand-year-old assumption of perfect circles, fought what he called his "war with Mars" for years, and in 1609 announced that planets ride ellipses with the sun at one focus. The whole heliocentric revolution turned on an eccentricity barely distinguishable from zero — and on one man's refusal to round it off.

Conclusion

The unit began with a plane slicing a cone into four apparently different curves; today the four closed ranks into one family, sorted by one number. Eccentricity measures how far the focus has strayed from the center — \(e = 0\) a circle, short of 1 an ellipse, exactly 1 a parabola, in excess a hyperbola — and Apollonius's twenty-two-century-old names turn out to describe precisely this trichotomy. You watched the whole family morph on a single Desmos slider, manufactured an ellipse to specification from a focus, a directrix, and the number \(\frac{1}{2}\), and let the classification bounds catch a machine holding the wrong formula. Tomorrow the family goes to work: the unit's capstone hands you an engineering brief and asks you to choose the right conic and defend the choice in writing, with your computed evidence on the table. Every curve in the family serves something; tomorrow you argue which servant the job requires.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Apollonius named the conics for "falling short," "exact," and "excess" centuries before eccentricity was defined. What exactly does each name fall short of or exceed — and why is it satisfying that the names still fit?

2. Why does \(c^2 = a^2 - b^2\) force every ellipse's eccentricity below 1, while \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\) forces every hyperbola's above it? What geometric fact about the foci do the two formulas encode?

3. Eccentricity measures shape and ignores size. Why is that separation exactly what an astronomer needs — and what second number completes the description of an orbit?

4. In the Desmos exploration, the parabola existed for only a single instant of the slider's journey. What does that fragility say about why parabolic orbits are the rarest kind in the solar system?
