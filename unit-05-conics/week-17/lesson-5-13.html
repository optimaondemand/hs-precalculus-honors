Conics and Systems

🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to solve systems involving conics both graphically and algebraically, choosing substitution or elimination deliberately rather than by habit.

  • I will be able to determine and defend the number of intersection points a system has — using guards, the discriminant, and the geometry of the curves.

  • I will be able to interpret intersections in context: where an orbit crosses another orbit, and where a path enters and leaves a radar range circle.

📚 VOCABULARY

Nonlinear system: A system of equations in which at least one equation is not a line — today, systems in which one or both equations are conics.

Substitution: The Unit 3 method of solving a variable (or a whole expression such as \(x^2\)) from one equation and installing it in the other. Still the workhorse.

Elimination: Adding or subtracting multiples of the equations so that a variable's terms cancel. With conics, it is often a squared term that gets eliminated.

Tangent: Touching at exactly one point. Algebraically, a tangency is a repeated root — the moment two intersection points merge into one.

Discriminant: The quantity \(b^2 - 4ac\) under the quadratic formula's radical. Positive, zero, or negative, it counts the real crossings before you find a single one.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Every August, the night sky over Florida streaks with the Perseid meteor shower — and the reason it arrives on schedule is a system of conics. Comet Swift–Tuttle rides an immense ellipse around the sun; Earth rides a much smaller, rounder one. The two ellipses cross, and the comet has littered its path with dust. Once a year our planet reaches the intersection point of the two orbits and plows through the debris: the shooting stars are the solution set of a system, arriving punctually because the intersection point does not move. Last week you saw the same idea serve sailors — a LORAN receiver located a ship at the point where two hyperbolas crossed. Curves that cross are curves that answer questions. Today we learn to find those crossings exactly.

The tools are not new. In Unit 3 you built the systems machinery — substitution and elimination, with graphs as the picture of the solution set — and solved your first nonlinear systems. That machinery returns today, welcome and unchanged. What is new is the cast of curves it acts on: circles, parabolas, ellipses, and hyperbolas, every one of which you can now write, graph, and read like a native. A solution of a system is still exactly what it was in Unit 3: a point that satisfies every equation at once — a point where the graphs genuinely meet.

Example 1

A harbor radar station sits at the origin and detects ships within 5 miles: its range boundary is the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 25\). A freighter sails the straight course \(y = x + 1\). Where does the freighter cross the range boundary?

Solution:

The line hands us \(y\) already isolated — the signal for substitution. Install \(y = x + 1\) in the circle:

\(x^2 + (x+1)^2 = 25\)

\(2x^2 + 2x + 1 = 25\)

\(x^2 + x - 12 = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; (x+4)(x-3) = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x = -4 \;\text{or}\; x = 3\)

Each \(x\) returns to the line for its partner: \(x = -4\) gives \(y = -3\); \(x = 3\) gives \(y = 4\). Check both points in the circle, because a solution must satisfy every equation: \((-4)^2 + (-3)^2 = 16 + 9 = 25\) ✓ and \(3^2 + 4^2 = 9 + 16 = 25\) ✓.

Answer: The freighter crosses the boundary at \((-4, -3)\) and \((3, 4)\) — entering radar coverage at one point and leaving it at the other. Between those crossings, the whole course lies inside the circle; the crossings are precisely where "visible" becomes "not."

The Machinery Returns: Choosing Your Weapon

Unit 3 gave you two methods; the conic family tells you which to draw.

  • Substitution when one equation offers a variable (or an expression like \(x^2\)) already isolated or easily isolated — a line paired with any conic, or a parabola \(y = \ldots\) paired with a circle.
  • Elimination when both equations carry matching squared terms — an ellipse and a hyperbola both holding \(x^2\) and \(y^2\) practically beg you to subtract.

And the standing law, unchanged since Unit 3: a solution must satisfy every equation in the system. Finish each candidate by checking it in the equation you did not use to produce it — that check is where impostors are caught.

Example 2

Solve the system \(x^2 + y^2 = 20\) and \(y = x^2\).

Solution:

The parabola offers \(x^2 = y\) whole — substitute the expression, not just a variable:

\(y + y^2 = 20\)

\(y^2 + y - 20 = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; (y+5)(y-4) = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; y = -5 \;\text{or}\; y = 4\)

Post the guard before going further. On the parabola, \(y = x^2 \geq 0\): no point of this curve has a negative height. The candidate \(y = -5\) fails the guard — it is a ghost of exactly the species you convicted in Unit 3's radical equations, produced by algebra that cannot know the curve's territory. Discard it with a written reason. The survivor \(y = 4\) gives \(x^2 = 4\), so \(x = 2\) or \(x = -2\) — both roots, always both roots.

Check in the circle: \((\pm 2)^2 + 4^2 = 4 + 16 = 20\) ✓.

Answer: \((2, 4)\) and \((-2, 4)\). Two curves, two crossings, one ghost turned away at the border. The algebra proposed three values; the geometry seated two points.

Example 3

Two orbits share a plane: an ellipse \(x^2 + 4y^2 = 25\) and a hyperbola \(x^2 - y^2 = 5\). Find every point the paths share.

Solution:

Both equations hold \(x^2\) with coefficient 1 — the signal for elimination. Subtract the hyperbola from the ellipse:

\((x^2 + 4y^2) - (x^2 - y^2) = 25 - 5\)

\(5y^2 = 20 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; y^2 = 4 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; y = 2 \;\text{or}\; y = -2\)

Then from the hyperbola, \(x^2 = 5 + y^2 = 5 + 4 = 9\), so \(x = 3\) or \(x = -3\) — for each value of \(y\). Every sign pairing is a candidate; check one in the ellipse and symmetry certifies the rest: \(3^2 + 4(2)^2 = 9 + 16 = 25\) ✓.

Answer: Four points: \((3, 2)\), \((3, -2)\), \((-3, 2)\), \((-3, -2)\). Both curves are symmetric across both axes, so the crossings arrive in a full choir of four — and losing even one of them means losing a place where the orbits genuinely meet. When you take a square root inside a system, write both roots or write an error.

How Many Crossings Are Even Possible?

You can bound the answer before you touch the algebra — and the bound is your best defense of a count.

  • A line and a conic meet in at most 2 points. Substituting a line into a second-degree equation produces a quadratic, and a quadratic has at most two real roots. The discriminant referees: positive → 2 crossings, zero → 1 (a tangency, two crossings merged), negative → 0.
  • Two distinct conics meet in at most 4 points. Two second-degree equations combine into an equation of degree at most four — Example 3 seated the full house of four.

So a claimed count of five is impossible, a claimed count of three is rare and special (it needs a tangency), and every count you report should be able to cite either a discriminant or a guard as its witness.

Example 4

(a) Show that the course \(y = x + 7\) never enters the range circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 9\). (b) Then find the values of \(c\) for which the course \(y = x + c\) is exactly tangent to the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 25\).

Solution:

(a) Substitute: \(x^2 + (x+7)^2 = 9\), so \(2x^2 + 14x + 49 = 9\), giving \(2x^2 + 14x + 40 = 0\), or \(x^2 + 7x + 20 = 0\). Discriminant: \(7^2 - 4(1)(20) = 49 - 80 = -31 < 0\). No real roots, no crossings — the ship passes entirely outside detection, and we know it without finding a single point.

(b) Substitute \(y = x + c\) into \(x^2 + y^2 = 25\):

\(2x^2 + 2cx + (c^2 - 25) = 0\)

Tangency is a repeated root — discriminant zero:

\((2c)^2 - 4(2)(c^2 - 25) = 4c^2 - 8c^2 + 200 = 200 - 4c^2 = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; c^2 = 50 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; c = \pm 5\sqrt{2}\)

Geometry corroborates: the line \(y = x + c\) is tangent to a circle of radius 5 exactly when its distance from the center equals 5, and that distance is \(\tfrac{|c|}{\sqrt{2}}\) — set it equal to 5 and again \(|c| = 5\sqrt{2}\). Two roads, one answer.

Answer: (a) Zero intersections, certified by a negative discriminant. (b) \(c = 5\sqrt{2}\) or \(c = -5\sqrt{2}\) — the two positions where a 2-crossing course collapses to a single grazing touch. The discriminant is not a formula fragment; it is a census-taker.

💻 In Desmos: Count Before You Compute 8 min

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), put Examples 1 and 4 under the lights:

  1. Graph x^2 + y^2 = 25 and y = x + 1. Click each intersection: \((-4,-3)\) and \((3,4)\), exactly as the algebra ruled.
  2. Now replace the line with y = x + c and add a slider for \(c\). Drag it upward and watch the census change live: two crossings, then — at one perfect instant — a single grazing touch, then none.
  3. Park the slider where the touch happens and read the value: \(c \approx 7.071\). Your algebra said \(5\sqrt{2}\); compute it — \(5\sqrt{2} \approx 7.0711\). The slider found the tangency by eye; the discriminant found it exactly.
  4. Finally graph Example 3's pair — x^2 + 4y^2 = 25 and x^2 - y^2 = 5 — and count the four crossings on screen. The calculator confirms; your algebra convinces.

⚠️ AI CHECKPOINT: THE PHANTOM CROSSINGS

A student asked an AI how many points the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 6\) and the parabola \(y = x^2\) share. It answered:

“Substituting gives y² + y − 6 = 0, so (y + 3)(y − 2) = 0, giving y = 2 and y = −3. For y = 2, x = ±√2; for y = −3, x = ±i√3. The curves intersect at four points.”

Audit it. The factoring is flawless, and \(x = \pm i\sqrt{3}\) really does satisfy both equations — as algebra. But an intersection point is a point where the graphs meet, and graphs live in the real plane: a point with coordinate \(i\sqrt{3}\) has no address there. The guard \(y = x^2 \geq 0\) had already barred \(y = -3\) from the parabola. The true census: \((\sqrt{2}, 2)\) and \((-\sqrt{2}, 2)\) — check: \(2 + 4 = 6\) ✓ — two points, not four.

Name the failure precisely: the machine did honest algebra and then miscounted what the algebra was counting — solutions of equations are not automatically points of graphs. When you defend an intersection count on this week's work and on Thursday's test, say not only how many, but how many real, and produce the guard or discriminant that turned the others away.

▶  Try it yourself: Solve the system \(x^2 + y^2 = 13\) and \(y = x - 1\), checking every point in both equations. Work it on paper before you peek.
Substitute: \(x^2 + (x-1)^2 = 13\) gives \(2x^2 - 2x + 1 = 13\), so \(x^2 - x - 6 = 0\), and \((x-3)(x+2) = 0\): \(x = 3\) or \(x = -2\). From the line: \((3, 2)\) and \((-2, -3)\). Circle check: \(9 + 4 = 13\) ✓ and \(4 + 9 = 13\) ✓. Two crossings, both genuine.
▶  Try it yourself: How many points do the ellipse \(\frac{x^2}{4} + y^2 = 1\) and the line \(y = x + 3\) share? Certify your count with a discriminant.
Substitute and clear fractions: \(x^2 + 4(x+3)^2 = 4\) gives \(5x^2 + 24x + 32 = 0\). Discriminant: \(24^2 - 4(5)(32) = 576 - 640 = -64 < 0\). Zero intersections — the line passes above the ellipse entirely, and the negative discriminant is the certificate. (Sketch it: the ellipse never rises above \(y = 1\), while the line is already at height 3 when \(x = 0\).)
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Writing \(y^2 = 4\), then \(y = 2\) — and moving on. Inside a system this does not merely lose a number; it loses points: in Example 3 it would silently erase \((3,-2)\) and \((-3,-2)\), reporting two crossings where four exist. Every square root taken inside a system produces two candidates, and every candidate must be paired with every partner value and judged. The symmetric curves of this unit almost always seat their solutions in symmetric sets — if your answer breaks the symmetry of the picture, the picture is telling you what you dropped.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The GPS receiver in a phone runs a cousin of today's lesson many times a second. Each satellite's signal says "you are at such-and-such distance from me" — the three-dimensional version of a range circle — and the receiver's position is the intersection of those spheres, found by solving the system. LORAN located ships at the crossing of two hyperbolas; GPS locates you at the crossing of spheres. Navigation has always been the art of intersecting loci — only the curves have changed.

Conclusion

The systems machinery of Unit 3 came back today and met the full conic family: substitution when an expression stood ready, elimination when squared terms matched, and — new muscle on old bones — a defended count. A line meets a conic at most twice, two conics at most four times, and every count you claim can produce its witness: a discriminant that tallied the crossings, or a guard that turned a phantom away. You watched a tangency emerge on a slider and then pinned it exactly at \(c = 5\sqrt{2}\), and you caught an AI counting complex numbers as places in the plane. Tomorrow the unit turns from where conics cross to what makes them kin: a single number, the eccentricity, that sorts every curve of this unit into one family — and tells you which member you are holding before you complete a single square.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why can a line never meet an ellipse in three points — and what does the degree of an equation have to do with the geometry of crossings?

2. In Example 2 the algebra produced \(y = -5\) and the curve refused it. How is that refusal the same discipline as the guard-and-judge method you learned for radical equations?

3. What exactly does a zero discriminant look like on a graph, and why is it fair to call a tangency "two intersections standing in the same place"?

4. The Perseids return every August; a GPS fix updates every second. What do both owe to the fact that the intersection of two fixed curves is itself fixed?
