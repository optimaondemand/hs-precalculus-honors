Conics Capstone: Defending a Design
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to select the conic that meets an engineering brief and derive its equation from the brief's stated constraints, with my coordinate system declared first.

  • I will be able to defend the selection in writing with the four-line model defense — claim, structure, long-run, domain of honesty — citing computed evidence, not preference.

  • I will be able to anticipate the strongest rival conic for a given job and refute it with a geometric property.

📚 VOCABULARY

Engineering brief: The client's statement of the job — dimensions, functions, constraints. The mathematician's first task is translating it into coordinates.

Constraint: A condition the design must satisfy — "the dish is 1.2 m across," "the emitter and the stone are 40 cm apart." Each constraint becomes an equation your curve must obey.

Focal length: The distance \(p\) from a parabola's vertex to its focus — for a reflector, the exact altitude at which the receiver must hang.

Model defense: A short written argument that a chosen model fits its job — the four-line discipline you first practiced in Unit 2, now applied to geometry: claim, structure, long-run, domain of honesty.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Apollonius wrote eight books on the conics with no machine in view — pure curves, studied because they were beautiful and true. Eighteen centuries later Kepler found the planets riding them. Seven decades after that, in 1672, a French designer named Laurent Cassegrain proposed a telescope that put two conics to work in one tube: a parabolic mirror to catch starlight and send it toward a focus, and a small hyperbolic mirror, sharing that very focus, to relay the light back through the primary to an eyepiece. The design still flies — a version of it rode aboard the Hubble Space Telescope. This is the arc of the whole unit, and of its virtue: curves conceived in wonder, refined into service. Today, the unit's capstone: you become the engineer. A brief arrives, you choose the conic, derive its equation, and — the honors heart of the task — defend the choice in writing, with the mathematics as your evidence.

The Service Properties: Which Curve Does Which Job

Every conic earns its keep with a geometric property no other curve possesses. This table is the engineer's menu — and the source of every defense you will write.

  • Circle — constant distance from the center. Serves: range boundaries, wheels, arches under uniform load.
  • Parabola — reflects rays parallel to its axis through the focus, and vice versa. Serves: satellite dishes, headlights, solar troughs, radio telescopes. One favored point.
  • Ellipse — reflects anything leaving one focus to the other focus. Serves: whispering galleries, lithotripsy (shattering kidney stones with focused shock waves), planetary orbits. Two favored points.
  • Hyperbola — a ray aimed at one focus reflects toward the other; constant difference of distances. Serves: Cassegrain telescopes, LORAN navigation — and cooling towers, whose hyperbolic profile can be built from straight steel lines and stands with famous strength.

Notice what the menu is really listing: how many favored points the job needs. Parallel-to-one-point is the parabola's monopoly; point-to-point is the ellipse's; point-past-point is the hyperbola's. Match the job's geometry, not your favorite curve.

Example 1

The brief: A satellite dish must be 8 feet across and 1.5 feet deep, with the receiver mounted at the correct height above the vertex. Design it — and defend it.

Solution:

Declare coordinates first. Set the vertex at the origin, axis vertical: the cross-section is \(x^2 = 4py\) for some focal length \(p > 0\).

Translate the constraints. "8 feet across, 1.5 feet deep" means the rim passes through the point \((4, 1.5)\) — half the width, full depth. Install it:

\(4^2 = 4p(1.5)\)

\(16 = 6p \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; p = \tfrac{8}{3} \approx 2.67 \text{ ft}\)

The equation is \(x^2 = \tfrac{32}{3}y\), and the receiver hangs at the focus, \(\left(0, \tfrac{8}{3}\right)\) — about 2 ft 8 in above the vertex. Verify against the brief: at \(y = 1.5\), \(x^2 = \tfrac{32}{3}(1.5) = 16\), so \(x = \pm 4\) — the rim is 8 ft across ✓.

Now the defense — four lines, the genre you will write on the Focus set:

Claim: the cross-section is the parabola \(x^2 = \frac{32}{3}y\), receiver at the focus \((0, \frac{8}{3})\). Structure: satellite signals arrive as rays effectively parallel to the axis, and only the parabola reflects every such ray through one point — the property is the job description. Long-run: the property holds across the entire 8-ft aperture, rim to rim — not approximately near the center, but exactly, from the locus definition. Domain of honesty: the model is the ideal cross-section on \(-4 \le x \le 4\); it ignores panel thickness, wind load, and pointing error, and it says nothing beyond the rim — where the dish itself stops existing.

Answer: \(x^2 = \tfrac{32}{3}y\), focus at \(\left(0, \tfrac{8}{3}\right)\). The commonest design fatality happened silently in step two and was refused: the rim point is \((4, 1.5)\), never \((8, 1.5)\). Width is a diameter; the equation eats a radius.

Example 2

The brief: A lithotripter — the medical device that shatters kidney stones without surgery — needs a reflector chamber in which a shock-wave emitter and the stone sit 40 cm apart, with the chamber's half-depth limited to 15 cm. Design the reflector, and refute the rival.

Solution:

Choose the curve from the job's geometry. Energy must leave one point and arrive at another point: two favored points — the ellipse's monopoly. Center the ellipse at the origin, foci on the \(x\)-axis: emitter at one focus, stone at the other.

Translate the constraints. Foci 40 cm apart: \(2c = 40\), so \(c = 20\). Half-depth 15 cm is the semi-minor axis: \(b = 15\). The ellipse's relation supplies the rest:

\(a^2 = b^2 + c^2 = 225 + 400 = 625 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; a = 25\)

\(\dfrac{x^2}{625} + \dfrac{y^2}{225} = 1, \qquad e = \dfrac{c}{a} = \dfrac{20}{25} = 0.8\)

The chamber is \(2a = 50\) cm long, and — the ellipse's gift — every reflected path from focus to focus has the same total length \(2a = 50\) cm, so all the shock waves arrive in step, their energy concentrated at one point of tissue and nowhere else.

Refute the rival. Why not a parabola, the other famous reflector? Because the parabola serves parallel-to-point: energy from its focus leaves in a parallel beam and never regathers. The job is point-to-point; only the two-focus curve does point-to-point. The rival fails on structure, not on taste.

Answer: \(\dfrac{x^2}{625} + \dfrac{y^2}{225} = 1\) with emitter and stone at \((\pm 20, 0)\) — an ellipse of eccentricity 0.8, chosen by the count of favored points and defended by the equal-path-length property.

Example 3

The brief: A power-station cooling tower has its narrowest radius, 30 m, at the throat. At 80 m above the throat, the structure must flare to a radius of 50 m. Model the profile.

Solution:

Coordinates: origin at the throat's center, \(x\) the horizontal radius, \(y\) the height above the throat. The silhouette is a hyperbola opening left and right, vertices at the throat: \(a = 30\), so

\(\dfrac{x^2}{900} - \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\)

Install the second constraint \((x, y) = (50, 80)\):

\(\dfrac{2500}{900} - \dfrac{6400}{b^2} = 1 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \dfrac{6400}{b^2} = \dfrac{2500}{900} - 1 = \dfrac{1600}{900}\)

\(b^2 = \dfrac{6400 \cdot 900}{1600} = 3600 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; b = 60\)

The profile is \(\dfrac{x^2}{900} - \dfrac{y^2}{3600} = 1\), asymptotes \(y = \pm 2x\). Verify: at \(y = 80\), \(x^2 = 900\left(1 + \frac{6400}{3600}\right) = 900 \cdot \frac{25}{9} = 2500\), so \(x = 50\) ✓.

Why a hyperbola at all? Two reasons an engineer can cite. Structurally, the hyperboloid (this profile revolved about the \(y\)-axis) is a ruled surface — it can be built entirely from straight beams, each lying wholly within the curved wall, giving concrete-and-steel strength from simple members. Functionally, the narrow throat accelerates the rising air (the same reason a nozzle narrows), then the flare above slows and disperses it. The curve is doing engineering on both counts.

Answer: \(\dfrac{x^2}{900} - \dfrac{y^2}{3600} = 1\), \(y\) in meters above the throat, valid on the tower's actual height — the domain of honesty ends where the concrete does.

The Four-Line Defense, Adapted for Conics

You first wrote this defense in Unit 2, arguing for truthful models; it returns now with geometric teeth. Every capstone defense — including the one your teacher method-grades this week — carries these four lines:

  • Claim — the conic, its equation, and your declared coordinate system, all in the brief's units.
  • Structure — the geometric property that does the job (reflection law, constant sum, constant difference, ruled surface), stated as the reason this family and no other.
  • Long-run — what the curve does at its edges: the aperture's limits, the asymptotes' direction, the closed orbit's return. Does the behavior at the boundary still serve the brief?
  • Domain of honesty — where the model stops: the interval on which the equation describes real material, and the physics (thickness, load, alignment) the idealized curve ignores. A defense that hides its limits is an advertisement, not an argument.

Four lines, each citing a computed number or a named property. That is what "citing evidence from the mathematics" means.

⚠️ AI CHECKPOINT: THE CROSS-EXAMINATION

On this week's Focus set you will draft a design defense and then hand it to your AI tutor with one instruction: “Attack the weakest line of this defense.” Your job is to judge whether the attack lands. Here is a rehearsal. A student defended the Example 1 dish, and the AI returned:

“Your structural claim is overstated. A circular arc of a large enough radius would focus the signal just as well and is cheaper to manufacture, so the parabola is not actually necessary.”

Sounds authoritative. It is false — and you can refute it from the locus definition. A parabola sends every ray parallel to its axis through one exact point: that is what "distance to focus equals distance to directrix" builds into the curve. A circular arc has no such point: rays striking far from its center miss the would-be focus (opticians call the blur spherical aberration), so a sphere "focuses" only the central rays, approximately. Across an 8-foot aperture the difference is the design. The attack does not land — and now the defense is stronger for having survived it.

This is the AI discipline of the whole course in one act: the machine is a useful opponent, and a mathematician judges the argument, never the confidence. When the AI attacks your capstone defense, verify every mathematical claim in the attack before you accept or dismiss a word of it — some attacks will land, and honest engineers thank the ones that do.

💻 In Desmos or Excel: Draw It to Scale 8 min

An engineer never defends an unseen design. Before you write a word of your capstone defense, build the picture — either tool serves:

  1. Desmos (desmos.com/calculator, or below): graph your design restricted to its real extent — for the Example 1 dish, y = 3x^2/32 {-4 <= x <= 4} — then plot the focus (0, 8/3) and check the picture against the brief: is the rim where the client said? Does the receiver hang inside the aperture's reach? For a hyperbola, add the asymptotes as dashed lines and watch the wall hug them.
  2. Excel: put \(x\) in column A from \(-4\) to \(4\) in steps of \(0.5\), the depth formula =3*A2^2/32 in column B, and read the table like a machinist: depth at the rim 1.5 ft ✓, depth at half-radius \(0.375\) ft — a quarter of the rim depth, because doubling \(x\) quadruples \(x^2\). An Insert → Scatter chart gives the profile a builder could check with a tape measure. (If you save the workbook, name it LastName_FirstName_ConicDesign_Precalculus.xlsx in your Optima → Math → PrecalculusHonors folder — the submission itself stays a PDF.)

Either way, the standard is the same: the picture confirms; your derivation convinces.

▶  Try it yourself: A headlight's bulb must sit at the focus, 2 cm from the vertex of its parabolic reflector. Write the equation, and find how wide the reflector is at a depth of 4.5 cm.
Vertex at the origin, \(p = 2\): the reflector is \(x^2 = 8y\). At depth \(y = 4.5\): \(x^2 = 8(4.5) = 36\), so \(x = \pm 6\) — the reflector is 12 cm wide at that depth. (Width is \(2x\): the radius-vs-diameter discipline again, in the other direction.) The bulb's light leaves the focus, strikes the parabola, and exits as a parallel beam — Example 1's property run in reverse.
▶  Try it yourself: A whispering gallery is a 30 m long, 18 m wide ellipse (a full ellipse in cross-section). Where should two people stand to hear each other whisper — and how far is each from the nearer end wall?
\(a = 15\), \(b = 9\): \(c^2 = a^2 - b^2 = 225 - 81 = 144\), so \(c = 12\). They stand at the foci, \((\pm 12, 0)\) — 24 m apart, and each stands \(a - c = 15 - 12 = 3\) m from the nearer end wall. Every whisper leaving one focus reflects off the wall to the other, and every path takes the same total distance \(2a = 30\) m, so the sound arrives whole.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Feeding a diameter to an equation that eats a radius. "The dish is 1.2 m across and 18 cm deep" puts the rim at \((0.6, 0.18)\) — substitute \(x = 1.2\) instead and your focal length quadruples, the receiver hangs uselessly high, and every line of a beautifully written defense defends the wrong dish. Declare your coordinates first, mark the rim point on a sketch before substituting, and let the verification step (put the focal length back, recover the brief's width) catch what haste hides. One sketch is worth four lines of apology.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The first hyperboloid tower was raised in 1896 by the Russian engineer Vladimir Shukhov for an exposition in Nizhny Novgorod — a water tower whose gracefully curved lattice was built entirely of straight steel members, because a hyperboloid is a ruled surface: through every point of it run two perfectly straight lines lying wholly within the surface. Shukhov patented the form, and its descendants — from Moscow's Shukhov radio tower to the cooling towers of Example 3 — still trade on the same theorem. A curve that can be built from straight lines is a curve that serves the builder twice.

Conclusion

The capstone asks of you what this unit has been quietly building toward: not another equation solved, but a choice defended — the right curve for the job, derived from the brief's own numbers and argued in four honest lines. That is this week's Focus assignment, the one your teacher grades for method: bring the derivation with its declared coordinates, and bring the defense with its named property, and let the AI's cross-examination sharpen both. On Thursday the Unit 5 test gathers the whole family into one sitting — the sliced cone, the four loci and their derivations, completing the square as the master key, eccentricity's single-number verdict, and the systems machinery counting crossings.

And then, look at where you stand. Eighteen weeks ago this course began with sequences — patterns marching one term at a time. From them grew the function families: polynomial and rational, exponential and logarithmic, radical and piecewise. Unit 4 taught the families to relate — composed, decomposed, inverted. And this unit drew the relations as curves in the plane, sliced from a cone and put to work in dishes, chambers, and towers. One semester, one arc: from counting to curving, every stage built on the one before. Next week — the Semester 1 finals week — you walk the whole arc in review and then show, on one cumulative final, that it holds together. It does. You built it.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Every conic's "service property" is a restatement of its locus definition. Trace one example all the way: how does "distance to focus equals distance to directrix" become "parallel rays meet at the focus"?

2. What makes a model defense an argument rather than an advertisement — and why is the domain-of-honesty line the one that most separates the two?

3. The lithotripter rejected the parabola on structure, not on taste. What question about a brief tells you, before any algebra, which conic family the job requires?

4. Apollonius studied these curves with no application in view, and the applications arrived centuries later. What does that pattern suggest about the worth of studying something because it is true and beautiful?
