📋 BEFORE YOU BEGIN

This test gathers the whole four-week arc into one sitting: the family sliced from the double cone, the four loci and their derivations, graphs read from standard form, completing the square as the master key to general form, eccentricity's one-number verdict, the service applications, and the systems machinery counting where curves cross. It has three parts: Part A (fluency), Part B (reasoning — write these as arguments), and Part C (one synthesis problem reaching back to Unit 3).

Ground rules. Set aside a quiet block of about 90 minutes and complete the test in one honest sitting. Work alone: no classmates, no AI tools, no solution websites — this is the one place where the mathematics must be entirely yours. A calculator may be used for arithmetic only (evaluating squares, roots, and quotients); every setup, derivation, completed square, and step of reasoning must appear on your page. Lesson pages and notes stay closed — the loci this test asks you to derive are ones you have derived yourself.

Write the honor line at the top of your first page and sign it: “I completed this test on my own, without help from people, notes, or AI tools.” Intellectual honesty is a quarter of the rubric — and in the unit whose virtue is service, honest work is the first service you owe your reader.

📝 PART A — FLUENCY (Lessons 5-1 through 5-14)

Neat vertical work for every problem — one step per line, equals signs aligned, exact values, labeled answers. Sketches drawn large, with foci marked and asymptotes dashed.

1
The sliced cone: A plane cuts a double cone four ways: ① perpendicular to the axis; ② tilted, but less steeply than the cone's edge; ③ parallel to exactly one edge; ④ steeply enough to cut both nappes. Name the curve each slice produces, and add one sentence: what is geometrically special about slice ③ that makes its curve the boundary case of the family?
2
Circle: The points \((-1, 4)\) and \((5, -4)\) are endpoints of a diameter. Find the circle's center and radius (both computations shown), write its standard-form equation, and verify that one of the given endpoints satisfies it.
3
Parabola: For \((y - 1)^2 = 8(x + 2)\): state the vertex, the value of \(p\), the direction of opening, the focus, and the directrix — then sketch, marking focus and directrix.
4
Ellipse: For \(\dfrac{(x-3)^2}{25} + \dfrac{(y+1)^2}{16} = 1\): state the center, vertices, co-vertices, foci, and eccentricity — each with the computation that produced it — and sketch with the foci marked.
5
Hyperbola: For \(\dfrac{(y+2)^2}{9} - \dfrac{(x-1)^2}{16} = 1\): state the center, vertices, foci, eccentricity, and the equations of both asymptotes — then sketch: central box, dashed asymptotes, both branches.
6
The master key: Classify and convert \(4x^2 + 9y^2 - 16x + 18y - 11 = 0\) to standard form by completing the square — both squares shown in full — and state the center, \(a\), \(b\), \(c\), and eccentricity of the resulting conic.
7
Eccentricity fluency: ① Classify sight-unseen the conics with \(e = 0\), \(e = 0.09\), \(e = 1\), and \(e = \frac{8}{5}\), one phrase of shape description each. ② Compute the eccentricity of \(\dfrac{x^2}{49} + \dfrac{y^2}{45} = 1\), showing \(a\), \(b\), \(c\), and confirming the answer is legal for its family.
8
A curve in service: A parabolic dish is 4 ft across and 1 ft deep. With the vertex at the origin, find the equation of its cross-section and the height of the receiver above the vertex — and add one sentence naming the geometric property that puts the receiver exactly there.
📝 PART B — REASONING (write these as arguments)

These problems are graded primarily on the quality of your reasoning. Full sentences where explanation is asked; every claim earned, not asserted.

9
Derive a locus. An ellipse has foci \((\pm 4, 0)\), and the sum of the distances from any of its points to the two foci is 10. Starting from \(\sqrt{(x+4)^2 + y^2} + \sqrt{(x-4)^2 + y^2} = 10\), derive the standard-form equation — every isolation, every squaring, and every simplification written, as you would for a classmate who has never seen the derivation. Conclude with two sentences: where in your algebra did the quantity \(a^2 - c^2\) appear, and why is it honest to rename it \(b^2\)?
10
Classify with full justification. For \(9x^2 - 4y^2 - 18x - 16y - 43 = 0\): ① predict the conic type from the equation's coefficients before any algebra, stating the sign evidence for the prediction; ② complete the square — the master key, every step shown — to reach standard form; ③ state the center, vertices, foci, eccentricity, and asymptotes; ④ sketch with the asymptotes dashed; ⑤ close with one sentence confirming the prediction and one naming the single algebraic move that unlocked the whole identification.
📝 PART C — SYNTHESIS (this unit meets Unit 3)
11
In Unit 3 you built the systems machinery; this unit taught it to count crossings of curves. Put both to work. A deep-space radar station sits at the origin with a detection range of 6 units: its range boundary is the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 36\). A comet crosses the region on the parabolic path \(y = \dfrac{x^2}{4} - 5\).
① Solve the system exactly, using the Unit 3 machinery: rewrite the path as \(x^2 = 4(y + 5)\), substitute into the circle, and solve the resulting quadratic in \(y\) — exact values only (the answers live with \(\sqrt{5}\); keep it).
② Judge every candidate. One value of \(y\) produced by the algebra cannot lie on the comet's path — post the guard that convicts it, in the manner you have practiced since Unit 3, and name the ghost.
③ Defend your count. Two conics can meet in as many as four points; state how many boundary crossings actually occur here and argue precisely why — how many candidates the quadratic offered, what the guard removed, and how many points each surviving \(y\) contributes and why. Verify one crossing point in both equations, arithmetic shown.
④ Interpret. In two or three closing sentences: the comet's vertex \((0, -5)\) lies inside the range circle — describe the comet's journey through detection (where it is visible and where it is not, in terms of your crossing points), and say what the station learns from the fact that the crossings come as a symmetric pair.
📊 GRADING RUBRIC

The whole test is graded against the standing four-criterion rubric, each criterion scored out of 10. Part A supplies most of the evidence for understanding and precision; Parts B and C supply most of the evidence for reasoning.

Criteria What excellent looks like Pts
Understanding of the material The right tool reaches every problem: features read from standard forms, squares completed cleanly, the correct Pythagorean relation for each family, eccentricities legal for their curves, notation exact throughout. 10 pts
Quality of reasoning The locus derivation reads as a real argument; the classification is predicted before it is computed; the intersection count in Part C is defended with the guard and the candidate census, not asserted; verifications are actually performed. 10 pts
Presentation & precision Clean vertical work throughout, aligned equals signs, exact values kept in radical form, sketches large with foci marked and asymptotes dashed, pages in order — forty points of work a stranger could follow. 10 pts
Intellectual honesty Honor line signed and honored; work shown as actually done; any problem you could not finish carries an honest note of where and why you stopped — which earns respect here, never punishment. 10 pts
Total: 40 pts
