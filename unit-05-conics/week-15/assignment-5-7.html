📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
This set drills the graphing craft of Lesson 5-7: reading standard form at sight — center, larger-denominator rule, four extreme points, foci from the triangle — unscrambling general form with the unit's master key, completing the square, and putting a number on every silhouette with eccentricity. This is a practice set: scored for honest completion, with worked solutions released after the due date for line-by-line self-correction.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
One completed square turns \(4x^2 + 9y^2 - 16x + 18y - 11 = 0\) into a portrait: center, axes, foci, eccentricity. What does standard form make visible that general form hides — and what must be balanced to get there honestly?
📝 PROBLEM SET

Show your work for every problem in neat vertical form — one step per line, equals signs aligned, final answers labeled. Draw graphs large: center dotted, all four extreme points labeled with coordinates, foci marked with ×, exact values throughout. In every completing-the-square, write the bookkeeping line: what was really added, factor included.

1
Read at sight: Graph \(\dfrac{x^2}{49} + \dfrac{y^2}{24} = 1\) — center, vertices, co-vertices, foci (exact), and eccentricity. State in a phrase how you knew the major axis's direction before computing anything.
2
Centered elsewhere: Graph \(\dfrac{(x+1)^2}{9} + \dfrac{(y-3)^2}{25} = 1\) — center, all four extreme points, foci, eccentricity. Mind the sign flips in the center's coordinates.
3
The master key: Write \(x^2 + 4y^2 - 6x + 16y + 21 = 0\) in standard form by completing the square — bookkeeping line included — then give center, orientation, vertices, foci (exact), and eccentricity.
4
Features to equation: Write the standard-form equation of the ellipse with center \((1, -2)\), a horizontal major axis of length 10, and a minor axis of length 6 — then locate its foci.
5
Eccentricity in reverse: An ellipse has vertices \((0, \pm 10)\) and eccentricity \(e = \frac{3}{5}\). Find \(c\), then \(b^2\), then write the equation and mark the foci.
6
Explain why: Two ellipses share \(a = 12\), but one has \(e = 0.1\) and the other \(e = 0.9\). In three or four sentences: what do the two curves share, what differs, where are their foci, and why does \(e = 0\) force a perfect circle? Sketch both silhouettes, roughly to scale.
7
Honors stretch: Unscramble \(25x^2 + 4y^2 + 100x - 8y + 4 = 0\) completely: standard form (bookkeeping line shown, both factored coefficients honored), center, orientation, all four extreme points, foci, and eccentricity — then a full hand graph. Finish with one sentence: this ellipse's \(e \approx 0.917\); which planet-or-comet silhouette from tomorrow's lesson is it closest to, a planet's or Halley's?
💡 CHECK YOURSELF WITH DESMOS
After solving on paper, audit problems 3 and 7 at desmos.com/calculator the way the lesson taught: type the general form exactly as printed, then type your standard form beneath it. One curve on the screen means your bookkeeping balanced; two curves means an added constant escaped its factor — hunt it down before you submit. The calculator confirms; your algebra convinces.
