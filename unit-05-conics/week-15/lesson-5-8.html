Ellipses That Serve: Orbits and Whispering Galleries
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to explain why an elliptical chamber carries a whisper from one focus to the other — both the equal-angle half and the equal-path-length half of the argument.

  • I will be able to compute a planet's or comet's perihelion and aphelion from its semi-major axis and eccentricity, and recover \(a\) and \(e\) from the two extremes.

  • I will be able to model real orbit data in an Excel table and, in VR, narrate why the whispering gallery and the planetary orbit are one theorem wearing two uniforms.

📚 VOCABULARY

Whispering gallery: A chamber with an elliptical (or ellipsoidal) ceiling in which a whisper spoken at one focus is heard clearly at the other, across a room where no one in between hears a thing.

Perihelion: An orbiting body's closest approach to the sun — Greek peri (near) + helios (sun) — at distance \(a - c = a(1 - e)\) from the focus the sun occupies.

Aphelion: The farthest point of the orbit — apo (away from) + helios — at distance \(a + c = a(1 + e)\).

Astronomical unit (AU): The mean Earth–sun distance, about 149.6 million kilometers — the solar system's own yardstick.

Kepler's First Law: Every planet travels an ellipse with the sun at one focus — a focus, not the center. Published in the Astronomia Nova, 1609.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Johannes Kepler inherited the finest observational data in the world — decades of planetary positions measured by Tycho Brahe, night after night, to a couple of arcminutes — and one stubborn problem: Mars. For years Kepler tried to fit Mars's path with the circles every astronomer since antiquity had assumed, and his best circular model missed Tycho's observations by a mere eight arcminutes, about a quarter of the width of the full moon. A lesser scientist would have shrugged; the ancients would never have noticed. Kepler refused to bluff away the discrepancy, and out of those eight minutes of arc, as he wrote, he built a new astronomy. The orbit of Mars was not a circle. It was an ellipse — Apollonius's eighteen-century-old curve — with the sun standing not at the center but at one focus. That is Kepler's First Law, published in 1609, and it means the curve you derived from two pins and a loop of string is the actual road every planet, comet, and spacecraft in the solar system travels. Today the ellipse goes to work twice: once overhead, in chambers that carry a whisper across a crowded room, and once in the sky.

The ellipse's reflective property. The parabola sent parallel rays to its one focus; the ellipse plays a two-focus version of the same music: a ray leaving one focus reflects off the ellipse and passes through the other focus. Every ray, from every point of the wall.

Why the Whisper Arrives — Twice Over

Half one: the angles. At any point \(P\) on the ellipse, the tangent line makes equal angles with the two focal segments \(PF_1\) and \(PF_2\). Here is the honors argument, in the spirit of 5-5's proof. The ellipse is the set of points where \(d_1 + d_2 = 2a\); every point strictly outside has distance-sum greater than \(2a\). The tangent at \(P\) touches the curve only at \(P\), so every other point \(X\) of the tangent line lies outside: \(XF_1 + XF_2 > 2a = PF_1 + PF_2\). So among all points of the tangent line, \(P\) is the one minimizing the trip \(F_1 \to X \to F_2\) — and the shortest bounce path off a line is precisely the one obeying the mirror law, angle in equals angle out (Heron of Alexandria proved that seventeen centuries before Fermat made it a principle of optics). Equal angles at \(P\); the sound leaving \(F_1\) reflects at \(P\) straight toward \(F_2\).

Half two: the timing. Reflection alone would scatter the whisper's arrivals in time — except that every path \(F_1 \to P \to F_2\) has the same total length: \(d_1 + d_2 = 2a\), the definition itself. Every echo travels the same distance, so every echo arrives at the same instant, stacked into one clear syllable rather than smeared into murmur. The gallery works because of both halves at once: the angles aim every ray at \(F_2\), and the string condition makes them all punctual. Same theorem, both barrels.

Example 1

A whispering gallery is 100 feet long, with a semi-elliptical ceiling 30 feet high at the center. Where should two people stand to whisper to each other, and how high is the ceiling directly above each of them?

Solution:

Center the ellipse at the room's midpoint: full length \(2a = 100\), so \(a = 50\); central height \(b = 30\). The whisperers belong at the foci:

\(c^2 = a^2 - b^2 = 2500 - 900 = 1600 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; c = 40\)

The foci sit 40 feet either side of center — 10 feet in from each end wall, 80 feet apart. For the ceiling height above a focus, set \(x = 40\):

\(\frac{40^2}{2500} + \frac{y^2}{900} = 1 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \frac{y^2}{900} = 1 - \frac{16}{25} = \frac{9}{25} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; y = 30 \cdot \frac{3}{5} = 18\)

Answer: Stand 10 feet from each end wall, on the long axis, 80 feet apart; the ceiling hangs 18 feet above each whisperer. Speak softly toward the ceiling, and the room itself delivers the message — every reflected path 100 feet long, every echo on time.

From ceilings to the sky. Now put the sun at the focus \(F_1\) and let \(P\) be a planet. The planet's distance from the sun changes constantly along its ellipse — but its extremes are easy to read off the major axis: the near vertex lies \(a - c\) from the focus, the far vertex \(a + c\). Since \(c = ae\), astronomers write:

\(\text{perihelion} = a(1 - e), \qquad \text{aphelion} = a(1 + e)\)

Example 2

Mars orbits with semi-major axis \(a = 1.524\) AU and eccentricity \(e = 0.0934\). Find its perihelion and aphelion.

Solution:

\(\text{perihelion} = 1.524(1 - 0.0934) = 1.524(0.9066) \approx 1.382 \text{ AU}\)

\(\text{aphelion} = 1.524(1 + 0.0934) = 1.524(1.0934) \approx 1.666 \text{ AU}\)

Answer: Mars swings from about \(1.38\) AU to about \(1.67\) AU from the sun — a spread of more than 40 million kilometers between its nearest and farthest approach. That spread, fed through Tycho's relentless measurements, is what betrayed the circle to Kepler; Mars, with the most eccentric orbit of the readily observable planets, was exactly the right planet to be assigned.

Example 3

Halley's comet has \(a = 17.8\) AU and \(e = 0.967\). Find its perihelion, its aphelion, and its semi-minor axis, and say what the numbers mean.

Solution:

\(\text{perihelion} = 17.8(1 - 0.967) = 17.8(0.033) \approx 0.59 \text{ AU}\)

\(\text{aphelion} = 17.8(1.967) \approx 35.0 \text{ AU}\)

For the semi-minor axis, use \(b = a\sqrt{1 - e^2}\) — which is just \(b^2 = a^2 - c^2\) with \(c = ae\) factored out:

\(b = 17.8\sqrt{1 - 0.967^2} = 17.8\sqrt{1 - 0.935089} = 17.8\sqrt{0.064911} \approx 17.8(0.2548) \approx 4.5 \text{ AU}\)

Answer: Halley's comet dives inside Venus's orbit (\(0.59\) AU) and retreats past Neptune's neighborhood (\(35\) AU) — the same loop of string, pulled long. Yet note \(b \approx 4.5\) AU: even at \(e = 0.967\), the orbit is still about a quarter as wide as it is long — an honest ellipse, not the needle the eccentricity might suggest. Eccentricity measures how far the foci are displaced, not how thin the curve has become; the two are related but not the same, as your Excel table is about to make vivid.

Example 4

Earth's perihelion is \(147.1\) million km and its aphelion \(152.1\) million km. Recover the orbit's semi-major axis and eccentricity.

Solution:

Run the formulas in reverse. Perihelion and aphelion sum to \((a - c) + (a + c) = 2a\) and differ by \(2c\):

\(a = \frac{147.1 + 152.1}{2} = \frac{299.2}{2} = 149.6 \text{ million km}\)

\(c = \frac{152.1 - 147.1}{2} = 2.5 \text{ million km}\)

\(e = \frac{c}{a} = \frac{2.5}{149.6} \approx 0.0167\)

Answer: \(a = 149.6\) million km — the astronomical unit itself — and \(e \approx 0.0167\). Two distance measurements, and the whole orbit surrenders its geometry: this is how astronomers actually pin down an orbit's shape. (Notice perihelion arrives in early January: Earth is closest to the sun in the Northern Hemisphere's winter. Seasons come from axial tilt, not distance — the ellipse is innocent of that particular charge.)

📊 In Excel: The Orbit Table 12 min

Kepler needed years for Mars; with formulas in hand, you can survey the solar system in one sitting. In a new Excel workbook:

  1. Head five columns: Body, a (AU), e, Perihelion (AU), Aphelion (AU) — and a sixth, b/a, for the shape ratio.
  2. Enter the data rows: Mercury \(0.387, 0.2056\) · Venus \(0.723, 0.0068\) · Earth \(1.000, 0.0167\) · Mars \(1.524, 0.0934\) · Jupiter \(5.204, 0.0489\) · Saturn \(9.583, 0.0565\) · Halley's comet \(17.8, 0.967\).
  3. In the perihelion column write =B2*(1-C2); aphelion =B2*(1+C2); shape ratio =SQRT(1-C2^2). Fill each formula down; format to three decimals.
  4. Now read your table like an astronomer. Which planets are nearly circles (\(b/a\) within a hair of 1) — and yet how many million kilometers is even Earth's sun off-center? Whose perihelion–aphelion spread first betrayed the ellipse to Kepler? And Halley: \(e = 0.967\), but what does \(b/a\) say about how “thin” the orbit actually is?

▶  Try it yourself: Venus has \(a = 108.2\) million km and \(e = 0.0068\). Find its perihelion and aphelion, and describe the orbit in one honest sentence.
Perihelion \(= 108.2(0.9932) \approx 107.5\) million km; aphelion \(= 108.2(1.0068) \approx 108.9\) million km. The most nearly circular orbit of any planet — a spread of barely 1.5 million km — yet still, honestly, an ellipse with the sun at a focus.
▶  Try it yourself: A gallery hall is 40 feet long with a semi-elliptical ceiling 12 feet high. Where do the whisperers stand, and how far apart are they?
\(a = 20\), \(b = 12\): \(c^2 = 400 - 144 = 256\), so \(c = 16\). Stand on the long axis 16 feet either side of center — 4 feet from each end wall — 32 feet apart. (A 12–16–20 right triangle: the 3–4–5 in disguise, still on duty.)
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Putting the sun at the center. It is the single most natural error in the room — twenty centuries of excellent astronomers made it — and it quietly wrecks every distance computation: from the center, the near and far vertices are both \(a\) away, and perihelion equals aphelion, which is exactly the circle Kepler had to abandon. The sun stands at a focus, \(c = ae\) off-center, and the whole spread between \(a(1-e)\) and \(a(1+e)\) exists because of that displacement. Before any orbit computation, mark the focus on your sketch first — and make the same check in the gallery problems: whisperers stand at the foci, never at the center of the room.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The U.S. Capitol's Statuary Hall — the old House chamber — has a smooth, roughly semi-elliptical ceiling, and it is one of the world's famous accidental whispering galleries. The story told to every visitor: John Quincy Adams, serving in the House after his presidency, had his desk near a focus and could overhear opposing members conferring in whispers across the room. Historians debate how much of the tale is true — the room has been remodeled since — but the acoustics are real, and guides demonstrate them daily. Geometry keeps no secrets; sometimes it keeps yours from staying secret.

Conclusion

This week the conics went to work. The parabola's one focus gathered signals and disciplined headlight beams; the ellipse's two foci carried whispers across a stone room and turned out to be the road map of the solar system — Kepler's eight stubborn arcminutes forcing the sun off-center and onto a focus, perihelion and aphelion falling out of the diagram as \(a(1-e)\) and \(a(1+e)\), and your Excel table surveying in minutes what once took a lifetime of nights. Service, twice over: a theorem that carries voices and holds planets. Next week, the family's last member — the one Apollonius named for excess. Change one word in the ellipse's definition — the difference of the focal distances is constant, rather than the sum — and the string snaps open into a curve with two branches, asymptotes for a compass, and its own kind of service: it is how ships found themselves at sea before satellites. One conic still unmet; it has been waiting since the week began.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. The whispering gallery needs both halves of the argument — equal angles and equal path lengths. What does each half contribute, and what would a listener at \(F_2\) experience if only the first half held?

2. Perihelion is \(a - c\) and aphelion is \(a + c\). Point to both distances on the ellipse's diagram: where do they begin and end, and why do they sum to \(2a\)?

3. Halley's comet has \(e = 0.967\), yet its orbit is about a quarter as wide as it is long. What does eccentricity actually measure — and what does it not?

4. Kepler moved the sun from the center of the circle to the focus of an ellipse. Why is that displacement — a few million kilometers — the whole difference between the old astronomy and the new?
