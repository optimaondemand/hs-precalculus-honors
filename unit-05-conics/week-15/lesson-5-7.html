Graphing Ellipses
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to graph an ellipse centered anywhere, locating its center, vertices, co-vertices, and foci from standard form.

  • I will be able to convert an ellipse from general form to standard form by completing the square — the master key of this unit — with every added constant accounted for honestly.

  • I will be able to compute and interpret eccentricity \(e = \frac{c}{a}\) as the single number that measures an ellipse's shape.

📚 VOCABULARY

Standard form: \(\frac{(x-h)^2}{a^2} + \frac{(y-k)^2}{b^2} = 1\) (or with \(a^2\) under the \(y\)-term) — the form that wears its geometry openly: center \((h,k)\), semi-axes \(a\) and \(b\).

General form: \(Ax^2 + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0\) with \(A\) and \(C\) both positive (and unequal) — the same ellipse with its geometry scrambled into one line. Completing the square unscrambles it.

Eccentricity: The ratio \(e = \frac{c}{a}\), with \(0 \leq e < 1\) for every ellipse — from the Latin ex centro, “out of center”: literally, how far off-center the foci sit, as a fraction of the curve's half-length.

Semi-major / semi-minor axis: The half-lengths \(a\) and \(b\); on a centered-anywhere ellipse they are measured from \((h, k)\) along and across the major axis.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Yesterday's derivation pinned the ellipse to the origin, but the universe is under no such obligation — the orbits, arches, and whispering chambers we meet tomorrow are centered wherever they please. The remedy is the same shift-of-center you have used since Algebra 2: replace \(x\) with \(x - h\) and \(y\) with \(y - k\), and every feature of the ellipse packs up and moves with the center. And when an ellipse arrives with its equation scrambled into general form, the tool that restores order is the one this unit has leaned on since the circle in Lesson 5-2: completing the square, the master key that has now opened three of the four conics. There is also a payoff waiting at the end of today's work — a single number, the eccentricity, that captures the shape of any ellipse: how round, how flattened, how far its foci have wandered out of center. Astronomers describe every orbit in the solar system with it, and tomorrow so will you.

Reading an Ellipse at Sight

From \(\frac{(x-h)^2}{\square} + \frac{(y-k)^2}{\square} = 1\):

  • Center: \((h, k)\) — the values that zero each parenthesis.
  • \(a^2\) is the larger denominator, wherever it sits. Under the \(x\)-term: major axis horizontal. Under the \(y\)-term: vertical. Orientation is a fact about sizes, never about which variable was written first.
  • Vertices: \(a\) units from center along the major axis. Co-vertices: \(b\) units along the minor axis.
  • Foci: \(c\) units from center along the major axis, with \(c^2 = a^2 - b^2\) — yesterday's right triangle, relocated but unchanged.

Four dots and two axis-lengths: with the center, two vertices, and two co-vertices plotted, the ellipse practically draws itself.

Example 1

Graph \(\dfrac{x^2}{9} + \dfrac{y^2}{25} = 1\), locating all key points, and find its eccentricity.

Solution:

Center \((0, 0)\). The larger denominator, 25, sits under \(y^2\): the major axis is vertical, with \(a = 5\) and \(b = 3\). Then

\(c^2 = a^2 - b^2 = 25 - 9 = 16 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; c = 4\)

Vertices \((0, \pm 5)\); co-vertices \((\pm 3, 0)\); foci \((0, \pm 4)\), on the major axis, inside the curve. Plot the four extreme points and sweep the oval through them.

Answer: Eccentricity \(e = \dfrac{c}{a} = \dfrac{4}{5} = 0.8\) — a noticeably elongated ellipse, its foci four-fifths of the way from center to vertex.

Example 2

Graph \(\dfrac{(x-2)^2}{16} + \dfrac{(y+1)^2}{7} = 1\), locating center, vertices, co-vertices, and foci exactly.

Solution:

Center \((2, -1)\) — note the sign flip: \(y + 1 = y - (-1)\). The larger denominator, 16, is under the \(x\)-term: major axis horizontal, \(a = 4\), \(b = \sqrt{7}\).

\(c^2 = 16 - 7 = 9 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; c = 3\)

March out from the center: vertices 4 left and right, \((-2, -1)\) and \((6, -1)\); co-vertices \(\sqrt{7} \approx 2.65\) up and down, \((2, -1 \pm \sqrt{7})\); foci 3 left and right, \((-1, -1)\) and \((5, -1)\).

Answer: \(e = \dfrac{3}{4} = 0.75\). Keep \(\sqrt{7}\) exact on the page and use the decimal only to place the pencil — exact values are the argument; decimals are just the drawing aid.

Example 3 — The Master Key

Write \(4x^2 + 9y^2 - 16x + 18y - 11 = 0\) in standard form and graph it.

Solution:

Group by variable and factor out each leading coefficient — all the way out, before any squares are completed:

\(4(x^2 - 4x) + 9(y^2 + 2y) = 11\)

Complete each square, and account for what was really added. Inside the first parentheses we add 4 — but it sits inside a factor of 4, so the left side truly gained \(4 \cdot 4 = 16\). Inside the second we add 1, inside a factor of 9: truly \(9\). Balance both:

\(4(x^2 - 4x + 4) + 9(y^2 + 2y + 1) = 11 + 16 + 9\)

\(4(x - 2)^2 + 9(y + 1)^2 = 36\)

Divide by 36 to reach the “\(= 1\)” that standard form demands:

\(\frac{(x-2)^2}{9} + \frac{(y+1)^2}{4} = 1\)

Answer: Center \((2, -1)\); horizontal major axis with \(a = 3\), \(b = 2\); \(c^2 = 9 - 4 = 5\), so foci \(\left(2 \pm \sqrt{5}, -1\right)\); vertices \((-1, -1)\) and \((5, -1)\); co-vertices \((2, 1)\) and \((2, -3)\); eccentricity \(e = \frac{\sqrt{5}}{3} \approx 0.745\). One completed square turned a five-term scramble into a portrait.

Example 4

Write the equation of the ellipse with vertices \((\pm 8, 0)\) and eccentricity \(e = \frac{1}{2}\).

Solution:

From the vertices: center at the origin, horizontal major axis, \(a = 8\). Eccentricity now works in reverse:

\(e = \frac{c}{a} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; c = ea = \tfrac{1}{2}(8) = 4\)

\(b^2 = a^2 - c^2 = 64 - 16 = 48\)

Answer: \(\dfrac{x^2}{64} + \dfrac{y^2}{48} = 1\), foci \((\pm 4, 0)\). An eccentricity of one-half sounds substantial, yet \(b = \sqrt{48} \approx 6.9\) — this ellipse is still nearly seven-eighths as tall as it is long. Eccentricity must grow quite large before the eye notices; hold that thought for tomorrow's orbits.

Key Fact: Eccentricity Is the Shape Number

For every ellipse, \(e = \dfrac{c}{a}\) and \(0 \leq e < 1\):

  • \(e = 0\) forces \(c = 0\): the foci merge at the center, \(b = a\), and the ellipse is a circle — the circle is not a rival to the ellipse but its roundest member.
  • As \(e \to 1\), the foci flee toward the vertices and the curve flattens toward a segment — but \(e = 1\) is never reached: that boundary belongs to the parabola, as the unit's honors extension will show.
  • Two ellipses with the same \(e\) are the same shape — one is a scaled photograph of the other, whatever their sizes.

One number, the whole silhouette. Earth's orbit: \(e = 0.017\). Halley's comet: \(e = 0.97\). Tomorrow those two numbers will do a great deal of talking.

💻 In Desmos: The Portrait and the Scramble 10 min

At desmos.com/calculator (or below):

  1. Audit Example 3. Desmos graphs implicit equations directly — type the general form exactly as given: 4x² + 9y² - 16x + 18y - 11 = 0. Then type your standard form: (x-2)²/9 + (y+1)²/4 = 1. If your completing-the-square was honest, the two graphs land exactly on top of each other — one curve, two costumes. If two curves appear, an added constant went unbalanced; hunt it down.
  2. Build the family. Add sliders h, k, a, b and graph (x-h)²/a² + (y-k)²/b² = 1. Drag \(h\) and \(k\) — the whole portrait travels; drag \(a\) past \(b\) and back — watch the major axis flip orientation the instant the larger denominator changes owners.
  3. Watch eccentricity work. With a slider e on \((0, 1)\), graph x²/a² + y²/(a²(1-e²)) = 1 and plot the foci (-ae, 0) and (ae, 0). Slide \(e\) from 0.05 to 0.95: circle, oval, cigar — and the foci racing outward. The calculator confirms; your algebra convinces.

▶  Try it yourself: Graph \(\frac{(x-4)^2}{25} + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1\) — center, vertices, foci, eccentricity. One focus lands somewhere memorable.
Center \((4, 0)\); horizontal, \(a = 5\), \(b = 3\), \(c^2 = 25 - 9 = 16\), \(c = 4\). Vertices \((-1, 0)\) and \((9, 0)\); co-vertices \((4, \pm 3)\); foci \((0, 0)\) and \((8, 0)\) — one focus is the origin itself. \(e = \frac{4}{5}\). (An ellipse described by its focus rather than its center is exactly how astronomy will hand them to you tomorrow: the sun sits at a focus, not at the center.)
▶  Try it yourself: Write \(9x^2 + y^2 - 18x - 4y + 4 = 0\) in standard form, then give center, orientation, foci, and eccentricity.
\(9(x^2 - 2x) + (y^2 - 4y) = -4\); complete: \(9(x-1)^2 - 9 + (y-2)^2 - 4 = -4\), so \(9(x-1)^2 + (y-2)^2 = 9\), giving \(\frac{(x-1)^2}{1} + \frac{(y-2)^2}{9} = 1\). Center \((1, 2)\); vertical major axis, \(a = 3\), \(b = 1\); \(c^2 = 8\), \(c = 2\sqrt{2}\); foci \((1,\ 2 \pm 2\sqrt{2})\); \(e = \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3} \approx 0.943\) — a tall, narrow ellipse, and the added constants (\(9\) and \(4\), not \(1\) and \(4\)) both balanced.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Completing the square inside a factored group and balancing only the inside number. In \(4(x^2 - 4x)\), adding 4 inside the parentheses adds \(4 \times 4 = 16\) to the equation — the factor multiplies everything it touches, including your new constant. Balance with 4 instead of 16 and the right side is wrong by 12; the “ellipse” that follows has the wrong size, wrong \(c\), wrong everything, while every individual step looks tidy. The bookkeeping line — “added \(4 \cdot 4 = 16\) and \(9 \cdot 1 = 9\)” — is not scratch work; write it, every time. Then let Desmos audit you: graph both forms, and demand one curve.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Earth's orbit has eccentricity \(0.0167\). Drawn to scale on this page, it would be indistinguishable from a perfect circle — the flattening is about one part in seven thousand, far finer than any pencil line. What is not negligible is where the sun sits: about 2.5 million kilometers off-center. An ellipse can hide its shape from the eye and still refuse to put its star in the middle — which is why it took a Kepler, and not just a sharp glance, to discover the truth. That story is tomorrow's lesson.

Conclusion

The ellipse now travels: standard form reads at sight — center, larger-denominator rule, four extreme points, foci from the unchanging right triangle — and general form surrenders to completing the square, the master key that has now opened the circle, the parabola, and the ellipse alike, with the factored-coefficient bookkeeping done honestly and audited in Desmos. And you met eccentricity, the one number that is an ellipse's whole silhouette: \(0\) for the circle, racing toward \(1\) for the cigar, identical for any two ellipses of the same shape. Tomorrow the ellipse goes to work — and the work is glorious: chambers that carry a whisper eighty feet to a waiting ear, and the planets themselves, which have been tracing ellipses around a focus — not a center — since long before anyone proved it. Bring your VR headset; you are going to stand at a focus and hear the theorem.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why does the larger denominator — and never the order of the variables — determine which way the major axis runs?

2. Completing the square has now unlocked the circle, the parabola, and the ellipse. What, exactly, does it accomplish for a conic in general form — what does it find, and what does it discard?

3. Two ellipses share the same \(a\) but have \(e = 0.1\) and \(e = 0.9\). What do they share, what differs, and where are their foci?

4. Why does \(e = 0\) force a circle — and why can an ellipse's eccentricity approach 1 but never arrive?
