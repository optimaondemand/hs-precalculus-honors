The Ellipse as a Locus
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to state the two-focus definition of the ellipse and use it directly — distances, not formulas — to test whether a point lies on a given ellipse.

  • I will be able to derive the standard equation \(\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\) from the definition, justifying every squaring step and the substitution \(b^2 = a^2 - c^2\).

  • I will be able to write the equation of an ellipse from its key features: foci, vertices, co-vertices, or the distance sum \(2a\).

📚 VOCABULARY

Ellipse: The set of all points \(P\) for which the sum of the distances to two fixed points \(F_1\) and \(F_2\) is a constant, written \(2a\). From the Greek elleipsis, “falling short” — Apollonius's own name for the curve.

Foci (singular focus): The two fixed points of the definition, at \((\pm c, 0)\) when the ellipse is centered at the origin with a horizontal major axis. The parabola had one hearth; the ellipse keeps two.

Major axis / vertices: The longest chord of the ellipse, running through both foci; its endpoints \((\pm a, 0)\) are the vertices, and its half-length \(a\) is the semi-major axis.

Minor axis / co-vertices: The shortest diameter, perpendicular to the major axis through the center; its endpoints \((0, \pm b)\) are the co-vertices, with semi-minor axis \(b\).

Center: The midpoint of the segment joining the foci — the ellipse's point of symmetry.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Press two pins into a board, drop a loop of string around them, pull the string taut with a pencil, and sweep. The pencil traces a closed, gracefully flattened curve — gardeners have laid out flowerbeds this way for centuries, and draftsmen call it the gardener's construction. What the string enforces is the definition itself: pencil-to-pin-one plus pencil-to-pin-two is always the same length of string. Around 200 BC, Apollonius of Perga studied this curve as a slice of the cone and named it elleipsis — “falling short,” because in his construction a certain area fell short of a target, where the parabola's fit exactly (parabolē, “placing alongside”). One member of his family of names is still owed to us; it arrives next week. Today's work is to take the string in one hand and algebra in the other and show they draw the same curve — the full derivation, done honestly, every squaring on the record.

The definition, precisely. Fix two foci \(F_1(-c, 0)\) and \(F_2(c, 0)\), and a constant \(2a\) with \(2a > 2c\) — the string must be longer than the distance between the pins, or the pencil cannot move at all. The ellipse is the set of points \(P(x, y)\) with

\(PF_1 + PF_2 = 2a\)

Example 1

An ellipse has foci \((\pm 3, 0)\) and distance sum \(2a = 10\). Using only the definition — no formula — verify that \((0, 4)\) and \((5, 0)\) lie on it.

Solution:

For \((0, 4)\), compute both focal distances:

\(d_1 = \sqrt{(0-(-3))^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5, \qquad d_2 = \sqrt{(0-3)^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5\)

\(d_1 + d_2 = 5 + 5 = 10\) ✓

For \((5, 0)\), the distances run along the axis: \(d_1 = 5 - (-3) = 8\) and \(d_2 = 5 - 3 = 2\), so \(d_1 + d_2 = 10\) ✓.

Answer: Both points belong to the ellipse. Notice what just happened at each point: at the vertex \((5,0)\) the two distances are \(a + c = 8\) and \(a - c = 2\); at the top point \((0,4)\) they are equal, 5 each — and 5 is exactly \(a\). Hold that thought; it is about to become the most useful triangle in the unit.

The master derivation. Now we earn the equation. Start from the definition and write the distances out in full:

\(\sqrt{(x+c)^2 + y^2} + \sqrt{(x-c)^2 + y^2} = 2a\)

Step 1 — isolate one radical. Two radicals in one equation is one too many; you learned this discipline solving radical equations in Unit 3.

\(\sqrt{(x+c)^2 + y^2} = 2a - \sqrt{(x-c)^2 + y^2}\)

Step 2 — square, carefully. The right side is a binomial; the cross term is the whole story:

\((x+c)^2 + y^2 = 4a^2 - 4a\sqrt{(x-c)^2 + y^2} + (x-c)^2 + y^2\)

Step 3 — let the symmetry do the canceling. Expand \((x+c)^2 - (x-c)^2\): the \(x^2\) and \(c^2\) terms cancel, leaving \(4cx\). The \(y^2\) terms cancel outright:

\(4cx = 4a^2 - 4a\sqrt{(x-c)^2 + y^2}\)

\(a\sqrt{(x-c)^2 + y^2} = a^2 - cx\)

Step 4 — square again. One radical remains, already isolated:

\(a^2\left[(x-c)^2 + y^2\right] = a^4 - 2a^2cx + c^2x^2\)

\(a^2x^2 - 2a^2cx + a^2c^2 + a^2y^2 = a^4 - 2a^2cx + c^2x^2\)

The \(-2a^2cx\) terms — the ugliest in sight — cancel identically. Collect what survives:

\((a^2 - c^2)x^2 + a^2y^2 = a^2(a^2 - c^2)\)

Step 5 — name the new quantity and divide. Because \(2a > 2c\), the number \(a^2 - c^2\) is positive — here, and only here, is where the string-longer-than-the-pins condition enters the algebra. Give it a name: \(b^2 = a^2 - c^2\). Divide through by \(a^2b^2\):

\(\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\)

Set \(x = 0\): \(y = \pm b\). The abbreviation \(b\) turns out to be the semi-minor axis — the curve's half-height. The equation is earned.

A word of honesty about those two squarings. In Unit 3, squaring an equation could smuggle in ghost solutions, and we posted guards. Why does no ghost haunt this derivation? Because both squared sides were already nonnegative wherever the curve lives: in Step 1 the right side \(2a - \sqrt{(x-c)^2+y^2}\) is nonnegative since no focal distance on the ellipse can exceed the whole string \(2a\), and in Step 4 the right side \(a^2 - cx\) is positive since \(|x| \leq a\) and \(c < a\). When both sides of a squaring carry the same sign, the step is reversible and the solution set never grows. The guards were posted; they simply had nothing to catch. That check is part of a derivation done honestly — not a decoration on it.

Why \(b^2 = a^2 - c^2\) Is Geometry, Not Bookkeeping

Stand at the co-vertex \((0, b)\), the top of the minor axis. By symmetry your two focal distances are equal, and they must sum to \(2a\) — so each one is exactly \(a\), just as Example 1 found at \((0,4)\).

Now look at the right triangle with legs \(b\) (up the minor axis) and \(c\) (out to a focus) and hypotenuse \(a\) (the focal distance you just computed): the Pythagorean theorem says \(b^2 + c^2 = a^2\) on the spot. The substitution in Step 5 was not an algebraic convenience — it was this triangle, waiting in the picture all along. Draw it on every ellipse you meet this week; it computes any one of \(a, b, c\) from the other two.

Example 2

Write the equation of the ellipse with foci \((\pm 3, 0)\) and vertices \((\pm 5, 0)\).

Solution:

The foci are on the \(x\)-axis, so the major axis is horizontal. Read off \(c = 3\) and \(a = 5\); the triangle gives

\(b^2 = a^2 - c^2 = 25 - 9 = 16\)

Answer: \(\dfrac{x^2}{25} + \dfrac{y^2}{16} = 1\). This is Example 1's ellipse — and the check is already done: we verified \((0, 4)\) and \((5, 0)\) by pure string-arithmetic before the formula existed.

Example 3

Write the equation of the ellipse with foci \((0, \pm 2)\) and distance sum 8.

Solution:

The foci sit on the \(y\)-axis: the major axis is vertical, so \(a^2\) belongs under \(y^2\). From \(2a = 8\), \(a = 4\); with \(c = 2\),

\(b^2 = a^2 - c^2 = 16 - 4 = 12\)

Answer: \(\dfrac{x^2}{12} + \dfrac{y^2}{16} = 1\). Quick definition-check at the vertex \((0, 4)\): distances to \((0, 2)\) and \((0, -2)\) are 2 and 6, summing to 8 ✓. Note the tell: the larger denominator sits under \(y^2\), and that — not any memory of which letter came first — is what announces a vertical major axis.

Example 4

Write the equation of the ellipse with vertices \((\pm 7, 0)\) and co-vertices \((0, \pm 2)\), and locate its foci.

Solution:

Both semi-axes are handed to us: \(a = 7\), \(b = 2\), major axis horizontal. The equation is immediate, and the triangle runs in reverse for \(c\):

\(c^2 = a^2 - b^2 = 49 - 4 = 45 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; c = \sqrt{45} = 3\sqrt{5}\)

Answer: \(\dfrac{x^2}{49} + \dfrac{y^2}{4} = 1\), foci \(\left(\pm 3\sqrt{5}, 0\right)\) — exact value, no decimal. This long, low ellipse hides its foci far from center: \(3\sqrt{5} \approx 6.7\), nearly at the vertices. Flatter ellipse, farther foci — tomorrow we will give that observation a name and a number.

💻 In Desmos: Ride the String 10 min

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), watch the definition and the equation agree in real time:

  1. Graph Example 2's ellipse: x²/25 + y²/16 = 1. Plot the foci as points: F1 = (-3, 0) and F2 = (3, 0).
  2. Put a rider on the curve: add a slider t, then the point P = (5cos(t), 4sin(t)). Drag \(t\) and watch \(P\) travel the ellipse.
  3. Now measure the string: type distance(P, F1) + distance(P, F2). Drag \(t\) anywhere — top, vertex, anywhere between — and watch the sum sit locked at 10, to every decimal Desmos will show.
  4. Break it on purpose: change \(P\) to \((5\cos t,\ 3.9\sin t)\), a point just off the curve, and watch the sum wobble as \(t\) moves. Only the true ellipse holds the string taut at exactly \(2a\). The calculator confirms; your algebra convinces.

▶  Try it yourself: Write the equation of the ellipse with foci \((\pm 2, 0)\) and distance sum 12. Work it on paper before you peek.
Horizontal major axis (foci on the \(x\)-axis). From \(2a = 12\), \(a = 6\); with \(c = 2\), \(b^2 = 36 - 4 = 32\). Equation: \(\frac{x^2}{36} + \frac{y^2}{32} = 1\). Those close-set pins on a long string make a nearly circular ellipse — \(b = \sqrt{32} \approx 5.66\), barely shy of \(a = 6\).
▶  Try it yourself: An ellipse has vertices \((0, \pm 5)\) and foci \((0, \pm 3)\). Write its equation, then verify the vertex \((0,5)\) with the bare definition.
Vertical major axis: \(a = 5\), \(c = 3\), so \(b^2 = 25 - 9 = 16\) and the equation is \(\frac{x^2}{16} + \frac{y^2}{25} = 1\). Definition check at \((0,5)\): distances to \((0,3)\) and \((0,-3)\) are \(2\) and \(8\); sum \(= 10 = 2a\) ✓.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Writing \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\). For the ellipse the foci live inside the curve, so \(c\) is the smallest of the three lengths and the triangle reads \(a^2 = b^2 + c^2\) — the hypotenuse \(a\) is the co-vertex's focal distance, as the blue box shows. (A plus-sign version of this law does exist, and it belongs to next week's curve, whose foci sit outside. Learn the triangle, not the formula, and you will never swap them.) Companion error: assuming \(a^2\) always sits under \(x^2\). It doesn't — \(a^2\) is simply the larger denominator, wherever it sits, and its position is what tells you which way the major axis runs.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Apollonius's three names never left us — they just moonlight in English class. A parable is a story placed alongside a truth (parabolē, exact application); an ellipsis is the punctuation mark … of words falling short (elleipsis, deficiency); and hyperbole is a claim thrown beyond the truth (hyperbolē, excess). You have now met two of the three curves. The third — the curve of excess — is waiting for you next week.

Conclusion

Today the string and the equation shook hands. From nothing but sum of two distances equals \(2a\), a two-radical monster surrendered — isolate, square, simplify, square again — to \(\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\), with the string-longer-than-the-pins condition surfacing precisely where \(a^2 - c^2\) needed to be positive, and the substitution \(b^2 = a^2 - c^2\) revealed as a right triangle that was in the picture all along. You checked the squarings for ghosts and found the derivation honest. This derivation is the centerpiece of this week's Focus assignment — the one set your teacher grades for method — where you will run the entire argument yourself on a specific ellipse, every step labeled, nothing skipped because it felt obvious. Tomorrow the ellipse leaves the origin: centers at \((h, k)\), a master key called completing the square, and a single number — eccentricity — that measures exactly how far any ellipse has fallen short of being a circle.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. The definition never mentions an equation, and the equation never mentions string. What, exactly, guarantees they describe the same curve?

2. Where in the derivation is the condition \(2a > 2c\) actually used — and what breaks, algebraically and physically, without it?

3. Why is \(b^2 = a^2 - c^2\) a statement about a right triangle rather than a bookkeeping substitution? Draw the triangle from memory.

4. The derivation squares both sides twice, yet no extraneous ghost appears — unlike the radical equations of Unit 3. What makes these squarings reversible?
