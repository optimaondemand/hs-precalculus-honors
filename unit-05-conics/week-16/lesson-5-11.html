Hyperbolas That Serve: Navigation and Cooling Towers
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to translate a measured time difference between two radio signals into a hyperbolic line of position, and explain why the constant time-difference is the constant distance-difference of the definition.

  • I will be able to solve LORAN-style location problems: build the hyperbola, choose the correct branch, and fix a position with one added piece of information.

  • I will be able to solve structural problems on hyperbolic cross-sections — reading and designing the profile of a cooling tower.

📚 VOCABULARY

LORAN (LOng RAnge Navigation): A radio navigation system, built out during the Second World War, in which shore stations broadcast synchronized pulses and a ship's receiver measures the tiny difference in their arrival times. Its mathematics is today's lesson.

Line of position: The curve on which a navigator knows the ship must lie — not yet a point, but an honest narrowing of the ocean to a single arc. One time difference buys one line of position.

Hyperboloid: The three-dimensional surface swept out when a hyperbola revolves about its conjugate axis — the pinched-waist silhouette of the power-plant cooling tower.

Ruled surface: A curved surface that can be generated entirely by straight lines. The hyperboloid is one — which is precisely why builders love it.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Apollonius studied the hyperbola because it was true and beautiful; he could not have guessed who would need it. Twenty-one centuries after the Conics, convoys crossed the North Atlantic under radio silence, and navigators fixed their positions by listening: two shore stations broadcast synchronized pulses, and the ship's receiver clocked the split-second gap between their arrivals. That gap put the ship on one branch of a hyperbola whose foci were the two stations. A second pair of stations gave a second hyperbola, and the ship sat at the crossing. The system was called LORAN, and its successors guided ships and aircraft for half a century until satellites — whose GPS mathematics is a cousin of the same idea — took the watch. This is Unit 5's virtue at full strength: the ellipse whispered in galleries and carried planets; the hyperbola stood watch over sailors. Curves serve.

Why a time difference draws a hyperbola. Radio pulses travel at the speed of light — about \(0.3\) km per microsecond (μs). Suppose the pulse from station \(B\) reaches the ship \(t\) microseconds before the pulse from station \(A\), and both were sent at the same instant. Then the ship is nearer to \(B\), and the two distances differ by exactly

\(d_A - d_B = 0.3\,t \text{ km}\)

The receiver has not learned either distance — only their difference. But a constant difference of distances to two fixed points is, word for word, the definition from Lesson 5-9. The ship lies on a hyperbola with foci \(A\) and \(B\), constant \(2a = 0.3\,t\) — and on one specific branch: the branch that wraps the nearer station, the one whose signal won the race. Physics measured a time; geometry heard a definition.

From Stopwatch to Curve — the Chain of Custody

  • Time difference → distance difference: multiply by the signal speed. \(2a = (0.3 \text{ km}/\mu\text{s}) \times t\).
  • Distance difference → hyperbola: the definition of 5-9, verbatim, with the stations as foci and \(c\) = half the station separation. Then \(b^2 = c^2 - a^2\), and the equation follows.
  • Sign of the difference → branch: whichever station's pulse arrives first is the nearer focus; the ship rides the branch that bends around it. One measurement can never put you on both branches — it tells you not just how much closer you are, but to whom.
  • Sanity check before any algebra: the distance difference must be less than the station separation, \(2a < 2c\) — the triangle inequality's fine print from 5-9. A “measured” difference of 120 km between stations 100 km apart is not a hard problem; it is an impossible reading, and a navigator who catches it has caught a broken instrument.

One hyperbola is a line of position, not a position: the ocean has been narrowed to an arc, and one more fact — a second station pair, a known offshore distance, a depth sounding — picks the point off the arc.

Example 1

Shore stations \(A\) and \(B\) sit 100 km apart on a straight east–west coast. Put them at \(A(-50, 0)\) and \(B(50, 0)\), with the sea toward positive \(y\). A ship's receiver clocks the pulse from \(B\) arriving \(200\) μs before the pulse from \(A\). Find the ship's line of position.

Solution:

Convert the time difference to a distance difference:

\(d_A - d_B = 0.3 \times 200 = 60 \text{ km}, \qquad \text{so } 2a = 60, \;\; a = 30\)

Sanity check: \(60 < 100\) ✓ — the reading is physically possible. The stations are the foci, so \(c = 50\), and

\(b^2 = c^2 - a^2 = 2500 - 900 = 1600, \qquad b = 40\)

A 30–40–50 triangle — the box builds itself. Since \(B\)'s pulse won the race, the ship is 60 km nearer to \(B\): the east branch, \(x > 0\).

Answer: \(\dfrac{x^2}{900} - \dfrac{y^2}{1600} = 1\), east branch (the branch around \(B\)), with \(y > 0\) since the ship is at sea. An arc of ocean — not yet a point.

Example 2

The same ship's radar shows it is exactly 80 km offshore — that is, \(y = 80\). Fix the position.

Solution:

Substitute \(y = 80\) into the line of position:

\(\dfrac{x^2}{900} - \dfrac{6400}{1600} = 1 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \dfrac{x^2}{900} = 1 + 4 = 5 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x^2 = 4500 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x = \pm 30\sqrt{5}\)

The east branch demands \(x > 0\): \(x = 30\sqrt{5} \approx 67.1\) km.

Judge the answer against the original definition — the habit transfers from equations to oceans. At \(P(30\sqrt{5},\, 80)\):

\(d_A^2 = (30\sqrt{5} + 50)^2 + 80^2 = 4500 + 3000\sqrt{5} + 2500 + 6400 = 13400 + 3000\sqrt{5} \approx 20108.2\)

\(d_B^2 = (30\sqrt{5} - 50)^2 + 80^2 = 13400 - 3000\sqrt{5} \approx 6691.8\)

\(d_A \approx 141.8 \text{ km}, \quad d_B \approx 81.8 \text{ km}, \quad d_A - d_B \approx 60.0 \text{ km} \;\checkmark\)

Answer: The ship is at \(\big(30\sqrt{5},\, 80\big) \approx (67.1,\, 80)\) — about 67 km east of the coastline's midpoint and 80 km out to sea. The difference measured by the clock is the difference measured by the map, to the tenth of a kilometer.

Example 3

A power-plant cooling tower has the pinched-waist profile of a hyperbola. Its narrowest cross-section — the waist — has radius 30 m; place the origin at the waist's center, \(y\) vertical. The tower's sides follow \(\dfrac{x^2}{900} - \dfrac{y^2}{2500} = 1\). Find the radius of the tower (a) at its base, 120 m below the waist, and (b) at its top rim, 50 m above the waist.

Solution:

The tower's silhouette is the two branches of this hyperbola; a horizontal slice at height \(y\) is a circle whose radius is the positive \(x\) on the curve. Here \(a = 30\) (the waist — the vertex is the point nearest the axis, which is exactly why the waist is the narrowest ring), and \(b = 50\).

(a) Base, \(y = -120\):

\(\dfrac{x^2}{900} = 1 + \dfrac{(-120)^2}{2500} = 1 + \dfrac{14400}{2500} = 1 + 5.76 = 6.76\)

\(x^2 = 900 \times 6.76 = 6084 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x = \sqrt{6084} = 78\)

(b) Top, \(y = 50\):

\(\dfrac{x^2}{900} = 1 + \dfrac{2500}{2500} = 2 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x^2 = 1800 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x = 30\sqrt{2} \approx 42.4\)

Answer: Base radius 78 m (diameter 156 m); top radius \(30\sqrt{2} \approx 42.4\) m (diameter ≈ 84.9 m). The tower flares wide at the ground, cinches to 60 m across at the waist, and opens again at the rim — two hyperbolic arcs doing structural work.

Example 4

Design in reverse. An engineer wants a tower with waist radius 40 m whose radius grows to 50 m at a height 60 m above the waist. Find the equation of the profile, and the tower's radius 120 m below the waist.

Solution:

The waist gives \(a = 40\), so the profile is \(\dfrac{x^2}{1600} - \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\) with one unknown, and the point \((50, 60)\) must satisfy it — the same “point pins the constant” move as Lesson 5-9's Example 3:

\(\dfrac{2500}{1600} - \dfrac{3600}{b^2} = 1 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \dfrac{3600}{b^2} = \dfrac{2500}{1600} - 1 = \dfrac{900}{1600} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; b^2 = \dfrac{3600 \times 1600}{900} = 6400\)

Check: \(\frac{2500}{1600} - \frac{3600}{6400} = 1.5625 - 0.5625 = 1\) ✓. Now the radius at \(y = -120\):

\(x^2 = 1600\left(1 + \dfrac{14400}{6400}\right) = 1600 \times 3.25 = 5200 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x = 20\sqrt{13} \approx 72.1\)

Answer: Profile \(\dfrac{x^2}{1600} - \dfrac{y^2}{6400} = 1\); the base ring, 120 m below the waist, has radius \(20\sqrt{13} \approx 72.1\) m. Exact first, decimal for the construction crew.

Key Fact: Why Engineers Choose the Hyperboloid

Rotate a hyperbola about its conjugate axis and you get a hyperboloid — and a small miracle of geometry: the curved surface is ruled, generated entirely by straight lines. Tilt a straight rod, spin it about a vertical axis, and the rod sweeps out exactly this pinched-waist surface. The consequences build towers:

  • Straight members, curved strength: a lattice of straight steel beams — cheap to roll, easy to check — can form the whole doubly-curved shell.
  • Material economy: the double curvature stiffens the shell against wind and buckling, so the concrete can run astonishingly thin for the height.
  • The flare serves the draft: the wide base swallows air, the narrowing waist speeds the updraft, and the flared rim releases it — the profile is aerodynamics and statics agreeing on one curve.

The Russian engineer Vladimir Shukhov built the first hyperboloid tower for an exposition in 1896; his steel-lattice water towers and radio masts — hundreds of them — proved the form, and the power industry adopted it worldwide. Apollonius's curve of excess, holding up the sky over every major power grid: service, rendered in concrete.

⚠️ AI CHECKPOINT: THE SUPERVISED TUTOR

Today's examples are a small fleet — your AI tutor can launch you more ships. Prompt it precisely, the way a working mathematician commissions a problem:

“Write one LORAN-style problem: two stations on a coordinate axis, a signal time difference in microseconds with radio waves at 0.3 km/μs, and one extra fact that fixes the ship's position. Give the setup only — no solution — and make all of a, b, c come out as whole numbers.”

Then supervise before you solve. Run the navigator's sanity check on the AI's setup: convert its time difference to kilometers and confirm \(2a < 2c\) — AI-generated problems fail the triangle inequality more often than you would think, handing you a hyperbola that cannot exist. If the setup survives, solve it in full, then ask the AI for its worked solution and audit the arithmetic line by line — especially the step where \(b^2 = c^2 - a^2\), which an AI will sometimes quietly swap for the ellipse's relation.

The order matters: verify the problem, solve it yourself, then judge the machine's solution against yours. A tool that is usually right is exactly the kind that needs a supervisor — usually is not a word navigators trust.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Setting \(a\) equal to the whole distance difference. The definition says \(|d_1 - d_2| = 2a\) — the measured difference is twice \(a\). A 60-km difference means \(a = 30\), not 60; forget the halving and every downstream number — \(b^2\), the box, the fix — inherits the error, plausibly enough that nothing looks wrong until the ship is 30 km from where you said it was. Its twin mistake: forgetting that the stations are the foci, so their separation is \(2c\), not \(c\). Both slips are the same disease — treating a full axis as a half axis — and the same habit cures both: label \(2a\), \(2c\) on your diagram before touching the formulas, then halve deliberately.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
The sound of a supersonic aircraft also draws a conic on the ground. The shock wave spreads as a cone trailing the plane; where that cone meets the flat earth, the boundary of the boom — in level flight — is one branch of a hyperbola. Everyone standing on the curve hears the boom at the same moment. Apollonius sliced a cone with a plane on papyrus; a fighter jet performs the same construction over the desert, at Mach 1.5, with geometry as the only draftsman.

Conclusion

The hyperbola earned its keep today twice over. At sea, a stopwatch difference became a distance difference became a line of position — the definition of 5-9 doing navigation verbatim, with the branch chosen by who won the race and the fix completed by one more honest fact. On land, the same curve stood up in concrete: waist at the vertex, flare by the equation, straight steel lines weaving a doubly-curved shell that serves the draft and shrugs at the wind. Both applications ran on the two relations you now own — \(|d_1 - d_2| = 2a\) and \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\) — plus the discipline of checking answers against the definition itself. Tomorrow the unit gathers its whole family for a reckoning. Circle, parabola, ellipse, hyperbola: all four hide inside one master equation, \(Ax^2 + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0\), and completing the square — the key that has opened every door in this unit — will make any such equation confess which conic it conceals.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. A LORAN receiver never learns how far the ship is from either station — only the difference. Why is that weaker information still strong enough to draw a curve, and what would it take to measure to get a circle instead?

2. One time-difference measurement places the ship on a single branch, never both. What does the sign of the difference know that the absolute value alone would not?

3. The triangle inequality caps every honest reading at \(2a < 2c\). What should a navigator conclude from a “measured” difference greater than the station separation — and from one exactly equal to it?

4. The cooling tower's profile could have been a cylinder, a cone, or a paraboloid. What does the hyperboloid offer — geometrically and structurally — that earns it the job, and in what sense is that an act of service?
