Identifying Conics from General Form
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to classify any second-degree equation \(Ax^2 + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0\) by inspecting the coefficients \(A\) and \(C\).

  • I will be able to convert between general form and standard form in both directions — completing the square one way, expanding the other.

  • I will be able to detect degenerate conics — a point, a pair of lines, or nothing at all — and explain what the coefficient shortcut cannot see that completing the square can.

📚 VOCABULARY

General form: The expanded, alphabetized shape of a conic's equation, \(Ax^2 + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0\) (with \(A\) and \(C\) not both zero). Every conic in this unit can be dressed this way — and undressed again.

Standard form: The completed-square shape — \((x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 = r^2\), \(\frac{(x-h)^2}{a^2} \pm \frac{(y-k)^2}{b^2} = 1\), \((y-k)^2 = 4p(x-h)\), and their siblings — in which center, vertices, foci, and radii can simply be read off.

Degenerate conic: What remains when the slicing plane of Lesson 5-1 passes through the cone's apex: a single point, one line, or a pair of intersecting lines. On the page, a degenerate hides inside an equation that wears an honest conic's coefficients.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Apollonius needed eight books to hold the conic sections. René Descartes, in the slim Géométrie of 1637 — the same book that chained the bar onto the radical sign you met in Lesson 3-10 — compressed the whole family into a single line of algebra. His invention of coordinates meant every curve became an equation, and the conics became precisely the curves of degree two:

\(Ax^2 + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0\)

Circle, parabola, ellipse, hyperbola: four curves, four weeks of this unit — one equation. (A fully general degree-two equation also carries a \(Bxy\) term, which rotates the conic off the vertical-horizontal grid; that term waits for a later course, and every conic we meet stays unrotated.) Today's craft is the naturalist's: given an equation in the wild, name the species at a glance — and then, because a glance can be deceived, prove the identification with the tool that has opened every lock in this unit. You completed the square to find a circle's center, a parabola's vertex, an ellipse's foci. Today it is promoted from tool to master key.

The Field Guide: Reading \(A\) and \(C\)

Only the two squared-term coefficients decide the species (assume the equation has a genuine graph — the honest, non-degenerate case):

  • \(A = C\) (both nonzero) → circle — the two square terms carry equal weight in every direction.
  • \(A \neq C\), same sign (\(AC > 0\)) → ellipse — both squares pull inward, unequally.
  • Opposite signs (\(AC < 0\)) → hyperbola — one square fights the other; yesterday's minus sign, spotted in the wild.
  • Exactly one of \(A\), \(C\) is zero → parabola — one variable is squared, the other only steers.

The shortcut predicts; completing the square proves. \(D\), \(E\), and \(F\) can never change the species — linear terms only slide the curve around the plane, which is why the glance works. But \(F\) can do something sneakier: it decides whether the specimen is alive at all. Two equations can share \(A\) and \(C\) exactly, and one draws an ellipse while the other draws a single point, or nothing. The coefficients name the species; only completing the square confirms the sighting.

Example 1

Classify and convert: \(3x^2 + 3y^2 - 12x + 6y - 12 = 0\).

Solution:

\(A = C = 3\): the glance says circle. Divide by 3 first — a circle deserves a leading coefficient of 1 — then complete both squares:

\(x^2 + y^2 - 4x + 2y - 4 = 0\)

\((x^2 - 4x + 4) + (y^2 + 2y + 1) = 4 + 4 + 1\)

\((x - 2)^2 + (y + 1)^2 = 9\)

Answer: A circle, center \((2, -1)\), radius 3. The right side came out positive — the specimen is alive. Hold that thought.

Example 2

Classify and convert: \(4x^2 + 9y^2 - 16x + 18y - 11 = 0\).

Solution:

\(A = 4\), \(C = 9\): same sign, unequal — ellipse. Group by variable, factor each squared coefficient out of its own group, and mind the bookkeeping:

\(4(x^2 - 4x) + 9(y^2 + 2y) = 11\)

\(4(x - 2)^2 - 16 + 9(y + 1)^2 - 9 = 11\)

— completing \(x^2 - 4x\) adds 4 inside parentheses multiplied by 4, so 16 has really been added, and 16 must be paid back; likewise the 9. Then:

\(4(x - 2)^2 + 9(y + 1)^2 = 36 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \dfrac{(x-2)^2}{9} + \dfrac{(y+1)^2}{4} = 1\)

Answer: An ellipse, center \((2, -1)\), \(a = 3\) horizontal, \(b = 2\); \(c^2 = 9 - 4 = 5\), foci \((2 \pm \sqrt{5},\, -1)\). Note the ellipse's own relation \(c^2 = a^2 - b^2\) — the minus sign belongs to the ellipse, the plus to yesterday's hyperbola.

Example 3

Classify and convert: \(x^2 - 4y^2 + 6x + 16y - 11 = 0\).

Solution:

\(A = 1\), \(C = -4\): opposite signs — hyperbola. Factor \(-4\) out of the \(y\)-group, signs and all:

\((x^2 + 6x) - 4(y^2 - 4y) = 11\)

\((x + 3)^2 - 9 - 4(y - 2)^2 + 16 = 11\)

— completing the \(y\)-square adds 4 inside parentheses multiplied by \(-4\): that is \(-16\) added, so \(+16\) appears to repay it. Then:

\((x + 3)^2 - 4(y - 2)^2 = 4 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \dfrac{(x+3)^2}{4} - \dfrac{(y-2)^2}{1} = 1\)

Answer: A hyperbola, center \((-3, 2)\), opening left–right; \(a = 2\), \(b = 1\), \(c^2 = 4 + 1 = 5\), foci \((-3 \pm \sqrt{5},\, 2)\), asymptotes \(y - 2 = \pm\frac{1}{2}(x + 3)\) — slopes read off yesterday's box, half-height 1 over half-width 2.

Example 4

Classify and convert: \(y^2 - 8x + 4y + 12 = 0\).

Solution:

\(A = 0\), \(C = 1\): only one square survives — parabola. Complete the square in \(y\) and herd everything else to the other side:

\(y^2 + 4y + 4 = 8x - 12 + 4\)

\((y + 2)^2 = 8x - 8 = 8(x - 1)\)

Answer: A parabola, vertex \((1, -2)\), opening right; \(4p = 8\) gives \(p = 2\), so the focus is \((3, -2)\) and the directrix is \(x = -1\) — Week 14's cast of characters, recovered from camouflage.

Example 5

Classify and convert: \(9x^2 + 4y^2 + 18x - 16y + 25 = 0\).

Solution:

\(A = 9\), \(C = 4\): the glance says ellipse. Now make the equation swear to it:

\(9(x^2 + 2x) + 4(y^2 - 4y) = -25\)

\(9(x + 1)^2 - 9 + 4(y - 2)^2 - 16 = -25\)

\(9(x + 1)^2 + 4(y - 2)^2 = 0\)

There is no ellipse. A sum of two nonnegative quantities equals zero only when both are zero: \(x = -1\) and \(y = 2\), simultaneously and only there.

Answer: The graph is the single point \((-1, 2)\) — a degenerate “ellipse.” The coefficients predicted a species; the completed square held the trial and returned a different verdict. Had the constant conspired to leave a negative right side, the verdict would have been harsher still: no graph at all, an equation naming an empty set. The shortcut predicts; completing the square proves.

Key Fact: Degenerates Are Apex Slices

The degenerate cases are not algebraic accidents — they are the slices you saw in this unit's opening VR session, when the cutting plane passes through the double cone's apex:

  • Tilt the plane at an ellipse's angle through the apex → it meets the cone in a single point (Example 5).
  • Tilt it at a hyperbola's angle through the apex → it slices along two intersecting lines — a hyperbola whose branches have collapsed onto their own asymptotes (today's honors accordion below).
  • Lay it tangent along the cone's side → a single line, the parabola's degenerate.

Every degenerate is a true conic section — cut through the one point where the two nappes meet. The family portrait was complete from the very first lesson; today the algebra catches up with the geometry.

💻 In Desmos: The Pixels Have Limits 8 min

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), Desmos will graph general form directly — no conversion needed. Use that power, then find its edge:

  1. Type each of Examples 1–4 exactly as given, general form and all. Circle, ellipse, hyperbola, parabola bloom on screen — instant confirmation of your four conversions and every center and vertex you computed.
  2. Now type Example 5: 9x^2 + 4y^2 + 18x - 16y + 25 = 0. The screen shows… nothing you can see. A single point has no width for a pixel grid to honor. Your completed square knows something the display cannot show — today, only the algebra convinces.
  3. Watch the degeneration happen: graph 9x^2 + 4y^2 + 18x - 16y + F = 0 with a slider for \(F\). Drag \(F\) upward: the ellipse shrinks as the right side \(25 - F\) dwindles… wait — compute: the completed square gives \(9(x+1)^2 + 4(y-2)^2 = 25 - F\). At \(F < 25\) an ellipse, collapsing toward its center; at \(F = 25\) the single point; past it, the screen goes empty for good. One coefficient walks the equation from conic to degenerate to nonexistence.
  4. The moral, stated once for the whole unit: the calculator confirms what has size; your algebra convinces about everything, including what doesn't.

▶  Try it yourself: Classify and convert \(x^2 + y^2 + 8x - 6y = 0\). Work it before you peek.
\(A = C = 1\): circle. \((x^2 + 8x + 16) + (y^2 - 6y + 9) = 16 + 9\), so \((x+4)^2 + (y-3)^2 = 25\): center \((-4, 3)\), radius 5. Bonus observation: \(F = 0\) means the origin satisfies the equation — this circle passes through \((0,0)\), and indeed \(16 + 9 = 25\) ✓.
▶  Try it yourself (honors): Classify and convert \(x^2 - y^2 + 2x + 2y = 0\) — and don't trust the glance.
\(A = 1\), \(C = -1\): the glance says hyperbola. Complete both squares: \((x+1)^2 - 1 - \big[(y-1)^2 - 1\big] = 0\), so \((x+1)^2 - (y-1)^2 = 0\). A difference of squares equal to zero factors: \((x + 1) = \pm(y - 1)\) — the two intersecting lines \(y = x + 2\) and \(y = -x\), crossing at the would-be center \((-1, 1)\). A degenerate hyperbola is its own pair of asymptotes: the box remains, the branches are gone.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Paying back the wrong amount. In \(4(x^2 - 4x)\), completing the square inserts \(+4\) inside parentheses that are multiplied by 4 — the equation has really been handed \(+16\), and 16 is what must be balanced on the other side. Add only 4 and the “standard form” that emerges is a fiction with a plausible face. The same trap doubles when the factored coefficient is negative: in \(-4(y^2 - 4y)\), inserting \(+4\) inside means adding \(-16\), so the repayment is \(+16\), sign and all. The discipline: factor first, complete inside, multiply out what you actually added, and pay back exactly that — every completed square in this unit, from circle to hyperbola, has lived or died on this bookkeeping.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Descartes published the Géométrie as an appendix — a demonstration piece bolted to the back of his philosophical Discourse on Method, as if analytic geometry were a worked example of thinking clearly. In one stroke it made every curve computable and every equation drawable, and the conics — guarded for eighteen centuries in Apollonius's Greek — became a single line any student could write. Fermat had the same idea independently at nearly the same moment and published almost nothing. The tools you used today are their handshake across the seventeenth century.

Conclusion

The week began with two pins and a rule about differences; it ends with the whole family under one roof. Any equation of the form \(Ax^2 + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0\) now answers to you: the coefficients \(A\) and \(C\) name the species at a glance, and completing the square — the master key that found every center, vertex, and focus in this unit — delivers the proof, or exposes the impostor: the point that dressed as an ellipse, the crossed lines that dressed as a hyperbola. Next week the unit finishes its work in three movements. The conics meet systems: circles crossing lines, orbits crossing orbits, with your Unit 3 solving skills called back to active duty. Then a single number — eccentricity — steps forward and unifies the entire family: circle, ellipse, parabola, hyperbola revealed as one curve family swept out by one parameter. And in the design capstone, you will choose a conic, defend it like an engineer, and put four weeks of geometry in service of a real design — the unit's virtue given the final word.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Why can \(A\) and \(C\) alone name a conic's species, while \(D\), \(E\), and \(F\) can only relocate it — and what, exactly, can \(F\) still do that the glance will never detect?

2. What earns completing the square the title of the unit's master key? Name every job it has done since Week 14 — and the one verdict in today's lesson that no calculator screen could deliver.

3. Each degenerate conic is a genuine slice of the double cone. Which slices produce the point, the single line, and the crossed lines — and why does the crossed-lines case deserve the name “a hyperbola reduced to its own asymptotes”?

4. Four curves, one second-degree equation. What does that unity suggest about the conics being a single family rather than four acquaintances — and what single number might parametrize the whole family? (Next week answers.)
