The Hyperbola as a Locus
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to derive the standard equation of a hyperbola from its two-focus definition — a constant difference of distances.

  • I will be able to write the equation of a hyperbola from its key features: foci, vertices, a point on the curve, or a shifted center.

  • I will be able to state and use the relation \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\), and explain precisely why it differs from the ellipse's \(c^2 = a^2 - b^2\).

📚 VOCABULARY

Hyperbola: The set of all points in the plane for which the difference of the distances to two fixed points (the foci) has a constant absolute value. From the Greek hyperbolē, “excess, a throwing beyond” — the same root as the rhetorical hyperbole.

Foci (singular focus): The two fixed anchor points of the definition, a distance \(2c\) apart. Same word as the ellipse's — only the rule that uses them has changed.

Transverse axis: The segment through the foci joining the two vertices — the points of the curve closest to the center. Its length is \(2a\), the constant difference itself.

Conjugate axis: The segment through the center perpendicular to the transverse axis, of length \(2b\). No point of the hyperbola lies on it — yet it will control the entire shape of tomorrow's graphs.

Branch: One of the hyperbola's two separate arcs. The ellipse's single closed loop has split in two — and the definition itself tells us why.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Around 200 BC, Apollonius of Perga wrote the Conics — eight books and nearly four hundred propositions on the curves cut from a cone, a work so complete that for eighteen centuries nobody improved on it. It was Apollonius who gave the three curves their names, drawn from a comparison of areas in his constructions: where the area fell short, he wrote elleipsis (ellipse); where it fit exactly, parabolē (parabola); and where it exceeded, hyperbolē — the hyperbola, the curve of excess. English still uses his very word: a hyperbole is a statement thrown beyond the truth. Today we meet the last and wildest member of his family.

You already own the key. Last week the ellipse was born from two pins and a loop of string: the set of points where the sum of the distances to two foci is constant. Now change one word. Keep the two foci — but demand instead that the difference of the two distances stay constant. That single edit tears the closed loop apart. Points closer to the right focus by the required amount form one arc; points closer to the left focus by the same amount form a mirror-image arc on the other side. One definition, two signs, two branches. The absolute value in the definition is not decoration — it is the reason the curve comes in two pieces.

The Definition — and Its Fine Print

A hyperbola is the set of all points \(P\) such that

\(\left|\, PF_1 - PF_2 \,\right| = 2a,\)

where \(F_1\) and \(F_2\) are the foci, a distance \(2c\) apart, and \(0 < 2a < 2c\).

The fine print \(2a < 2c\) is not arbitrary — it is the triangle inequality speaking. In triangle \(PF_1F_2\), the difference of two sides can never reach the third side, so \(|PF_1 - PF_2| < F_1F_2 = 2c\) for every point \(P\) off the line through the foci. Ask for a difference equal to \(2c\) and only points on two rays qualify; ask for more and nothing in the plane qualifies at all. Compare the ellipse's fine print, \(2a > 2c\): the string must be longer than the pin gap. Each curve's inequality is the triangle inequality wearing that curve's uniform — and each one will decide which Pythagorean-flavored relation the curve obeys.

The derivation. Place the center at the origin with foci \(F_1(-c, 0)\) and \(F_2(c, 0)\), and let \(P(x, y)\) be any point of the locus. The definition says the difference of the two distances is \(\pm 2a\) — plus on one branch, minus on the other:

\(\sqrt{(x+c)^2 + y^2} - \sqrt{(x-c)^2 + y^2} = \pm 2a\)

Isolate one radical and square — the same irreversible move you supervised so carefully with radical equations in Unit 3, used here on purpose and with both signs kept deliberately:

\((x+c)^2 + y^2 = 4a^2 \pm 4a\sqrt{(x-c)^2 + y^2} + (x-c)^2 + y^2\)

Now \((x+c)^2 - (x-c)^2 = 4cx\), so after subtracting and dividing by 4:

\(cx - a^2 = \pm a\sqrt{(x-c)^2 + y^2}\)

Square once more and expand both sides:

\(c^2x^2 - 2a^2cx + a^4 = a^2x^2 - 2a^2cx + a^2c^2 + a^2y^2\)

\((c^2 - a^2)\,x^2 - a^2y^2 = a^2(c^2 - a^2)\)

Here is the payoff of the fine print: because \(c > a\), the quantity \(c^2 - a^2\) is positive, and we are entitled to name it — let \(b^2 = c^2 - a^2\). Divide through by \(a^2b^2\):

\(\dfrac{x^2}{a^2} - \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\)

Two squarings, and no ghosts to hunt: unlike a radical equation, where the reflected twin was an intruder, here the \(\pm\) belonged to the definition from the start — the two signs are the two branches, and the final equation holds exactly the locus, both arcs together. The minus sign between the terms is the ellipse's plus sign, transformed by the same one-word edit that transformed the definition.

Key Facts: The Two Standard Forms

With center \((h, k)\), transverse axis length \(2a\), conjugate axis length \(2b\), and \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\):

  • Opens left–right: \(\dfrac{(x-h)^2}{a^2} - \dfrac{(y-k)^2}{b^2} = 1\) — vertices \((h \pm a,\, k)\), foci \((h \pm c,\, k)\).
  • Opens up–down: \(\dfrac{(y-k)^2}{a^2} - \dfrac{(x-h)^2}{b^2} = 1\) — vertices \((h,\, k \pm a)\), foci \((h,\, k \pm c)\).

The positive term steers. Whichever variable owns the positive term names the direction the branches open, and \(a^2\) is always the denominator of that positive term — regardless of size. Unlike the ellipse, a hyperbola's \(a\) may be smaller than its \(b\): the roles are assigned by sign, not by seniority. And since \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\), the foci always sit farther from the center than the vertices — outside the curve's turning points, where the ellipse tucked its foci inside.

Example 1

Find the equation of the hyperbola with foci \((\pm 5, 0)\) whose distance-difference is 6 — then verify the definition at an actual point of the curve.

Solution:

The foci give \(c = 5\); the constant difference is \(2a = 6\), so \(a = 3\). Then

\(b^2 = c^2 - a^2 = 25 - 9 = 16\)

The foci lie on the \(x\)-axis, so the \(x\)-term is positive: \(\dfrac{x^2}{9} - \dfrac{y^2}{16} = 1\).

Now make the equation testify. Take the point of the curve directly above the right focus, \(x = 5\): then \(\frac{25}{9} - \frac{y^2}{16} = 1\) gives \(y^2 = 16 \cdot \frac{16}{9}\), so \(y = \frac{16}{3}\). Measure both distances from \(P\left(5, \frac{16}{3}\right)\):

\(PF_1 = \sqrt{(5+5)^2 + \left(\tfrac{16}{3}\right)^2} = \sqrt{\tfrac{900}{9} + \tfrac{256}{9}} = \sqrt{\tfrac{1156}{9}} = \tfrac{34}{3}\)

\(PF_2 = \tfrac{16}{3}, \qquad PF_1 - PF_2 = \tfrac{34}{3} - \tfrac{16}{3} = \tfrac{18}{3} = 6 \;\checkmark\)

Answer: \(\dfrac{x^2}{9} - \dfrac{y^2}{16} = 1\). The difference came out to exactly \(2a = 6\) — the equation and the definition are the same statement in two languages.

Example 2

Write the equation of the hyperbola with vertices \((0, \pm 2)\) and foci \((0, \pm 3)\).

Solution:

Vertices and foci on the \(y\)-axis: the branches open up–down, so \(y\) owns the positive term. From the vertices, \(a = 2\); from the foci, \(c = 3\); so

\(b^2 = c^2 - a^2 = 9 - 4 = 5\)

Answer: \(\dfrac{y^2}{4} - \dfrac{x^2}{5} = 1\). Note \(a^2 = 4\) is the smaller denominator here — on a hyperbola that is perfectly legal. The positive term, not the bigger number, wears the \(a^2\).

Example 3

Find the equation of the hyperbola with vertices \((\pm 4, 0)\) that passes through the point \((8, 6)\).

Solution:

Vertices on the \(x\)-axis give \(a = 4\) and the form \(\dfrac{x^2}{16} - \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\). The point \((8, 6)\) must satisfy it:

\(\dfrac{64}{16} - \dfrac{36}{b^2} = 1 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; 4 - \dfrac{36}{b^2} = 1 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \dfrac{36}{b^2} = 3 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; b^2 = 12\)

Check: \(\frac{64}{16} - \frac{36}{12} = 4 - 3 = 1\) ✓. And the foci: \(c^2 = 16 + 12 = 28\), so \(c = 2\sqrt{7}\).

Answer: \(\dfrac{x^2}{16} - \dfrac{y^2}{12} = 1\), with foci \((\pm 2\sqrt{7},\, 0)\). Exact values — \(2\sqrt{7}\), not a rounded 5.29.

Example 4

Write the equation of the hyperbola with foci \((-1, 3)\) and \((7, 3)\) and vertices \((1, 3)\) and \((5, 3)\).

Solution:

The center is the midpoint of the foci: \(\left(\frac{-1+7}{2}, 3\right) = (3, 3)\). All four given points share \(y = 3\), so the transverse axis is horizontal. Distances from the center:

\(c = 7 - 3 = 4, \qquad a = 5 - 3 = 2, \qquad b^2 = c^2 - a^2 = 16 - 4 = 12\)

Answer: \(\dfrac{(x-3)^2}{4} - \dfrac{(y-3)^2}{12} = 1\). The center \((h,k)\) travels exactly as it did for circles, parabolas, and ellipses: every conic in this unit shifts by the same grammar.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Importing the ellipse's habits. On an ellipse, \(a\) is the larger denominator and \(c^2 = a^2 - b^2\); on a hyperbola, \(a^2\) is the denominator of the positive term — larger or not — and \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\). Write \(c^2 = a^2 - b^2\) here and your foci land inside the vertices, which no hyperbola permits: the foci of a hyperbola always sit beyond the vertices, because each branch bends around its focus. One memory hook: the ellipse subtracts because its foci are trapped inside a bounded curve; the hyperbola adds because its foci must be chased outside the vertices. When in doubt, re-derive: \(b^2\) was defined as \(c^2 - a^2\), which rearranges only one way.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Apollonius earned the title the Great Geometer from antiquity itself, and his Conics set a record for patience: the eighth and final book is lost, and books V–VII survived only in Arabic translation, recovered by European mathematicians in the seventeenth century — nearly two thousand years after they were written. When Kepler needed ellipses for planetary orbits and Newton needed conics for gravity, the curves were waiting for them, fully worked out, in a book older than the Roman Empire's peak. Mathematics keeps.

Conclusion

One word changed — sum to difference — and a new curve was born: two branches instead of one loop, foci flung outside the vertices instead of held within, and a new Pythagorean relation \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\) issued by the triangle inequality's fine print. You derived the equation by squaring twice with both signs kept on purpose, wrote equations from foci, vertices, points, and shifted centers, and made a point of the curve testify that the definition still holds. But one member of today's cast has done no visible work yet: \(b\), the conjugate axis, which touches no point of the curve. Tomorrow it takes the stage — \(b\) builds the box, the box builds the asymptotes, and the asymptotes tell the truth about where these branches are ultimately headed. If that word asymptote stirs a memory of rational functions and honest long-run statements from Unit 2 — good. It should.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. The ellipse's definition keeps a sum constant and produces one closed curve; the hyperbola's keeps a difference and produces two separate branches. Where, exactly, in the definition do the two branches come from?

2. Why must the constant difference \(2a\) be smaller than the focal distance \(2c\) — and what happens to the locus at the two boundary cases \(2a = 2c\) and \(2a = 0\)?

3. In \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\), the number \(b\) measures an axis that no point of the hyperbola ever touches. What entitles us to define \(b^2 = c^2 - a^2\) at all, and what part of the fine print guarantees the definition makes sense?

4. The derivation squared both sides twice — the same irreversible operation that manufactured ghost solutions in Unit 3. Why does this derivation end with no ghosts to judge?
