Graphing Hyperbolas
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to graph any hyperbola in standard form by the box-and-asymptotes method: center, central box, asymptotes, vertices, branches.

  • I will be able to identify vertices, co-vertices, foci, and branches from either standard form, in either orientation, centered anywhere.

  • I will be able to explain why the asymptotes are honest long-run statements about the branches — and prove the branches never touch them.

📚 VOCABULARY

Asymptote: A line a curve approaches without bound but never reaches — from the Greek asymptōtos, “not falling together.” You met asymptotes in Unit 2 as the honest long-run statements of rational functions; a hyperbola has two, and they cross at its center.

Central box (fundamental rectangle): The rectangle centered at the hyperbola's center, \(2a\) wide along the transverse axis and \(2b\) along the conjugate axis. The hyperbola never touches it — but its extended diagonals are exactly the asymptotes.

Co-vertices: The endpoints of the conjugate axis, a distance \(b\) from the center. Not points of the hyperbola — they are scaffolding, and scaffolding is what makes an accurate graph possible.

Orientation: Which way the branches open — left–right when the \(x\)-term is positive, up–down when the \(y\)-term is positive. The positive term steers; the denominators never decide.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

When Apollonius of Perga organized his Conics, he gave the hyperbola's strangest feature an entire book: Book II is largely devoted to the asymptotes — the two lines that a hyperbola approaches forever and touches never. He proved, with nothing but Greek geometry, what we will prove today with a single rationalization: the gap between branch and line shrinks below any size you name, yet never closes. The very word is his era's: asymptōtos, “not falling together.” Two thousand years later, you spent Unit 2 learning to read asymptotes as a rational function's honest testimony about its long-run behavior. Today the same honesty returns wearing geometry: a hyperbola's asymptotes are the truth about where its branches are headed.

Yesterday's mystery guest goes to work immediately. The number \(b\) touches no point of the curve — but mark the co-vertices at distance \(b\) from the center, draw the central box through vertices and co-vertices, and extend its diagonals: those diagonals are the asymptotes, and the branches thread the box's open ends, hugging the diagonals ever more closely. For \(\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\), the diagonals of the box run through the origin with slope \(\pm\frac{b}{a}\) — rise \(b\), run \(a\), straight off the box's corners:

\(y = \pm \dfrac{b}{a}x \qquad\) (centered form; shift to \(\;y - k = \pm \dfrac{b}{a}(x - h)\;\) for center \((h,k)\))

For the vertical form \(\frac{(y-k)^2}{a^2} - \frac{(x-h)^2}{b^2} = 1\), the slopes are \(\pm\frac{a}{b}\). Do not memorize which letter goes on top — read the slope off the box: the diagonal always rises by the half-height and runs by the half-width of the rectangle you just drew. The box cannot lie.

Why the Asymptotes Tell the Truth — a Proof, Not a Promise

Solve \(\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\) for \(y\) on the upper-right quarter: \(y = \frac{b}{a}\sqrt{x^2 - a^2}\). Compare it with the asymptote \(y = \frac{b}{a}x\). The gap between them is

\(\dfrac{b}{a}\left(x - \sqrt{x^2 - a^2}\right) = \dfrac{b}{a} \cdot \dfrac{\left(x - \sqrt{x^2 - a^2}\right)\left(x + \sqrt{x^2 - a^2}\right)}{x + \sqrt{x^2 - a^2}} = \dfrac{ab}{x + \sqrt{x^2 - a^2}}\)

Read that final fraction like a Unit 2 mathematician: the numerator \(ab\) is a fixed positive number, and the denominator grows without bound as \(x\) does. So the gap is always positive — the branch runs strictly below the line, never touching — and shrinks toward zero — the line is exactly where the branch is headed. Both halves of the asymptote's testimony, proved in one rationalization.

This is the same virtue asymptotes practiced in Unit 2, arriving by a different road: a rational function's asymptote came from a denominator's long-run dominance; the hyperbola's comes from a difference of squares flattening at long range. Different mechanism — same honesty: the asymptote is the graph's own true statement about its far behavior. A hyperbola drawn without its asymptotes is a witness who hasn't been sworn in.

Key Facts: The Five-Step Graphing Discipline

  1. Center. Plot \((h, k)\).
  2. Box. Mark \(\pm a\) along the transverse direction (the positive term's variable) and \(\pm b\) along the other; draw the rectangle lightly — it is scaffolding, so dash it.
  3. Asymptotes. Extend both diagonals of the box well past its corners.
  4. Vertices. Darken the two box-edge midpoints on the transverse axis — the only points where curve and box meet the same line.
  5. Branches. From each vertex, sweep outward, bending toward the asymptotes without crossing them.

Bonus — the foci for free: the corner of the box sits at distance \(\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = c\) from the center. Put your compass point at the center, pencil on a box corner, and swing an arc to the transverse axis: it lands exactly on a focus. The relation \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\) is not just algebra — it is the box's own diagonal, measured.

Example 1

Graph \(\dfrac{x^2}{25} - \dfrac{y^2}{9} = 1\), giving vertices, foci, and asymptotes.

Solution:

The \(x\)-term is positive: branches open left–right, center \((0,0)\), \(a = 5\), \(b = 3\).

\(c^2 = a^2 + b^2 = 25 + 9 = 34, \qquad c = \sqrt{34}\)

Box from \(x = \pm 5\), \(y = \pm 3\); diagonals give asymptotes \(y = \pm\frac{3}{5}x\); vertices \((\pm 5, 0)\); foci \((\pm\sqrt{34}, 0) \approx (\pm 5.83, 0)\) — just beyond the vertices, as they must be. Sweep the branches from the vertices toward the diagonals.

Watch the honesty at work: at \(x = 50\), the branch is at \(y = \frac{3}{5}\sqrt{2500 - 25} = \frac{3}{5}\sqrt{2475} \approx 29.85\), while the asymptote is at \(y = 30\). Ten box-widths out and the gap is already down to about \(0.15\) — and by the proof above it equals \(\frac{ab}{x + \sqrt{x^2 - a^2}} = \frac{15}{50 + \sqrt{2475}} \approx 0.15\) ✓.

Answer: Vertices \((\pm 5, 0)\); foci \((\pm\sqrt{34}, 0)\); asymptotes \(y = \pm\frac{3}{5}x\).

Example 2

Graph \(\dfrac{y^2}{16} - \dfrac{x^2}{20} = 1\), giving vertices, foci, and asymptotes.

Solution:

The \(y\)-term is positive: branches open up–down. Here \(a^2 = 16\) rides the positive term even though \(20 > 16\) — sign assigns the roles, not size. So \(a = 4\), \(b = 2\sqrt{5}\), and

\(c^2 = 16 + 20 = 36, \qquad c = 6\)

Vertices \((0, \pm 4)\); foci \((0, \pm 6)\); box half-width \(2\sqrt{5} \approx 4.47\), half-height 4. The asymptote slope is rise over run off the box: \(\pm\dfrac{4}{2\sqrt{5}} = \pm\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{5}} = \pm\dfrac{2\sqrt{5}}{5}\).

Answer: Vertices \((0, \pm 4)\); foci \((0, \pm 6)\); asymptotes \(y = \pm\frac{2\sqrt{5}}{5}x\). An irrational box builds a perfectly good graph — exact values first, decimals only to place your pencil.

Example 3

Graph \(\dfrac{(x+2)^2}{9} - \dfrac{(y-1)^2}{16} = 1\), giving center, vertices, foci, and asymptotes.

Solution:

Center \((-2, 1)\); \(x\)-term positive, so left–right; \(a = 3\), \(b = 4\),

\(c^2 = 9 + 16 = 25, \qquad c = 5\)

March from the center: vertices \((-2 \pm 3,\, 1) = (-5, 1)\) and \((1, 1)\); foci \((-2 \pm 5,\, 1) = (-7, 1)\) and \((3, 1)\). The box runs 3 left-right and 4 up-down from the center, so the diagonals climb \(\pm\frac{4}{3}\):

\(y - 1 = \pm\dfrac{4}{3}(x + 2)\)

Answer: Center \((-2,1)\); vertices \((-5,1)\), \((1,1)\); foci \((-7,1)\), \((3,1)\); asymptotes \(y - 1 = \pm\frac{4}{3}(x+2)\). A 3–4–5 triangle hides in the box's corner — the compass swing from corner to focus is exactly 5.

Example 4

A hyperbola has asymptotes \(y = \pm 2x\) and vertices \((0, \pm 6)\). Find its equation and foci.

Solution:

Vertices on the \(y\)-axis: vertical orientation, \(a = 6\), and the asymptote slope for a vertical hyperbola is \(\pm\frac{a}{b}\) — the box's rise is now \(a\). So

\(\dfrac{a}{b} = 2 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \dfrac{6}{b} = 2 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; b = 3, \qquad c^2 = 36 + 9 = 45, \;\; c = 3\sqrt{5}\)

Answer: \(\dfrac{y^2}{36} - \dfrac{x^2}{9} = 1\), foci \((0, \pm 3\sqrt{5})\). Reading the graph backward is the same discipline run in reverse: the asymptotes hand you the box, and the box hands you the equation.

  2. Zoom out, twice, three times. Watch curve and lines fuse into a single X on the screen. That fusion is the asymptote's testimony rendered in pixels — at long range, the hyperbola is its asymptotes, to within any tolerance your zoom can display.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Reciting “the asymptote slope is \(b/a\)” as a spell. It is \(\pm\frac{b}{a}\) only for the horizontal form; for the vertical form it is \(\pm\frac{a}{b}\), and a memorized fraction applied to the wrong orientation draws asymptotes the curve will visibly disobey. The cure is never memory but the box: slope = box half-height over box half-width, read directly from the rectangle you drew. A second habit to refuse: sketching branches that curl back and touch the asymptote to “finish” the drawing. The gap \(ab/\big(x+\sqrt{x^2-a^2}\,\big)\) is positive at every \(x\) — a branch that touches its asymptote is not artistic license; it is a false statement about long-run behavior.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Apollonius proved the asymptote property as pure geometry — no coordinates, no algebra, no square-root symbol, none of which existed yet. His Book II shows that if you draw a line through a hyperbola parallel to an asymptote, it cuts the curve exactly once; the asymptote itself cuts it exactly never. Eighteen centuries later, Descartes' coordinates turned that geometry into the one-line rationalization you saw today. Same theorem, two civilizations of proof.

Conclusion

The hyperbola now graphs like a discipline rather than a guess: center, box, asymptotes, vertices, branches — five steps, with the foci thrown in free by the box corner's compass swing, since \(c\) is nothing but the box's own half-diagonal. The asymptotes turned out to be Unit 2's old virtue in new clothing: honest long-run statements, this time proved outright — the gap \(ab/\big(x + \sqrt{x^2 - a^2}\,\big)\) is forever positive and forever shrinking, so the branches chase their lines eternally and reach them never. So far, though, the hyperbola has been a resident of the mathematics classroom. Tomorrow it goes to work. A ship at sea hears two radio pulses a split second apart and knows — from that time difference alone — that it floats somewhere on one branch of a hyperbola. A 500-foot cooling tower stands against the wind because its silhouette is two hyperbolic arcs. The curve of excess turns out to be a servant — and the constant difference you derived yesterday is exactly what the navigator measures.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. The asymptotes belong to no point of the hyperbola, yet no honest graph can be drawn without them. What, precisely, do they tell the truth about — and how does the rationalization proof establish both halves of their testimony?

2. The central box is built from \(a\) and \(b\), and \(b\) measures an axis the curve never touches. How does this invisible scaffolding end up dictating the vertices, the asymptotes, and the foci?

3. Unit 2's asymptotes came from denominators dominating at long range; the hyperbola's come from \(\sqrt{x^2 - a^2}\) flattening toward \(x\). What do the two mechanisms share that makes “honest long-run statement” the right name for both?

4. Given only a hyperbola's two asymptotes and one vertex, can you always reconstruct the entire curve — equation, foci, and all? What does each given piece contribute?
