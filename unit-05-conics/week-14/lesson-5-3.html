The Parabola as a Locus
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to state the locus definition of the parabola and derive \(x^2 = 4py\) from it using only the distance formula.

  • I will be able to find the focus, directrix, and opening direction of any parabola with vertex at the origin.

  • I will be able to write a parabola's equation from its focus and directrix, or from its orientation and a point it passes through.

📚 VOCABULARY

Parabola: The locus of all points equidistant from a fixed point (the focus) and a fixed line (the directrix). No formula in the definition — only a balance.

Focus: The fixed point of the definition — Latin for “hearth,” the fireplace at the center of a Roman house. Kepler coined the term in 1604, and Lesson 5-5 will show how well he chose it.

Directrix: The fixed line of the definition — from the Latin for “that which directs.” The curve never touches it; it stands off-stage, directing the shape.

Vertex: The parabola's turning point — the one point on the axis of symmetry, forced by the definition to sit exactly halfway between focus and directrix.

Axis of symmetry: The line through the focus perpendicular to the directrix; the parabola is its own mirror image across it.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

You have been drawing parabolas since Algebra 1 — always as pictures of quadratic rules, \(y = ax^2 + bx + c\). Today the parabola is reborn with no formula in sight. Around 200 BC, in a treatise called On Burning Mirrors, the Greek geometer Diocles studied the parabola as pure geometry — a curve balanced between a point and a line — and proved the property that lets a curved mirror gather sunlight to a single burning point. Eighteen centuries later Johannes Kepler gave that special point its permanent name: focus, Latin for hearth — the fireplace where a household's warmth collects. The name is a small poem about what the point does, and by Lesson 5-5 you will watch it earn the name in satellite dishes and headlights. But first, the geometry. Here is the entire definition of the parabola, and notice that it is one sentence with no algebra in it:

The Locus Definition

A parabola is the set of all points \(P\) in the plane whose distance to a fixed point \(F\) (the focus) equals their distance to a fixed line (the directrix):

\(d(P, F) = d(P, \text{directrix})\)

Every consequence in this lesson — the equation, the vertex's position, the opening direction — is this one balance, unpacked. Note immediately: the vertex is the point of the curve on the axis of symmetry, and the balance forces it to sit exactly halfway between focus and directrix. If it leaned toward either, the two distances would disagree.

Now let coordinates do what Descartes built them to do. Place the vertex at the origin, put the focus on the \(y\)-axis at \(F = (0, p)\), and let the directrix be the horizontal line \(y = -p\), the mirror position below. (Draw it with \(p > 0\); the algebra will not care.) Take any point \((x, y)\) and write the balance. Its distance to the focus comes from the distance formula; its distance to the horizontal line \(y = -p\) is the vertical gap \(|y - (-p)| = |y + p|\):

\(\sqrt{x^2 + (y - p)^2} = |y + p|\)

Both sides are distances — nonnegative by nature — so squaring is fully reversible: no ghost solutions can enter (the same certificate of safety as the circle's derivation yesterday, and the same reason the Unit 3 discipline stays in its holster). Square, expand, and watch the equation collapse:

\(x^2 + (y-p)^2 = (y+p)^2\)

\(x^2 + y^2 - 2py + p^2 = y^2 + 2py + p^2\)

\(x^2 - 2py = 2py\)

\(x^2 = 4py\)

Six symbols. The \(y^2\) and \(p^2\) terms annihilated each other, and what survived is the entire curve. Turning the picture on its side — focus at \((p, 0)\), directrix \(x = -p\) — runs the identical computation with the roles of \(x\) and \(y\) exchanged, giving \(y^2 = 4px\).

Parabolas with Vertex at the Origin

  • \(x^2 = 4py\) — vertical axis. Focus \((0, p)\), directrix \(y = -p\). Opens up if \(p > 0\), down if \(p < 0\).
  • \(y^2 = 4px\) — horizontal axis. Focus \((p, 0)\), directrix \(x = -p\). Opens right if \(p > 0\), left if \(p < 0\).

The number \(p\) is the focal distance — the signed distance from vertex to focus (and, in the other direction, from vertex to directrix). Three reading rules: the squared variable names the axis; the sign of \(p\) picks the direction; and the curve always wraps around its focus and turns its back on its directrix. The coefficient in the equation is \(4p\) — never \(p\) itself.

Example 1

Find the equation of the parabola with focus \((0, 2)\) and directrix \(y = -2\), and verify one point on it.

Solution:

The focus sits on the \(y\)-axis, \(2\) above the origin, and the directrix mirrors it \(2\) below: vertex at the origin, vertical axis, \(p = 2\).

\(x^2 = 4py = 4(2)y \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; x^2 = 8y\)

Verify: the point \((4, 2)\) satisfies \(4^2 = 8(2) = 16\) ✓, so it should be equidistant from focus and directrix. Distance to the focus \((0,2)\): \(\sqrt{(4-0)^2 + (2-2)^2} = 4\). Distance to the line \(y = -2\): \(2 - (-2) = 4\). Equal ✓.

Answer: \(x^2 = 8y\). The verification is not decoration — it is the locus definition checked in the flesh, and it will be a required step on this week's Focus assignment.

Example 2

Find the focus and directrix of \(x^2 = -12y\), and describe the curve.

Solution:

Vertical axis (\(x\) is squared). Read the coefficient as \(4p\):

\(4p = -12 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; p = -3\)

Negative \(p\) drops the focus below the vertex and opens the curve downward. Focus: \((0, -3)\). Directrix: \(y = -p = 3\), standing guard above.

Check with a point: \((6, -3)\) is on the curve since \(36 = -12(-3)\) ✓. Distance to focus \((0,-3)\): \(6\). Distance to directrix \(y = 3\): \(|-3 - 3| = 6\) ✓.

Answer: Focus \((0, -3)\), directrix \(y = 3\), opening downward — the curve wraps around its focus below and turns its back on the line above.

Example 3

Write the equation of the parabola with focus \((5, 0)\) and directrix \(x = -5\).

Solution:

The focus lies on the \(x\)-axis and the directrix is vertical: horizontal axis, vertex at the origin (halfway between), \(p = 5\), opening right — toward the focus, away from the line.

\(y^2 = 4px = 4(5)x \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; y^2 = 20x\)

Check: \((5, 10)\) is on the curve (\(100 = 20 \cdot 5\) ✓). To the focus: \(\sqrt{(5-5)^2 + 10^2} = 10\). To the directrix: \(5 - (-5) = 10\) ✓.

Answer: \(y^2 = 20x\).

Example 4

A parabola has its vertex at the origin, opens to the right, and passes through \((2, 6)\). Find its equation, focus, and directrix.

Solution:

Opening right with vertex at the origin means \(y^2 = 4px\) with \(p > 0\). The point \((2, 6)\) must satisfy the equation — feed it in and solve for \(p\):

\(6^2 = 4p(2)\)

\(36 = 8p \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; p = \tfrac{9}{2}\)

\(y^2 = 4 \cdot \tfrac{9}{2} \cdot x = 18x\)

Verify the locus balance at \((2,6)\): to the focus \(\left(\tfrac{9}{2}, 0\right)\): \(\sqrt{\left(2 - \tfrac{9}{2}\right)^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{\tfrac{25}{4} + 36} = \sqrt{\tfrac{169}{4}} = \tfrac{13}{2}\). To the directrix \(x = -\tfrac{9}{2}\): \(2 + \tfrac{9}{2} = \tfrac{13}{2}\) ✓.

Answer: \(y^2 = 18x\); focus \(\left(\tfrac{9}{2}, 0\right)\); directrix \(x = -\tfrac{9}{2}\). Note the workflow: orientation chose the form, one known point bought \(p\), and the locus definition audited the purchase.

💻 On Your Calculator: Watch the Balance Hold 8 min

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), make the locus definition move. Build Example 1's parabola:

  1. Graph x^2 = 8y (Desmos accepts it as written), plot the focus F = (0, 2), and add the directrix y = -2.
  2. Create a slider a, then the point P = (a, a^2/8) — a point pinned to the parabola that rides it as \(a\) slides.
  3. Type d_1 = distance(P, F) and d_2 = a^2/8 + 2 (the vertical drop from \(P\) to the directrix). Desmos displays both numbers live.
  4. Drag \(a\) slowly from \(-8\) to \(8\) and watch \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) stay locked together, digit for digit, at every position — the equidistance balance holding everywhere at once. Then drag \(P\)'s definition off the curve (try (a, a^2/8 + 1)) and watch the balance break. The calculator confirms; your algebra convinces.

Vertical axis; \(4p = -4\) gives \(p = -1\). Focus \((0, -1)\); directrix \(y = 1\); opens downward. Quick audit with \((2, -1)\): on the curve since \(4 = -4(-1)\) ✓; to the focus \(\sqrt{4 + 0} = 2\); to the directrix \(|-1 - 1| = 2\) ✓.
▶  Try it yourself: Write the equation of the parabola with focus \(\left(0, \tfrac{1}{2}\right)\) and directrix \(y = -\tfrac{1}{2}\).
Vertex at the origin, vertical axis, \(p = \tfrac{1}{2}\), so \(x^2 = 4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot y = 2y\). Fractional focal distances are coming back in tomorrow's honors stretch — the parabolas of Algebra 1 have been hiding small values of \(p\) all along.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Reading the coefficient as \(p\): seeing \(x^2 = 8y\) and declaring the focus to be \((0, 8)\). The equation's coefficient is \(4p\), so \(8 = 4p\) gives \(p = 2\) — the focus is \((0,2)\), four times closer than the misreading claims. The factor of 4 was born honestly in the derivation (watch \(2py + 2py\) merge), and it never leaves. Say it as a habit before every problem: coefficient equals \(4p\); divide by 4 first. The companion slip is placing the directrix on the same side as the focus — it always stands on the opposite side of the vertex, at the mirror distance.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
When Kepler needed a name in 1604 for the special point of a conic, he reached for the Latin focus — “hearth,” the fireplace at the heart of a Roman household. It was a physicist's joke with perfect aim: Diocles had shown eighteen centuries earlier that a parabolic mirror gathers parallel sunlight to that very point and can kindle a fire there. Every “focal point,” every camera that “focuses,” every student told to “focus” is borrowing Kepler's fireplace — and in Lesson 5-5 you will compute exactly where in a satellite dish the fire sits.

Conclusion

A point, a line, and one balance between them — that is all a parabola is, and you watched the whole curve fall out of the distance formula in four lines: \(x^2 = 4py\), with its sideways twin \(y^2 = 4px\). The number \(p\) turned out to be the curve's whole biography — the focus at distance \(p\) on one side, the directrix at distance \(p\) on the other, sign choosing the direction, the vertex pinned halfway by the balance itself. You read \(p\) out of equations (dividing the coefficient by 4, always), wrote equations from focus and directrix, bought \(p\) with a single known point, and audited every answer against the locus definition itself. This is the material of this week's Focus assignment — the one set your teacher grades for method — and the derivation you just watched is the argument you will be asked to make yourself, with numbers, nothing skipped. Tomorrow the vertex leaves the origin: the same curve, translated anywhere in the plane, plus one new piece of anatomy — a chord through the focus called the latus rectum — that makes an honest hand sketch possible in under a minute.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. How does a single equidistance condition manufacture an entire curve — and why does that condition force the vertex to sit exactly halfway between focus and directrix?

2. In \(x^2 = 4py\), what exactly does \(p\) measure, and what do its sign and size each control?

3. Why is squaring both sides of the locus equation perfectly reversible here, when the same operation manufactured ghosts in Unit 3?

4. Why can a parabola never touch its own directrix — what contradiction would the balance suffer at such a point?
