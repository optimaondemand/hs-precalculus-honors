The Circle: Equation, Graphs, Applications
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to derive the equation of a circle directly from the distance formula and explain what each symbol in \((x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 = r^2\) records.

  • I will be able to write the equation of a circle from its center and radius, from a point it passes through, from the endpoints of a diameter, or from a real-world description.

  • I will be able to convert a circle's general form to standard form by completing the square, then graph it and locate points inside, on, or outside it.

📚 VOCABULARY

Circle: The locus of all points in a plane at a fixed distance \(r\) (the radius) from a fixed point \((h, k)\) (the center). One sentence — the whole curve.

Standard form: \((x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 = r^2\) — the form that announces its center and radius openly.

General form: \(x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0\) — the same circle with everything expanded and scrambled. It keeps its secrets until the square is completed.

Completing the square: The Unit 2 technique — add \(\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2\) to turn \(x^2 + bx\) into the perfect square \(\left(x + \frac{b}{2}\right)^2\) — returning this week as the master key of the whole unit.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

The circle is the oldest locus in mathematics, and for two thousand years it needed no equation at all. Euclid's third postulate simply grants the right “to describe a circle with any center and distance” — and the compass in your desk drawer is that postulate made of metal: pin down the center, fix the distance, sweep. The pin is \((h,k)\); the fixed opening is \(r\); every point the pencil visits satisfies one condition, my distance from the pin is exactly \(r\). What the Greeks could only draw, René Descartes taught the world to write: his Géométrie of 1637 married algebra to geometry through coordinates, so that a geometric condition could be frozen into an equation. Today we perform that marriage on the compass itself.

The officiant is the distance formula — which is nothing but the Pythagorean theorem wearing coordinates. A point \((x, y)\) lies on the circle with center \((h,k)\) and radius \(r\) exactly when its distance to the center equals \(r\):

\(\sqrt{(x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2} = r\)

Both sides are distances, hence nonnegative — so squaring both sides is fully reversible. (Remember Unit 3, where squaring an equation with a possibly negative side let ghost solutions in? Here both sides are certified \(\geq 0\) before we square, so no ghosts can enter. The discipline is knowing when the danger exists, not fearing the operation everywhere.) Squaring:

\((x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 = r^2\)

The Circle, Standard Form

\((x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 = r^2\)

  • Center: \((h, k)\) — read with flipped signs: \((x-3)^2\) means \(h = 3\); \((y+2)^2\) means \(k = -2\).
  • Radius: \(r = \sqrt{\text{right side}}\) — the equation stores \(r^2\), not \(r\).
  • Centered at the origin it collapses to \(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\) — the distance formula at its barest.

This equation is nothing but the distance formula frozen in place. Every fact about a circle you will ever compute comes from un-freezing it.

Example 1

Write the equation of the circle with center \((3, -2)\) and radius 4.

Solution:

Substitute \(h = 3\), \(k = -2\), \(r = 4\) into standard form:

\((x - 3)^2 + (y - (-2))^2 = 4^2\)

\((x-3)^2 + (y+2)^2 = 16\)

Answer: \((x-3)^2 + (y+2)^2 = 16\). Watch the two traps in one line: the \(+2\) inside the parentheses records a center coordinate of \(-2\), and the right side is \(16\), not \(4\) — the equation stores the square of the radius.

Example 2

Write the equation of the circle centered at \((-1, 5)\) that passes through the point \((1, 2)\).

Solution:

The radius is the distance from center to the given point — but notice we only ever need \(r^2\), so we never need to un-square:

\(r^2 = (1 - (-1))^2 + (2 - 5)^2 = 2^2 + (-3)^2 = 4 + 9 = 13\)

Answer: \((x+1)^2 + (y-5)^2 = 13\). The radius itself is the irrational number \(\sqrt{13}\), and the equation never blinks: it wanted \(r^2\) all along. Check by substituting \((1,2)\): \((1+1)^2 + (2-5)^2 = 4 + 9 = 13\) ✓.

Example 3

The points \((-2, -1)\) and \((4, 7)\) are the endpoints of a diameter. Find the circle's equation.

Solution:

The center is the midpoint of the diameter:

\(\left(\dfrac{-2+4}{2}, \dfrac{-1+7}{2}\right) = (1, 3)\)

The radius squared is the distance squared from the center to either endpoint — use \((4,7)\):

\(r^2 = (4-1)^2 + (7-3)^2 = 9 + 16 = 25\)

Honest check with the other endpoint: \((-2-1)^2 + (-1-3)^2 = 9 + 16 = 25\) ✓ — both ends agree, as a true center must arrange.

Answer: \((x-1)^2 + (y-3)^2 = 25\); center \((1,3)\), radius 5.

Example 4

Find the center and radius of the circle \(x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 4y - 12 = 0\), and describe its graph.

Solution:

This is general form — the circle with its identity scrambled. The master key from Unit 2, completing the square, unscrambles it. Group the \(x\)-terms and the \(y\)-terms, move the constant across, and complete each square, adding to both sides:

\((x^2 - 6x \phantom{+9}) + (y^2 + 4y \phantom{+4}) = 12\)

\(\left(\tfrac{-6}{2}\right)^2 = 9 \qquad \left(\tfrac{4}{2}\right)^2 = 4\)

\((x^2 - 6x + 9) + (y^2 + 4y + 4) = 12 + 9 + 4\)

\((x - 3)^2 + (y + 2)^2 = 25\)

Answer: Center \((3, -2)\), radius \(\sqrt{25} = 5\). To graph it by hand: plot the center, then step \(5\) right, left, up, and down to the four compass points \((8,-2)\), \((-2,-2)\), \((3,3)\), \((3,-7)\), and sweep the curve through them. The general form hid all of this; two completed squares told the truth.

What the Right Side Confesses

Complete the squares on any equation \(x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0\) and you reach \((x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 = c\) for some constant \(c\). Only now does the equation confess what it describes:

  • \(c > 0\): a genuine circle, radius \(\sqrt{c}\);
  • \(c = 0\): a single point — the degenerate circle from Lesson 5-1, the sum of two squares forced to vanish;
  • \(c < 0\): no points at all — a sum of squares cannot be negative.

A general form can look perfectly circular and describe nothing whatsoever. Unit 2's virtue was honesty about long-run behavior; this is the same honesty about existence — and only the completed square extracts the confession. There is also a second confession worth taking: a point \((a,b)\) lies inside the circle when \((a-h)^2 + (b-k)^2 < r^2\), on it at equality, and outside when the left side exceeds \(r^2\) — no picture required.

Example 5

A broadcast tower stands at \((5, -2)\) on a county map measured in miles, and its signal reaches 13 miles in every direction. Write the equation of the coverage boundary, and determine whether a house at \((10, 10)\) and a farm at \((-1, 3)\) receive the signal.

Solution:

The boundary is the circle centered at the tower with radius 13:

\((x-5)^2 + (y+2)^2 = 169\)

Test each location against \(r^2 = 169\) — compare squared distances, never bothering with square roots:

House \((10, 10)\): \((10-5)^2 + (10+2)^2 = 25 + 144 = 169\) — exactly \(r^2\)

Farm \((-1, 3)\): \((-1-5)^2 + (3+2)^2 = 36 + 25 = 61 < 169\)

Answer: The farm sits comfortably inside the coverage circle. The house sits precisely on the boundary — thirteen miles from the tower to the foot, the last address in the county the signal reaches. An engineer would not promise that house good reception; the mathematics says “on the locus,” and the honest report says “at the very edge.”

💻 On Your Calculator: The Circle, Scrambled and Revealed 8 min

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), Desmos graphs implicit equations directly — no solving for \(y\) required. Put Example 4 on trial:

  1. Type the general form exactly as given: x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 4y - 12 = 0. A circle appears — but where is its center? The screen shows the shape; it does not announce \((h, k)\) or \(r\).
  2. In the next line type your completed form: (x-3)^2 + (y+2)^2 = 25. It traces the identical curve — your algebra and the machine agree the two forms are one circle.
  3. Now build the whole family: type (x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 = r^2 and click to create sliders for \(h\), \(k\), and \(r\). Drag \(h\) and \(k\) and watch the circle glide; drag \(r\) and watch it breathe. Set them to \(3\), \(-2\), \(5\) and land exactly on Example 4.
  4. Finally, plot the points (10, 10) and (-1, 3) with the Example 5 circle (x-5)^2 + (y+2)^2 = 169 and see the inside/on/outside verdicts you computed. The calculator confirms; your algebra convinces.

▶  Try it yourself: Find the center and radius of \(x^2 + y^2 + 8x - 2y + 8 = 0\). Complete both squares on paper before you peek.
Group and move the constant: \((x^2 + 8x) + (y^2 - 2y) = -8\). Add \(\left(\frac{8}{2}\right)^2 = 16\) and \(\left(\frac{-2}{2}\right)^2 = 1\) to both sides: \((x+4)^2 + (y-1)^2 = -8 + 16 + 1 = 9\). Center \((-4, 1)\), radius 3.
▶  Try it yourself: Write the equation of the circle with center \((0, 3)\) and radius \(\sqrt{7}\), and decide — without graphing — whether \((2, 1)\) lies inside, on, or outside it.
Equation: \(x^2 + (y-3)^2 = 7\). Test \((2,1)\): \(2^2 + (1-3)^2 = 4 + 4 = 8 > 7\) — the point lies just outside, by the thinnest of margins. Squared distances settle it with no square roots and no sketch.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Completing the square on the left and forgetting the right: writing \((x^2 - 6x + 9) + (y^2 + 4y + 4) = 12\) after adding \(9\) and \(4\) to the left side only. An equation is a balance — whatever is added to one pan must be added to the other, or the equation now describes a different circle (here one of radius \(\sqrt{12}\) instead of 5). The companion trap is reading centers without flipping signs: \((x+4)^2\) puts the center at \(x = -4\), never \(+4\). The parentheses store \(x - h\); the center is what makes them vanish.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Euclid's Elements rests on just five postulates, and the third is the circle: the bare permission “to describe a circle with any center and distance.” Notice what it grants — not a formula, but a locus condition: a center and a distance are all a circle is. Twenty-two centuries later, when Descartes' coordinates arrived, that one Greek sentence compressed into six symbols: \((x-h)^2+(y-k)^2 = r^2\). The equation is the postulate, translated.

Conclusion

One locus condition — all points at distance \(r\) from the center — passed through the distance formula and one safe squaring, and out came the circle's equation entire. From it you wrote circles out of centers, points, diameters, and county maps; and when the equation arrived scrambled as a general form, completing the square — the master key from Unit 2, back in service for the whole unit — revealed the center, the radius, and occasionally the confession that no circle exists at all. Tomorrow the locus condition changes by a single clause: instead of all points equidistant from one point, we collect all points equidistant from a point and a line. That small edit builds an entirely different curve — the parabola — and hands it two pieces of anatomy, a focus and a directrix, whose usefulness will still be paying dividends when this unit reaches satellite dishes and whispering galleries.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. In what precise sense is \((x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 = r^2\) “the distance formula frozen in place,” and why does the equation store \(r^2\) rather than \(r\)?

2. Squaring both sides manufactured ghost solutions in Unit 3. Why is the squaring in today's derivation perfectly safe — what guarantees it?

3. What is completing the square actually doing, geometrically, when it converts a general form to standard form — and what do a zero or negative right side confess?

4. How do squared distances decide inside, on, or outside a circle without a single square root or sketch — and why did the Example 5 engineer still owe the edge house an honest warning?
