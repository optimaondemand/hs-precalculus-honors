Graphing Parabolas
Lesson
🎯 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • I will be able to graph parabolas with vertex \((h, k)\) in both orientations, identifying vertex, focus, directrix, axis of symmetry, and latus rectum.

  • I will be able to convert a parabola's general form to standard form by completing the square — including factoring out \(4p\) before reading the vertex.

  • I will be able to write a parabola's equation from any sufficient set of key features.

📚 VOCABULARY

Standard form (vertex \((h,k)\)): \((x-h)^2 = 4p(y-k)\) for a vertical axis, \((y-k)^2 = 4p(x-h)\) for a horizontal axis — yesterday's origin forms, translated.

Latus rectum: The chord of the parabola through the focus, perpendicular to the axis of symmetry; its length is always \(|4p|\). Latin for “upright side” — the term is a direct translation of Apollonius' own Greek, still on duty after twenty-two centuries.

Focal chord: Any chord through the focus; the latus rectum is the shortest one, and the only one perpendicular to the axis.

Axis of symmetry: For vertex form, the line \(x = h\) (vertical case) or \(y = k\) (horizontal case) — the fold line of the curve.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In 1638, under house arrest in Arcetri, Galileo published his Two New Sciences and in it a claim both simple and revolutionary: a projectile — any thrown stone, any cannonball — travels a path that “is a parabola, exactly.” The curve Apollonius had sliced from a cone for pure love of geometry turned out to be drawn in the air by every fountain and fastball on Earth. But Galileo's parabolas do not sit politely with their vertices at the origin; they arc over castle walls with vertices wherever the throw puts them. Today we follow them there. The tool is one you have owned since Unit 3: translation. Replacing \(x\) with \(x - h\) and \(y\) with \(y - k\) slides any graph \(h\) right and \(k\) up — and it extends the same courtesy to curves that are not functions at all. Yesterday's two equations slide into today's two:

Parabolas with Vertex \((h, k)\)

  • \((x-h)^2 = 4p(y-k)\) — vertical axis \(x = h\). Focus \((h, k+p)\); directrix \(y = k - p\). Opens up (\(p>0\)) or down (\(p<0\)).
  • \((y-k)^2 = 4p(x-h)\) — horizontal axis \(y = k\). Focus \((h+p, k)\); directrix \(x = h - p\). Opens right (\(p>0\)) or left (\(p<0\)).
  • Latus rectum: length \(|4p|\), centered on the focus, perpendicular to the axis — its two endpoints sit \(|2p|\) either side of the focus.

The five-step graphing routine: ① the squared variable names the axis direction; ② read the vertex \((h,k)\) with flipped signs; ③ divide the coefficient by 4 to get \(p\), sign and all; ④ step \(p\) along the axis to the focus, and mirror to the directrix; ⑤ mark the two latus rectum endpoints and sweep the curve through them and the vertex. Three plotted points and a dashed line — an honest sketch in under a minute.

Example 1

Graph \((x-3)^2 = 8(y+1)\): find the vertex, focus, directrix, axis, and latus rectum endpoints.

Solution:

Run the routine in order:

Axis: \(x\) squared → vertical axis, the line \(x = 3\)

Vertex: \((3, -1)\) — signs flipped from \((x-3)\) and \((y+1)\)

\(4p = 8 \;\Rightarrow\; p = 2 > 0\): opens upward

Focus: \((3, -1 + 2) = (3, 1)\)   Directrix: \(y = -1 - 2 = -3\)

Latus rectum: length \(|4p| = 8\), endpoints \((3 \pm 4, 1) = (-1, 1)\) and \((7, 1)\)

Answer: Plot the vertex \((3,-1)\), the endpoints \((-1,1)\) and \((7,1)\), dash in the directrix \(y = -3\), and sweep the upward curve through the three points, wrapping around the focus \((3,1)\). Sanity check: the endpoint \((7,1)\) should satisfy the equation — \((7-3)^2 = 16\) and \(8(1+1) = 16\) ✓.

Example 2

Analyze \((y+2)^2 = -4(x-1)\).

Solution:

Axis: \(y\) squared → horizontal axis, the line \(y = -2\)

Vertex: \((1, -2)\)

\(4p = -4 \;\Rightarrow\; p = -1 < 0\): opens left

Focus: \((1 + (-1), -2) = (0, -2)\)   Directrix: \(x = 1 - (-1) = 2\)

Latus rectum: length \(|-4| = 4\), endpoints \((0, -2 \pm 2) = (0, 0)\) and \((0, -4)\)

Answer: Vertex \((1,-2)\), focus \((0,-2)\), directrix \(x = 2\), curve opening left through \((0,0)\) and \((0,-4)\). Notice the geometry stayed loyal: the curve wraps around its focus (to the left) and turns its back on its directrix (standing to the right). Check an endpoint: \((0 - (-2))^2 \Rightarrow (0+2)^2 = 4\) and \(-4(0-1) = 4\) ✓.

Example 3

Find the vertex, focus, and directrix of \(y^2 + 6y - 8x + 1 = 0\).

Solution:

General form — and the master key turns again. \(y\) is the squared variable, so complete the square in \(y\) and herd everything else to the other side:

\(y^2 + 6y = 8x - 1\)

\(y^2 + 6y + 9 = 8x - 1 + 9\)

\((y + 3)^2 = 8x + 8\)

One step remains, and it is the step most often skipped: factor out the \(4p\) coefficient before reading anything.

\((y+3)^2 = 8(x + 1)\)

Vertex \((-1, -3)\); \(4p = 8 \Rightarrow p = 2\): opens right

Focus: \((-1 + 2, -3) = (1, -3)\)   Directrix: \(x = -3\)

Answer: Vertex \((-1,-3)\), focus \((1,-3)\), directrix \(x = -3\), latus rectum endpoints \((1, -3 \pm 4) = (1, 1)\) and \((1, -7)\). Had we read the vertex from \((y+3)^2 = 8x + 8\) without factoring, we would have announced \(x = -8\) — a vertex seven units from the truth. The factoring is not cosmetic; it is where the honest \(h\) lives.

Example 4

Write the equation of the parabola with vertex \((2, 4)\) and focus \((2, 7)\).

Solution:

Vertex and focus share \(x = 2\), so the axis is vertical and the form is \((x-h)^2 = 4p(y-k)\). The focus sits \(7 - 4 = 3\) above the vertex:

\(p = 3 \;\Rightarrow\; 4p = 12\)

\((x - 2)^2 = 12(y - 4)\)

Answer: \((x-2)^2 = 12(y-4)\), opening upward, with directrix \(y = 4 - 3 = 1\) — three units below the vertex, mirroring the focus three above, exactly as the locus definition demands.

Why the Latus Rectum Makes a Sketch Honest

A vertex alone tells you where a parabola sits, but nothing about how wide it opens — and a freehand curve through one point is a guess, not a graph. The latus rectum ends the guessing:

  • Its endpoints are exact points of the curve at the focus's height, so they pin the parabola's true width where its geometry is busiest.
  • Its length \(|4p|\) makes \(p\) visible: a large \(|p|\) (focus far from vertex) gives a long latus rectum and a wide, open curve; a small \(|p|\) hugs the focus tight and the curve closes up narrow.

Vertex, two latus rectum endpoints, dashed directrix: three true points and a guardrail. That is the whole apparatus of an honest hand sketch — and when your Desmos graph disagrees with it, one of the two of you has misread \(p\).

At desmos.com/calculator (or below), build every parabola of this lesson at once:

  1. Type (y-k)^2 = 4p(x-h) and let Desmos create sliders for \(h\), \(k\), and \(p\).
  2. Add the focus as the point (h+p, k) and the directrix as the line x = h-p. They now travel with the sliders.
  3. Set \(h = 1\), \(k = -2\), \(p = -1\) and land exactly on Example 2 — vertex \((1,-2)\), focus \((0,-2)\), directrix \(x=2\), matching your hand analysis point for point.
  4. Now drag \(p\) slowly through zero. Watch the curve open wide at large \(|p|\), pinch narrow near zero, degenerate at \(p = 0\), and flip direction as the sign changes — while focus and directrix trade places across the vertex. The slider is Lesson 5-3's theory running as a machine. Verify Example 3 the same way with the vertical form (x-h)^2 = 4p(y-k). The calculator confirms; your algebra convinces.

▶  Try it yourself: Convert \(x^2 - 4x - 4y + 8 = 0\) to standard form and list vertex, \(p\), focus, directrix, and latus rectum endpoints. Full routine on paper before you peek.
\(x^2 - 4x = 4y - 8\); add 4 to both sides: \((x-2)^2 = 4y - 4 = 4(y - 1)\). Vertex \((2, 1)\); \(4p = 4\) so \(p = 1\), opening up; focus \((2, 2)\); directrix \(y = 0\) (the \(x\)-axis itself); latus rectum endpoints \((2 \pm 2, 2) = (0,2)\) and \((4,2)\). Check an endpoint: \((0-2)^2 = 4\), \(4(2-1) = 4\) ✓.
▶  Try it yourself: Write the equation of the parabola with vertex \((-1, 2)\) and directrix \(y = 5\).
The directrix is horizontal and stands above the vertex, so the axis is vertical and the curve opens down, away from the line: \(p = 2 - 5 = -3\). Equation: \((x+1)^2 = 4(-3)(y-2) = -12(y-2)\). Focus: \((-1, 2 + (-3)) = (-1, -1)\), mirroring the directrix three units on the other side.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Reading the vertex before factoring out \(4p\): stopping at \((y+3)^2 = 8x + 8\) and declaring \(h = -8\). The standard form requires the right side to be \(4p \cdot (\,\text{variable} - h\,)\) — coefficient pulled all the way out — and only then is \(h\) sitting where you can read it: \(8(x+1)\) means \(h = -1\), not \(-8\). The companion slip is halving when you should quarter: the latus rectum runs \(|2p|\) either side of the focus for a total of \(|4p|\); make its endpoints with \(2p\), not \(4p\), or your sketch will be twice as wide as the truth.
💡 DID YOU KNOW?
Galileo could not prove his parabola claim with calculus — it did not exist yet. He argued it from proportions: horizontal motion covers equal distances in equal times, while vertical fall grows with the square of time, and a curve whose one coordinate is proportional to the square of the other is precisely \(x^2 = 4py\) in disguise. He also carefully noted the fine print modern physics still teaches: no air resistance, and a flat Earth approximation — an honest mathematician labeling the domain where his model tells the truth. Unit 2 would approve.

Conclusion

The parabola now travels: \((x-h)^2 = 4p(y-k)\) and \((y-k)^2 = 4p(x-h)\) put the vertex anywhere in the plane, and the five-step routine — axis from the squared variable, vertex with flipped signs, \(p\) from the coefficient divided by four, focus and directrix mirrored across the vertex, latus rectum endpoints \(2p\) either side of the focus — turns any such equation into an honest three-point sketch. When the equation arrived scrambled, completing the square unscrambled it, with one non-negotiable finishing move: factor out \(4p\) before reading the vertex. This closes the week that opened on Apollonius' cone: you have sliced the family, mastered the circle, and built the parabola twice — from its locus and now on the full plane. But one question has been waiting since Kepler named the focus a hearth: what actually happens at that point? Next lesson, the answer — a proof that every ray arriving parallel to the axis reflects off the parabola straight into the focus, and with it the satellite dish, the headlight, and the radio telescope. The curve is about to start serving.

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. How do you read a parabola's entire anatomy — axis, vertex, \(p\), focus, directrix, width — from a standard-form equation in a single pass?

2. Why must \(4p\) be factored out completely before the vertex can be read, and what kind of error does skipping that step produce?

3. What exactly do the latus rectum's endpoints pin down, and why does a sketch through vertex-plus-endpoints deserve to be called honest?

4. Galileo's cannonballs and Apollonius' cone produce the same curve. What would it take to convince a skeptic that the projectile's arc really is a conic section and not merely a look-alike?
